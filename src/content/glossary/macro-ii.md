# Macro II Concepts

> One-stop glossary for Macro-Economics II (Year 2, Semester 1). Each entry: a one-line definition and the note where it is taught. Solow and growth concepts have no lecture deck, so they point to the first past paper that uses them. Tracker of which links exist: [[_Wiki-Link Registry]]. Subject hub: [[Macro-Economics II]].

## Classical theory and national income

### Production Function
Y = F(K, L): output produced from capital and labour; in the classical model it fixes full-employment output. → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

### Cobb-Douglas
Production function Y = K^a L^(1-a) with constant returns to scale; the exponents are the factor income shares. → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

### Marginal Product
Extra output from one more unit of an input; in the classical model factors are paid their marginal products (MPL = real wage, MPK = real rental rate). → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]

### Marginal Propensity to Consume
Fraction of an extra unit of disposable income that is consumed; sets the size of the Keynesian-cross multiplier 1/(1 - MPC). → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]

### National Saving
S = Y - C - G, private plus public saving; it is the supply of loanable funds. → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]

### Loanable Funds Market
Market where saving (supply) meets investment (demand) and the real interest rate clears it. → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]

### Real Interest Rate
Nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation; in the classical model it adjusts to equate saving and investment. → [[Lec_03-Classical Theory & National Income|Lec 03]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

## Money and inflation

### Money Supply
Total stock of money in the economy, set jointly by the central bank and the banking system. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Money Multiplier
Ratio of the money supply to the monetary base, 1/(reserve ratio) in the simple model; each deposit is re-lent and re-deposited. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Fractional Reserve Banking
Banks hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest, which creates money. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Monetary Base
Currency in circulation plus bank reserves, the part of the money supply the central bank controls directly. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Federal Reserve
The US central bank; it controls the monetary base through open-market operations, the discount rate and reserve requirements. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Bank Capital
Owners' equity in a bank, the resources the owners put in; assets minus liabilities. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Leverage
Ratio of a bank's assets to its capital; high leverage magnifies both gains and losses. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Quantitative Easing
Central-bank purchases of long-term assets (2008 to 2011 in the lecture) to lower long rates once short rates hit zero. → [[Lec_04-The Monetary System|Lec 04]]

### Quantity Theory of Money
MV = PY with V constant: the price level moves in proportion to the money supply, and money growth equals inflation when output is given. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Velocity of Money
V = PY/M, the rate at which money changes hands; assumed constant in the quantity theory. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Money Neutrality
Changes in the money supply move nominal variables (prices, nominal wages) but not real ones (output, real wage, real rate). → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Fisher Effect
i = r + pi: when expected inflation rises one for one, the nominal interest rate rises one for one and the real rate is unchanged. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Classical Dichotomy
Real variables are determined by real forces and nominal variables by money; the two blocks can be analysed separately. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Nominal Interest Rate
The interest rate as quoted, not adjusted for inflation: i = r + pi. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

### Money Demand
Real money balances demanded, (M/P)^d = L(i, Y); falls with the nominal interest rate and rises with income. → [[Lec_05-Inflation|Lec 05]]

## Growth (Solow)

### Solow Model
Growth model in which capital per worker accumulates through saving s f(k) and is eroded by break-even investment; saving changes the level of income per worker, only technology changes its long-run growth (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_01-Solow, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2021 Sample Exam)|PP 01]]

### Steady State
Capital per worker k* where saving equals break-even investment, s f(k) = (delta + n + g) k; k and y are then constant and total output grows at n + g (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_01-Solow, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2021 Sample Exam)|PP 01]]

### Golden Rule
The saving rate and capital stock that maximise steady-state consumption; MPK = delta, or MPK - delta = n + g with growth. With Cobb-Douglas f(k) = k^a the golden-rule saving rate equals the capital share a. Above it saving is too high, below it too low (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_01-Solow, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2021 Sample Exam)|PP 01]]

### Break-even Investment
Investment needed to keep capital per worker constant, (delta + n + g) k: depreciation plus equipping new workers; a higher n rotates the line up and lowers k* (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_01-Solow, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2021 Sample Exam)|PP 01]]

### Effective Worker
Labour in efficiency units, L x E (workers times technology); output per effective worker Y/(LE) is constant in the steady state while output per worker grows at g (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_01-Solow, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2021 Sample Exam)|PP 01]]

### Dynamic Inefficiency
Capital per worker above the golden-rule level (MPK below delta + n + g, over-saving): steady-state consumption would rise if saving fell (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_08-Solow, Classical Theory, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & AS-AD (2025 Moed A)|PP 08]]

### AK Model
Endogenous growth with y = Ak: no diminishing returns to capital, so capital and income grow at the constant rate sA - delta and the saving rate permanently affects growth (past papers only, no lecture deck). → [[PP_05-Neoclassical Saving, Solow Growth, IS-LM, Mundell-Fleming & Phillips Curve (2022 Moed B)|PP 05]]

## AS-AD and fluctuations

### Aggregate Demand
Total planned spending on output at each price level; the AD curve slopes down because a higher P lowers real money balances. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Short-Run Aggregate Supply
SRAS: with sticky prices, output deviates from its natural level when the price level differs from expectations; flat in the extreme fixed-price case. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Long-Run Aggregate Supply
LRAS: vertical line at natural output Y-bar, determined by capital, labour and technology. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Okun's Law
Empirical negative link between the output gap and unemployment: a fall in output below trend raises cyclical unemployment. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Price Stickiness
Prices adjust slowly to demand changes in the short run, so output moves instead of prices. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Supply Shock
Event that shifts SRAS (oil prices, drought, productivity); an adverse shock raises prices and cuts output. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

### Demand Shock
Event that shifts AD (spending, money, confidence); moves output and prices in the same direction. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Stabilization Policy
Monetary or fiscal action meant to offset shocks and keep output at Y-bar. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Business Cycle
Short-run fluctuations of output and employment around the long-run trend. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

### Stagflation
Falling output together with rising prices, typical of an adverse supply shock. → [[Lec_06-AS-AD Model & Economic Fluctuations|Lec 06]]

## IS-LM

### IS Curve
Combinations of Y and r that clear the goods market, Y = C(Y - T) + I(r) + G; downward sloping. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### LM Curve
Combinations of Y and r that clear the money market, M/P = L(r, Y); upward sloping. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Keynesian Cross
Model where planned expenditure PE = C + I + G is plotted against income Y and equilibrium is PE = Y; gives the multiplier. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Liquidity Preference
Theory that the interest rate adjusts to equate money supply and demand; the real money supply is fixed and demand falls with r. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Fiscal Multiplier
Change in income per unit change in G (1/(1 - MPC) in the Keynesian cross, smaller in IS-LM because r rises). → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Short-Run Equilibrium
The (Y*, r*) at which IS and LM intersect, so both goods and money markets clear. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Inventory Adjustment
Firms change output when actual spending differs from planned, moving Y toward the Keynesian-cross equilibrium. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Unplanned Inventory Investment
Gap between actual and planned expenditure when Y is off equilibrium: goods produced but unsold pile up, so firms cut production (and the reverse when stocks run down). → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### Balanced-Budget Multiplier
Raising G and T by the same amount raises income by exactly that amount in the Keynesian cross (multiplier of 1); less in IS-LM because r rises. → [[Lec_07-Aggregate Demand I & the IS-LM Model|Lec 07]]

### IS-LM Equilibrium
Intersection of IS and LM; the starting point for policy analysis. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### Crowding Out
Higher government spending raises r and reduces private investment, so income rises by less than the multiplier; in the classical model it is one for one. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

### IS Shocks
Spending shifts such as confidence, taxes or G that move the IS curve. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### LM Shocks
Money-demand or money-supply changes that move the LM curve. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### Aggregate Demand Curve
Derived from IS-LM: a higher P lowers M/P, shifts LM left, and cuts Y. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### AD-AS Model
Combines the IS-LM-derived AD curve with SRAS and LRAS to trace the short-run to long-run adjustment. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### Monetary-Fiscal Policy Interaction
Fiscal action changes the effect of money policy and vice versa, e.g. holding r or Y constant while the other instrument moves. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### Money Neutrality (LR)
In the long run a money change only moves the price level, leaving Y and r at their natural values. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]

### Fiscal Policy
Government choices on spending G and taxes T used to shift IS. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

### Monetary Policy
Central-bank control of the money supply or the interest rate used to shift LM. → [[Lec_08-Aggregate Demand II & Applying the IS-LM Model|Lec 08]]
Also in Macro (Year 2, Sem 2).

## Open economy and Mundell-Fleming

### Mundell-Fleming Model
IS-LM for a small open economy with perfect capital mobility: r = r*, with IS* and LM* in (Y, e) space. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### IS* Curve
Goods-market equilibrium in (Y, e) space; slopes down because a lower e raises net exports. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### LM* Curve
Money-market equilibrium at r = r*; vertical at the income level set by M/P; under a fixed rate the central bank shifts LM* to defend the peg. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Floating Exchange Rate
Rate set by the market; fiscal and trade shocks move e only (Y fixed by LM*), while money policy moves Y. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Fixed Exchange Rate
Central bank defends the peg and the money supply becomes endogenous; fiscal policy is potent, monetary policy is powerless. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Net Exports
NX = exports - imports; falls when the real exchange rate rises. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Net Capital Outflow
NCO = S - I = NX; domestic saving not used at home and lent abroad. A fall in r raises NCO, supplies more domestic currency and depreciates the exchange rate. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Country Risk Premium
Extra return theta investors demand on a country's assets, r = r* + theta; a rise causes capital flight and depreciation. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Nominal Exchange Rate
Foreign currency per unit of domestic currency, e (a higher e means the home currency appreciates). → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Real Exchange Rate
Relative price of goods across countries, e P/P*. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Capital Mobility
Ability of funds to flow across borders; perfect mobility ties the domestic rate to the world rate. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Beggar-Thy-Neighbour Policy
Boosting domestic output by depreciating the currency, shifting demand from foreign to domestic goods. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Self-Fulfilling Currency Crisis
Expected depreciation raises the risk premium, which causes the actual depreciation. → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

### Large Open Economy
Economy big enough to move the world interest rate, so its interest rate sits between the closed-economy and small-open-economy cases (r not pinned at r*). → [[Lec_09-The Open Economy Revisited & Mundell-Fleming Model|Lec 09]]

## Aggregate supply and the Phillips curve

### Sticky-Price Model
Some firms set prices in advance, so the price level responds only partly to demand and SRAS is upward sloping. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### SRAS Curve
Y = Y-bar + a(P - P^e): output above natural when prices exceed expectations. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Phillips Curve
Inflation = expected inflation - b(u - u_n) + supply shock: the unemployment-inflation trade-off. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Adaptive Expectations
Expected inflation equals last period's inflation. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Rational Expectations
People use all available information, so predicted policy does not create surprises. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Sacrifice Ratio
Percentage points of annual output lost per point of inflation reduced. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Hysteresis
Temporary downturns can raise the natural rate of unemployment permanently. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Inflation Inertia
Past inflation shapes expectations, wages and prices, so inflation persists. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Natural Rate of Unemployment
Rate toward which unemployment gravitates in the long run; where inflation is at expectations. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Cost-Push Inflation
Inflation from adverse supply shocks that raise costs; shifts the Phillips curve up. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Demand-Pull Inflation
Inflation from high aggregate demand pushing unemployment below its natural rate. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Cyclical Unemployment
Deviation of unemployment from its natural rate over the cycle. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Natural-Rate Hypothesis
Demand changes affect output and unemployment only in the short run; in the long run the economy returns to Y-bar and u_n. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Insider-Outsider Dynamics
Employed insiders bargain for themselves while the long-term unemployed lose bargaining power, a source of hysteresis. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Painless Disinflation
The claim that credible disinflation under rational expectations costs no output; the evidence is mixed. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]

### Volcker Disinflation
1979 to 1980 monetary tightening by Fed Chair Volcker to crush 10%+ inflation, at the cost of deep recession. → [[Lec_14-Aggregate Supply & the Phillips Curve|Lec 14]]
