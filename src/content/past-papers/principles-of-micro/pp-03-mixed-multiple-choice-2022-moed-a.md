## What this paper tests

20 multiple-choice questions, no open questions. The paper is a fast sweep of the whole course, so the best revision frame is by topic:

1. **Market mechanics** (Q9, Q10, Q19, Q20): shifts of demand and supply, and which effects are ambiguous. One shift gives a clear answer on price and quantity. Two shifts usually fix only one of the two.
2. **Elasticity and revenue** (Q2, Q7): percentage-change formula, and the revenue test (price up and revenue up means inelastic).
3. **Costs and firm decisions** (Q3, Q4): implicit versus explicit costs, and produce where $P = MC$.
4. **Trade and PPF** (Q8, Q11): opportunity cost decides comparative advantage and the shape of the PPF.
5. **Policy** (Q5, Q12, Q13): price ceilings, and who gets a subsidy.

7. **Externalities and goods** (Q1, Q6, Q18): types of goods, and market versus social optimum.

## Q1 — Which kind of good is a Covid-19 vaccine?

**Question.** Which of the following is true about the Covid-19 vaccine?
a. public good, b. common resource, c. private good, d. club good.

> [!success] Official answer: c (private good)

A good is classified by two tests, see [[Lec_13-Public Goods and International Trade]]:

| | Rival | Not rival |
|---|---|---|
| **Excludable** | Private good | Club good |
| **Not excludable** | Common resource | Public good |

- **Excludable:** whoever holds the vaccine can withhold it from others. The government giving it away free does not make it non-excludable. It is free because of the positive externality of universal vaccination, not because it cannot be withheld.
- **Rival:** one dose goes into one person. If I use it, nobody else can.

Excludable and rival means a **private good**. a is wrong (public needs non-excludable and non-rival), b is wrong (common resource is non-excludable), d is wrong (club good is non-rival).

> [!tip] Intuition
> Free is not the same as non-excludable. Ask "could the owner stop me using it?" If yes, it is excludable.

## Q2 — Price change needed for a 25 unit rise in quantity

**Question.** Price is USD 25 and quantity demanded is 450. By how much must price change (to the nearest whole number) to raise quantity demanded by 25 units, if the price elasticity is $-0.8$? Options: 1) 23, 2) 27, 3) 7, 4) a, b, c all wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: 1) 23

Use $\varepsilon = \%\Delta Q / \%\Delta P$, see [[Lec_08-Elasticity]].

> [!example] Working
> $\%\Delta Q = 25 / 450 = 5.56\%$
>
> $\%\Delta P = 5.56\% / (-0.8) = -6.94\%$
>
> $\Delta P = 25 \times (-0.0694) = -1.74$, so the new price is $25 - 1.74 = 23.26 \approx 23$.

The midpoint method gives about 23.4, which also rounds to 23, so the answer does not depend on the method.

Wrong options: 27 has the wrong sign (a price rise would cut quantity). 7 is the new price after a much too large fall.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Demand is inelastic ($|\varepsilon| < 1$), so a 5.6% quantity rise needs a price fall bigger than 5.6%. That is why the price drops by about 7%.

## Q3 — Economic profit versus accounting profit

**Question.** Mel leaves a job at MAC Cosmetics (salary NIS 60,000 per year) to run her own make-up business. She uses a studio she owns, which she could have rented out for NIS 6,000 per month. Materials, advertising, workers and electricity cost NIS 200,000 per year. Revenue is NIS 350,000 per year. What is the difference between economic and accounting profit? a) 132,000, b) 66,000, c) 150,000, d) 25,000.

> [!success] Official answer: a) 132,000

See [[Lec_05-Supply and Costs of Production]]. Accounting profit subtracts only **explicit costs**. Economic profit also subtracts **implicit costs** (the opportunity cost of her own resources), so the difference between the two is exactly the implicit costs.

> [!example] Working
> Accounting profit $= 350{,}000 - 200{,}000 = 150{,}000$.
>
> Implicit costs $= 60{,}000$ (forgone salary) $+ 6{,}000 \times 12 = 72{,}000$ (forgone rent) $= 132{,}000$.
>
> Economic profit $= 150{,}000 - 132{,}000 = 18{,}000$. The difference is 132,000.

Wrong options: 66,000 uses one month of rent instead of twelve. 150,000 is the accounting profit itself, not the gap. 25,000 matches nothing in the case.

## Q4 — Nuby's profit-maximising output

**Question.** Nuby is a competitive firm. Fixed cost is USD 20 per day, market price is USD 50 per bottle. Total variable cost by bottles per day:

| Bottles | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total variable cost (USD) | 0 | 10 | 30 | 60 | 100 | 150 | 210 |

Which output and daily profit is possible? A) 5 bottles, profit 80. B) 3 bottles, profit 120. C) 6 bottles, profit 100. D) 1 bottle, profit 30.

> [!success] Official answer: A) 5 bottles, daily profit 80

A competitive firm takes the price as given and produces up to where $P = MC$, see [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]]. [[Marginal Cost]] is the change in variable cost per extra bottle: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60.

> [!example] Working
> $MC = 50 = P$ at the **fifth** bottle, so Q = 5.
>
> Profit $= TR - TVC - FC = 50 \times 5 - 150 - 20 = 80$.

Full profit table, with $\pi = 50Q - TVC - 20$:

| Q | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Profit | -20 | 20 | 50 | 70 | 80 | 80 | 70 |

Profit is 80 at both Q = 4 and Q = 5 (at Q = 5, $MC = P$ exactly, so the fifth bottle adds zero). That is why the question says "a possible optimal quantity". Wrong options: B stops early: profit at Q = 3 is 70, not 120. C produces past $P = MC$ (the sixth bottle costs 60 and sells for 50), and profit at 6 is 70, not 100. D stops far too early (profit 20, not 30).

## Q5 — A law banning price increases on masks

**Question.** After a prolonged virus outbreak the price of masks rises sharply. The government passes a law prohibiting price increases for masks during outbreaks. Such a law would most likely: a) make masks more available, b) create a shortage, c) shift demand right, d) shift supply left.

> [!success] Official answer: b) create a shortage of masks

A ban on raising the price is a **price ceiling** held below the new equilibrium, see [[Lec_10-Price Controls]]. At the capped price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a shortage.

- a is wrong: a binding ceiling makes goods less available, because producers supply less at the lower price.
- c and d are wrong: a price control does not shift either curve. It moves the price off the equilibrium along the curves. (The outbreak itself shifted the curves, and that is what raised the price.)

## Q6 — Tobacco: market versus socially optimal quantity

**Question.** Marginal private benefit is $P = 80 - 10Q$, marginal private cost is $P = 10Q$, marginal social cost is $P = 10Q + 20$. What is the difference between the free-market quantity and the socially optimal quantity? A) 1, B) 2, C) 3, D) 4.

> [!success] Official answer: A) 1

See [[Lec_12-Externalities]]. The market uses private cost, society uses social cost. The extra 20 per unit is a [[Negative Externality]] (cost borne by others).

> [!example] Working
> Free market: $80 - 10Q = 10Q$, so $Q = 4$ (price 40).
>
> Social optimum: $80 - 10Q = 10Q + 20$, so $20Q = 60$ and $Q = 3$ (price 50).
>
> Difference $= 4 - 3 = 1$.

![[PP03_q6_tobacco_externality.png|640]]

*Theory diagram. The market produces where private benefit meets private cost (Q = 4). The social optimum is where private benefit meets the higher social cost line (Q = 3). The market overproduces by one unit.*

Wrong options: 2, 3 and 4 are not any of the equilibrium gaps. 3 and 4 are the two quantities themselves, a common slip.

## Q7 — Car prices doubled and revenue rose

**Question.** Car sellers in Israel raised the price because of higher production costs, and revenue then rose. Original price NIS 100,000, original quantity 100, new price NIS 200,000. What is a possible new quantity? a) Q < 50, b) Q = 50, c) Q > 50, d) not enough information.

> [!success] Official answer: c) Q > 50
> No option is highlighted in the key for this question. Option c is taken from the official written explanation.

The official explanation says it directly: the price doubled and revenue went up, so quantity fell by **less than half**. See [[Lec_08-Elasticity]].

> [!example] Working
> Original revenue $= 100{,}000 \times 100 = 10{,}000{,}000$.
>
> New revenue $= 200{,}000 \times Q > 10{,}000{,}000$, so $Q > 50$.

Price up and revenue up means demand is **inelastic** over this range ($|\varepsilon| < 1$): the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price. Also, since the higher cost moves the supply curve and demand slopes down, $Q < 100$. So any $50 < Q < 100$ works. a and b give revenue that is lower or equal. d is wrong, because the revenue information is enough.

> This is solution-file number 8 (paper number 7).

## Q8 — Absolute and comparative advantage, Israel and the UK

**Question.** Hours needed to produce one unit:

| Country | 1 litre of milk | 1 kg of cheese |
|---|---|---|
| Israel | 2 | 6 |
| United Kingdom | 4 | 9 |

a) Israel: absolute advantage in both, comparative advantage in milk. b) Israel: absolute in both, comparative in cheese. c) UK: absolute in both, comparative in cheese. d) UK: absolute in both, comparative in milk.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_02-PPF and Gains from Trade]].

- **Absolute advantage** is fewer hours per unit. Israel needs 2 < 4 for milk and 6 < 9 for cheese, so Israel has the absolute advantage in both. That rules out c and d.
- **Comparative advantage** is lower opportunity cost.

> [!example] Opportunity costs
> Milk: Israel gives up $2/6 = 1/3$ kg of cheese per litre. The UK gives up $4/9 \approx 0.44$ kg. Israel is lower, so Israel has the comparative advantage in **milk**.
>
> Cheese: Israel gives up $6/2 = 3$ litres of milk per kg. The UK gives up $9/4 = 2.25$ litres. The UK is lower, so the UK has the comparative advantage in cheese.

So b is wrong (Israel's comparative advantage is milk, not cheese). A country can hold the absolute advantage in both goods, but never the comparative advantage in both.

> This is solution-file number 9.

## Q9 — Almond milk and cookies get cheaper

**Question.** Milk and almond milk are substitutes. Milk and cookies are complements. The price of almond milk falls and the price of cookies falls. Which is true? a) equilibrium price of milk rises, b) it falls, c) equilibrium quantity rises, d) no conclusion can be drawn about price or quantity.

> [!success] Official answer: d)

See [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]] and [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]].

1. Cheaper almond milk (a substitute) means consumers switch away, so **demand for milk shifts left**.
2. Cheaper cookies (a complement) means people buy more cookies and more milk to go with them, so **demand for milk shifts right**.

The two shifts push in opposite directions. Price and quantity both depend on which shift is bigger, and the question gives no sizes.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> Opposite shifts on the same curve cancel in an unknown amount. When two events move the same curve in opposite directions, price and quantity are both ambiguous. Answers a, b and c each claim something that cannot be shown.

> [!note] Solution file numbering
> This is solution-file number 10.

## Q10 — Coffee after a hurricane and an income rise

**Question.** Coffee is a normal good. October 2019: $Q_s = -4 + 8P$ and $Q_d = 26 - 2P$. In March 2020 (I) a hurricane destroys coffee trees, and (II) consumer income rises. What can be said about equilibrium price and quantity in March 2020? a) price > 3, b) price < 3, c) quantity < 20, d) quantity > 20.

> [!success] Official answer: a) equilibrium price is greater than 3

> [!example] Starting point
> $-4 + 8P = 26 - 2P$, so $10P = 30$, $P = 3$ and $Q = 20$.

| Event | Curve | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| I. Hurricane | Supply shifts left | up | down |
| II. Income up (normal good) | Demand shifts right | up | up |
| **Combined** | | **up (clear)** | **ambiguous** |

Both events push price up, so price is above 3 for certain. They push quantity in opposite directions, so quantity could end up above or below 20, which rules out c and d. b is wrong because price cannot fall. See [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]].

## Q11 — PPF from three types of workers

**Question.** Output if a worker type specialises:

| | X | Y |
|---|---|---|
| Workers C | 10 | 10 |
| Workers B | 70 | 140 |
| Workers A | 40 | 60 |

Which is true?
1. When the two least efficient workers at producing X are put on Y, output is 10 X and 200 Y.
2. The same allocation gives 70 X and 70 Y.
3. The PPF is linear, maximum X is 120 and maximum Y is 210.
4. All of 1, 2, 3 are incorrect.

(The paper labels these a to d, the solution file labels them 1 to 4.)

> [!success] Official answer: 1)

See [[Lec_02-PPF and Gains from Trade]]. The least efficient at X is the one with the **highest opportunity cost of X**.

> [!example] Opportunity cost of one unit of X, in units of Y
> C: $10/10 = 1$. A: $60/40 = 1.5$. B: $140/70 = 2$.

B and A are the least efficient at X, so they go to Y: $Y = 140 + 60 = 200$, and C makes $X = 10$. That is option 1. Option 2 mixes up the numbers and is false.

Option 3 has correct end points ($X_{max} = 10 + 70 + 40 = 120$, $Y_{max} = 10 + 140 + 60 = 210$) but is **false because the PPF is not linear**. Different workers have different opportunity costs, so the frontier is built segment by segment, cheapest X first, and bows outward:

| Segment | Workers moved to X | Slope (Y lost per X) | Point reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | none | | (0, 210) |
| 1 | C | 1 | (10, 200) |
| 2 | A | 1.5 | (50, 140) |
| 3 | B | 2 | (120, 0) |

![[PP03_q11_three_worker_ppf.png|640]]

*Theory diagram. The slope steepens with each segment, which is the increasing opportunity cost that makes the PPF bow out. The point (10, 200) is option 1.*

## Q12 — A price ceiling on Tel Aviv apartments

**Question.** The government caps apartment prices in Tel Aviv to help young couples and students. Which is likely? a) apartments are less likely to be renovated, b) apartments are more affordable so there is no reason for a black market, c) a ceiling can not be binding under any circumstances, d) a, b, c all wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_10-Price Controls]]. A binding ceiling (below the equilibrium rent or price) creates a shortage, and landlords no longer compete on price.

- **a is right:** with excess demand, landlords can rent out apartments in any condition, so there is little reason to spend on upkeep. Quality falls.
- **b is wrong:** a shortage means many people who want an apartment at the capped price cannot get one, which is exactly when black markets and side payments appear.
- **c is wrong:** a ceiling is binding whenever it is set below the equilibrium price. Only a ceiling above equilibrium is non-binding.

## Q13 — A subsidy for young couples in the Negev

**Question.** Young couples who buy an apartment in the Negev get a subsidy of NIS 100,000. Using three diagrams (non-periphery market, periphery couples, the whole Israeli housing market), what happens? Option A: periphery couples pay a lower price and buy more, other buyers face a higher price and buy fewer, total apartments sold in Israel **rise**. Options B to D differ in direction (B and C say the total falls and others' prices fall, C has couples paying more, D has couples buying less and others buying more).

> [!success] Official answer: A)

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]] (a subsidy is the mirror image of a tax) and [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]].

1. The subsidy lets periphery couples pay less out of pocket, so quantity demanded in the periphery market rises.
2. In the whole Israeli market, total demand rises (a new group of buyers is paid to buy). With an upward-sloping supply, the market price rises and the total quantity sold rises.
3. **Other buyers** face that higher price, so they buy fewer apartments.
4. **Periphery couples** pay the new market price minus the subsidy. Because the subsidy is large, their net price is lower than before, and they buy more.

So price up for others, down for couples, and the total up. B and C claim a lower total, which contradicts the rise in total demand. B also has the others' price falling. D has the couples buying less despite being paid to buy.

> [!warning] Wording difference
> Option D in the question paper ("buy a lower quantity... others will buy fewer apartments") reads differently from the solution file ("...will buy more apartments"). The answer A is the same in both files.

## Q14 — Tomato market: equilibrium

**Shared setup for Q14 to Q18.** The tomato market. $P$ is in cents per kg and $Q$ in kg.

$$
D:\; P = 675 - 0.5Q \qquad S:\; P = -225 + 0.25Q
$$

**Question.** What are the equilibrium price and quantity? A) Q = 1200, P = 75. B) Q = 2400, P = 150. C) Q = 900, P = 50. D) Q = 900, P = 100.

> [!success] Official answer: A) Q = 1200, P = 75 cents

> [!example] Working
> Set demand equal to supply: $675 - 0.5Q = -225 + 0.25Q$.
>
> $900 = 0.75Q$, so $Q = 1200$.
>
> $P = 675 - 0.5 \times 1200 = 75$. Check with supply: $-225 + 0.25 \times 1200 = 75$.

See [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]]. Options B, C and D do not satisfy both curves. For example, Q = 900 gives 225 on demand but 0 on supply.

## Q15 — Consumer and producer surplus

**Question.** What are the consumer and producer surplus? A) CS 360,000 and PS 180,000. B) PS 300,000 and CS 350,000. C) CS 720,000 and PS 360,000. D) PS 720,000 and CS 300,000.

> [!success] Official answer: A) CS = 360,000 and PS = 180,000

See [[Lec_09-Welfare Consumer and Producer Surplus]]. Each surplus is a triangle with the equilibrium quantity as its base.

> [!example] Working
> The demand intercept (choke price) is 675 and the supply intercept is $-225$.
>
> $CS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times (675 - 75) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 600 = 360{,}000$.
>
> $PS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times (75 - (-225)) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 300 = 180{,}000$.

![[PP03_q14_tomato_surplus.png|640]]

*Theory diagram. The blue triangle (above the price, below demand) is consumer surplus. The red triangle (below the price, above supply) is producer surplus. The supply curve has a negative intercept, so the PS triangle extends below zero.*

> [!warning] Exam trap
> The supply intercept is negative ($-225$). Using 0 as the bottom of the PS triangle would give PS = 45,000, which is not an option. Use the real intercept. Option C is exactly double the right answer: it forgets the one-half.

## Q16 — A 75 cent tax on tomato producers

**Question.** The government taxes producers 75 cents per kg. Find the new supply curve and the new equilibrium. a) buyers pay 125, Q = 1100, sellers receive 50. b) buyers pay 50, Q = 1100, sellers receive 125. c) buyers pay 75, Q = 900, sellers receive 50. d) a, b, c wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]]. A tax on producers shifts the supply curve **up** by the tax: sellers need 75 more per kg to supply the same quantity.

> [!example] Working
> New supply: $P = -225 + 75 + 0.25Q = -150 + 0.25Q$.
>
> Set equal to demand: $675 - 0.5Q = -150 + 0.25Q$, so $825 = 0.75Q$ and $Q = 1100$.
>
> Buyers pay $675 - 0.5 \times 1100 = 125$ (the price on the demand curve).
>
> Sellers receive $125 - 75 = 50$ (also $-225 + 0.25 \times 1100 = 50$ on the original supply curve).

Buyers' price rose from 75 to 125 (up 50), and sellers' price fell from 75 to 50 (down 25). Buyers bear two thirds of the tax, because demand is less elastic than supply here (slope 0.5 versus 0.25). b swaps the two prices. c has a price gap of only 25, not the tax of 75.

![[PP03_q16_tomato_tax.png|640]]

*Theory diagram. The dashed line is supply after the tax. The gap between the 125 and 50 lines at Q = 1100 equals the tax. The grey triangle is the deadweight loss (Q17).*

## Q17 — Deadweight loss of the tax

**Question.** With the same tax, which is correct? The solution file offers: E) deadweight loss is 3750, F) 7500, G) 150,000, H) a, b, c wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: E) deadweight loss is 3750

> [!example] Working
> $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{tax} \times (Q_{\text{before}} - Q_{\text{after}})$
>
> $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 75 \times (1200 - 1100) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 75 \times 100 = 3750$.

The triangle sits between the demand curve and the original supply curve, from Q = 1100 to Q = 1200, with height 75 at Q = 1100.

> [!warning] The question paper lists different options
> The solution file offers 3750 as option E. The **uploaded question paper** instead lists E) 750 and F) 375 (with G) 150,000 and H) none of them). On that wording 3750 is not on the list, so the right pick there would be H. The official key highlights 3750, which suggests it was written for a corrected set of options. Keep 3750 as the official answer (it matches my own working above). Both 750 and 375 are the result of dropping a factor of 5 or 10.

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]] and [[Lec_09-Welfare Consumer and Producer Surplus]].

## Q18 — Hitting Q = 1300 with a positive externality

**Question.** Tomatoes turn out to have a positive externality. The government wants the new equilibrium quantity to be 1300. What can it do? A) a subsidy of 100 cents. B) a subsidy of 75 cents. C) a Pigouvian tax of 75 cents. D) a, b, c wrong. (The paper labels these I to L.)

> [!success] Official answer: B) a subsidy of 75 cents

See [[Lec_12-Externalities]]. A positive externality means the market produces too little, so the fix is a **subsidy**, which pushes the quantity up. A tax (option C) would push it down, away from 1300.

> [!example] Working
> At Q = 1300 buyers are willing to pay $675 - 0.5 \times 1300 = 25$.
>
> Producers need $-225 + 0.25 \times 1300 = 100$ to supply 1300.
>
> The gap is $100 - 25 = 75$, so the subsidy is **75 cents**.
>
> Check: subsidised supply is $P = -300 + 0.25Q$. Setting it equal to demand gives $675 - 0.5Q = -300 + 0.25Q$. That is $975 = 0.75Q$ and $Q = 1300$.

A subsidy of 100 overshoots: $675 - 0.5Q = -325 + 0.25Q$ gives $Q = 1333.3$, not 1300.

![[PP03_q18_tomato_subsidy.png|640]]

*Theory diagram. At Q = 1300 buyers pay 25 and sellers receive 100, a gap of 75 per kg that the government pays.*

## Q19 — Toilet paper in a perfectly competitive market

**Question.** Population keeps rising, so demand for toilet paper increases. What happens? a) a rise in the long-run price and a rise in the long-run quantity supplied. b) a rise in the price in the short run and a rise in the quantity supplied in the long run. c) the demand curve rises and the supply curve falls. d) a rise in the price in the short run and a fall in the quantity supplied in the long run.

> [!success] Official answer: b)

See [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]].

1. **Short run:** demand shifts right, so the price rises. Existing firms earn positive economic profit.
2. **Long run:** the profit attracts new firms. Supply shifts right, pushing the price back down toward the minimum of average total cost. The industry's quantity ends up higher than before.

So price rises in the short run, and quantity supplied is higher in the long run. a is wrong because entry drives the long-run price back to its original level (this is the standard constant-cost case). c is wrong because supply shifts right, not left. d is wrong because long-run quantity rises, it does not fall.

## Q20 — The lemon market

**Question.** Which statement can be true? a) A fall in the price of lemons shifts the supply curve left. b) A rise in the price of lemon seeds decreases the quantity supplied of lemons. c) A rise in the efficiency of lemon pickers shifts supply right. d) Expectations that lemon seeds will become cheaper shift supply right.

> [!success] Official answer: c)

See [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]]. Supply shifts only when something **other than the good's own price** changes (input prices, technology and productivity, expectations, number of sellers).

- **c is right:** more efficient pickers lower the cost of each lemon, so supply shifts right.
- **a is wrong:** the price of lemons itself only moves producers **along** the supply curve (a fall in quantity supplied), it does not shift it.
- **b is wrong in wording:** dearer seeds raise costs and shift supply **left**. That is a decrease in supply, not a decrease in "quantity supplied", which is a movement along the curve.
- **d is not the official answer.** Expecting cheaper seeds in future gives a firm a reason to delay some buying and planting, so if anything current supply moves left, not right.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Change in quantity supplied means a move along the curve (own price). Change in supply means the whole curve shifts (anything else).
