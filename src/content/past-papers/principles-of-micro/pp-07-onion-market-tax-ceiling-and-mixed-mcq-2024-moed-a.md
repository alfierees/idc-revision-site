## What this paper tests

1. **One onion market, five questions (Q1 to Q5).** Linear demand and supply, then surplus, a per-unit tax, deadweight loss and a price ceiling. Solve the equilibrium once and reuse it.
2. **Qualitative market effects (Q6, Q7, Q9, Q11, Q12, Q13).** Minimum wage, subsidy, tax burden, shifts of demand and supply. Always ask which curve moves and which way.
3. **Firm and cost questions (Q10, Q14, Q15, Q18).** Profit maximisation at P = MC, and fixed cost from profit data, opportunity cost and the long-run zero-profit condition.
4. **Trade and elasticity (Q16, Q17, Q20).** Comparative advantage from opportunity costs, and the midpoint method.
5. **Goods and externalities (Q8, Q19).** Rival or excludable, and the education externality graph.

> [!warning] Same data, several questions
> Q1 to Q5 all use $P = 16 - 0.02Q$ (demand) and $P = 0.02Q$ (supply), with $Q$ in thousands of kg and $P$ in NIS per kg.

# Onion market (Q1 to Q5)

Demand: $P = 16 - 0.02Q_d$. Supply: $P = 0.02Q_s$. Quantities are in thousands of kg.

## Q1 — Equilibrium price and quantity

Set the two price equations equal:

$$
16 - 0.02Q = 0.02Q
$$

$$
16 = 0.04Q \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q^* = 400
$$

Then $P^* = 0.02 \times 400 = 8$.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Equilibrium quantity 400,000 kg and price 8 NIS.

- **b** (600, 3.6) and **c** (200, 4) are not on both curves. At Q = 200 demand gives 12 and supply gives 4, so the market is not in equilibrium. Quantity 600 is far beyond where the curves cross.
- **d** is wrong because a is right.

![[PP07_q1_equilibrium_surplus.png|640]]

*Both curves meet at P = 8, Q = 400. The shaded triangles are the surpluses used in Q2.*

See [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]].

## Q2 — Consumer surplus versus producer surplus

Surplus triangles share the base $Q^* = 400$ and have heights set by the demand intercept (16) and supply intercept (0) measured from $P^* = 8$.

$$
CS = \tfrac12 \times 400 \times (16 - 8) = 1600
$$

$$
PS = \tfrac12 \times 400 \times (8 - 0) = 1600
$$

> [!success] Answer: a
> Consumer surplus and producer surplus are equal (1,600 in thousand NIS each).

- **b** is wrong because the two triangles are the same size.
- **c** is wrong: the equations are given, so the surpluses can be calculated.

> [!tip] Intuition
> The demand curve starts 8 above the equilibrium price and the supply curve starts 8 below it. Same height, same base, same area.

See [[Lec_09-Welfare Consumer and Producer Surplus]].

## Q3 — Tax of 1 NIS on suppliers

A per-unit tax on sellers shifts the supply curve up by the tax. New supply:

$$
P = 0.02Q + 1
$$

Equate with demand:

$$
16 - 0.02Q = 0.02Q + 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 15 = 0.04Q \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q = 375
$$

The buyer pays the price on the demand curve at 375: $16 - 0.02(375) = 8.5$. The seller keeps the buyer's price minus the tax: $8.5 - 1 = 7.5$ (check: $0.02 \times 375 = 7.5$).

> [!success] Answer: a
> Buyers pay 8.5, the quantity is 375,000 kg, and sellers receive 7.5.

- **b** swaps the two prices: buyers always pay more than sellers receive when a tax is imposed.
- **c** has a quantity of 300 and prices 9 and 6, which do not lie on the curves. Demand at Q = 300 gives 10 and supply gives 6.
- Tax incidence: buyers pay 0.5 more than before, sellers receive 0.5 less. The burden splits equally because the curves have the same slope magnitude.

![[PP07_q3_tax_dwl.png|640]]

*The tax wedge is 1 NIS (8.5 minus 7.5). Quantity falls from 400 to 375. The dark triangle is the deadweight loss.*

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]].

## Q4 — Deadweight loss of the tax

The deadweight loss is the triangle between the old and new quantities with height equal to the tax:

$$
DWL = \tfrac12 \times \text{tax} \times \Delta Q = \tfrac12 \times 1 \times (400 - 375) = 12.5
$$

In thousand units that is 12.5 thousand NIS, shown in the options as 12,500.

> [!success] Answer: a (12,500)
> The key marks 12,500, read as the 12.5 above in thousands.

- **b** (25,000) is the rectangle $1 \times 25$ with the one-half left out, doubling the true loss.
- **c** (10,000) does not come from any base and height here.

> [!warning] Units
> The question says "in thousands" while the options read 12,500. The triangle gives 12.5 when Q is in thousands. That is what 12,500 refers to, so the answer is unambiguous.

## Q5 — Price ceiling at P = 6

The ceiling is below the equilibrium price of 8, so it binds.

$$
Q_d: \; 6 = 16 - 0.02Q_d \;\Rightarrow\; Q_d = 500
$$

$$
Q_s: \; 6 = 0.02Q_s \;\Rightarrow\; Q_s = 300
$$

Shortage $= 500 - 300 = 200$.

> [!success] Answer: a
> $Q_d = 500$K, $Q_s = 300$K, a shortage of 200K.

- **b** reverses demand and supply, which would describe a price floor creating a surplus.
- **c** is off by a factor of ten.

> [!tip] Intuition
> A price below equilibrium makes buyers want more and sellers offer less. A binding ceiling always produces a shortage.

![[PP07_q5_price_ceiling.png|640]]

*At P = 6, sellers supply 300 and buyers demand 500. The gap is the shortage.*

See [[Lec_10-Price Controls]].

# Stand-alone questions

## Q6 — Minimum wage and unemployment

> [!question] Question
> The biggest labor union in Israel has fought and achieved an increase in the minimum wage. Which is correct? (a) Unemployment will develop and will be bigger if the elasticity of demand and elasticity of supply are bigger than 1. (b) ... smaller than 1. (c) ... equal to 1. (d) All the answers are wrong.

A minimum wage is a price floor in the labour market. If it is set above the equilibrium wage, workers supplied exceeds workers demanded, so unemployment appears. The more elastic labour demand and labour supply are, the more quantities respond to the higher wage, so the gap between them is bigger.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Unemployment develops, and it is larger when both elasticities are greater than 1.

- **b** is the reverse: with inelastic curves, quantities barely respond, so the gap is small.
- **c** is a single point on the spectrum, not the condition that makes unemployment bigger.

See [[Lec_10-Price Controls]] and [[Lec_08-Elasticity]].

## Q7 — Subsidy to electric vehicle producers

A per-unit subsidy to producers shifts supply to the right, down by the subsidy. Equilibrium quantity rises, the price consumers pay falls, and the effective price producers receive (market price plus subsidy) rises.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Producers receive more, consumers pay less, and the equilibrium quantity increases.

- **b** has the prices backwards, which is what a tax does.
- **c** has the right prices but a falling quantity, which contradicts the rightward shift.

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]] (a subsidy is a negative tax).

## Q8 — Air Force show: what type of good?

Watching the planes costs nothing and nobody can be stopped from looking, so the good is **non-excludable**. One person's viewing does not reduce another's, and the question says the beach is not congested, so it is **non-rival**.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Non-excludable and non-rival: a public good.

- **b** (excludable) is wrong because no one can be blocked from watching.
- **c** (rival) is wrong because the beach is not full, so more viewers do not reduce the show for others.

> [!warning] The "not congested" clause matters
> If the beach were crowded, viewing space would become rival, and the good would turn into a common resource. The wording rules that out.

See [[Lec_13-Public Goods and International Trade]].

## Q9 — Tax on cigarettes

A tax lowers the equilibrium quantity, raises the price buyers pay, and lowers the price sellers receive. The side that is less elastic bears more of the burden. Cigarettes are addictive, so demand is inelastic: smokers keep buying when the price rises, and consumers carry the bigger share.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Quantity falls, consumers pay more, producers receive less, and the bigger share of the burden falls on consumers.

- **b** puts the bigger burden on producers, which would need demand to be more elastic than supply.
- **c** has the prices moving the wrong way: a tax never lowers the price buyers pay.

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]] and [[Lec_08-Elasticity]].

## Q10 — Competitive firm: profit-maximising quantity

A price taker in a competitive market maximises profit where price equals marginal cost, on the **rising** part of MC. In the graph, the horizontal demand line at $P^*$ crosses MC on its rising branch at **Q3**. At Q3, ATC lies below $P^*$, so price exceeds average cost and profit per unit is positive.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Q3, profits are positive.

- **b**: Q4 is where ATC meets $P^*$ (zero profit), but producing beyond Q3 means MC exceeds price on each extra unit, so profit falls.
- **c**: Q1 is where $P^* = MC$ on the **falling** part of MC, a profit minimum rather than a maximum.

![[PP07_q10_firm_profit_max.png|640]]

*Schematic reconstruction of the exam graph (not to scale): produce where P* equals MC on the rising branch. The shaded rectangle is profit because P* exceeds ATC there.*

See [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]].

## Q11 — Which event lowers the equilibrium price of oranges?

> [!question] Options (key's wording)
> (a) New studies suggest oranges contain traces of cancer-causing substances due to pesticide residue. (b) Weather this growing season is worse than last year. (c) The price of apples, a substitute, is higher this year. (d) Firms in the orange industry launch an effective advertising campaign.

The price falls when demand falls or supply rises.

- **a**: the health scare makes consumers want fewer oranges, demand shifts left, price falls. Correct.
- **b**: bad weather cuts supply, which raises price.
- **c**: dearer apples push consumers toward oranges, demand shifts right, price rises.
- **d**: effective advertising raises demand, which raises price.

> [!success] Answer: a

See [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]] and [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]].

## Q12 — Cheaper lemons and the lemonade market

Lemons are an input. A lower input price cuts production costs, so supply increases and shifts right. With demand unchanged, the equilibrium price falls and the quantity rises.

> [!success] Answer: c

- **a** has supply shifting left, which a rise in input cost would do.
- **b** has the right shift but a higher price, which is impossible when only supply shifts right.

See [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]].

## Q13 — Eggs: chicken flu and a taste for protein

Starting equilibrium: $120 - 3P = 40 + 2P$, so $80 = 5P$, $P = 16$, $Q = 120 - 48 = 72$.

- Event I (flu kills chickens) is a fall in supply: supply shifts left. Price up, quantity down.
- Event II (preferences toward eggs) is a rise in demand: demand shifts right. Price up, quantity up.

Both shifts push the price up. The quantity effects point in opposite directions, so the net change in quantity is ambiguous without numbers.

> [!success] Answer: a
> The price of eggs is now higher than 16.

- **b** is impossible because both shifts raise price.
- **c** and **d** each claim a definite quantity direction, but the two shifts offset each other there, so neither can be guaranteed.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Two shifts that agree on one variable (price) and disagree on the other (quantity): the agreed variable is certain, the other is indeterminate.

See [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]] and [[Simultaneous Shifts]].

## Q14 — Sarah's bakery: fixed cost

Per-loaf profit is price minus average total cost, where ATC = AVC + AFC:

$$
5 - (1.25 + 0.75) = 3
$$

Total profit is 3,000, so quantity is $3000 / 3 = 1000$. Fixed cost is average fixed cost times quantity:

$$
FC = 0.75 \times 1000 = 750
$$

> [!success] Answer: a (750)

- **b** (1,000) is the quantity, not the fixed cost.
- **c** (1,250) is variable cost: $1.25 \times 1000$.

See [[Lec_05-Supply and Costs of Production]] and [[Lec_14-Production Costs Worksheet]].

## Q15 — John's workout studio

Economic profit subtracts **all** opportunity costs. Per year:

- Forgone salary at Holmes Place: 150,000
- Forgone rent on the basement: $2000 \times 12 = 24{,}000$
- Upkeep and electricity: $750 \times 12 = 9{,}000$

Total economic cost: $150{,}000 + 24{,}000 + 9{,}000 = 183{,}000$ per year, or 15,250 per month. At 200 NIS per hour, break-even is $15{,}250 / 200 = 76.25$ hours per month, or $183{,}000 / 200 = 915$ hours per year.

- **a**: 77 hours per month earns 15,400 > 15,250, so economic profit is positive. Correct.
- **b**: 900 hours per year earns 180,000 < 183,000, so economic profit is negative.
- **c**: 1,000 hours per year earns 200,000 > 183,000, so economic profit is positive, not negative.

> [!success] Answer: a

> [!tip] Intuition
> Salary and rent he gives up are real costs of the studio even though he writes no cheque for them.

See [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]], [[Opportunity Cost]] and [[Economic Profit]].

## Q16 — New Zealand and Ghana

Hours needed per unit: New Zealand needs 4 for a bottle of wine and 2 for a dress; Ghana needs 6 and 4.

Opportunity cost of one bottle of wine, in dresses:

$$
\text{New Zealand: } 4/2 = 2 \qquad \text{Ghana: } 6/4 = 1.5
$$

Ghana gives up fewer dresses per bottle, so Ghana has the comparative advantage in wine.

> [!success] Answer: b

- **a** is wrong: New Zealand's opportunity cost is higher (2 dresses).
- **c** is wrong: absolute advantage means using fewer hours. New Zealand uses fewer hours for both goods (4 versus 6 and 2 versus 4), so it holds the absolute advantage in both.

> [!warning] Absolute versus comparative
> New Zealand is better at both goods, yet Ghana still has the comparative advantage in wine. Comparative advantage depends on opportunity cost, not on being the best.

See [[Lec_02-PPF and Gains from Trade]], [[Comparative Advantage]] and [[Absolute Advantage]].

## Q17 — Elasticity by the midpoint method

Market A: price falls from 250 to 200, quantity rises from 8 to 12.

$$
\%\Delta Q = \frac{12 - 8}{(12 + 8)/2} = \frac{4}{10} = 40\%
$$

$$
\%\Delta P = \frac{200 - 250}{(200 + 250)/2} = \frac{-50}{225} \approx -22.2\%
$$

$$
E_d = \frac{40\%}{-22.2\%} = -1.8
$$

> [!success] Official answer: b
> The key highlights b: consumers in market B are not very responsive to a change in price.

> [!warning] Official answer looks wrong
> The midpoint elasticity is $-1.8$, so $|E_d| > 1$ and demand is **elastic**, which means quantity is *very* responsive: option **a**. Option b says the opposite. The key's mark is kept above, as the official answer. Option **c** ($-2.5$) is what you get using the starting point instead of the midpoint: $50\% / (-20\%) = -2.5$, which is the intended trap. Check this one with your lecturer before relying on it.

See [[Lec_08-Elasticity]], [[Midpoint Method]] and [[Price Elasticity of Demand]].

## Q18 — Denis Corp in long-run equilibrium

A price-taking firm in a competitive market has $P = MC$, so $P = 5$. Total revenue is $P \times Q$:

$$
Q = 1000 / 5 = 200
$$

Zero economic profit means $TC = TR = 1000$. Variable cost is $AVC \times Q = 4 \times 200 = 800$, so

$$
FC = TC - VC = 1000 - 800 = 200
$$

> [!success] Answer: a
> Output 200, fixed cost 200.

- **b** and **c** have output 250, which would need $P = 4$, but price must equal $MC = 5$.

See [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]].

## Q19 — Education externality graph

The marginal social benefit curve lies **above** the marginal private benefit curve, so education has a positive externality. The market produces where private benefit meets cost (the lower quantity), which is less than the socially optimal quantity where social benefit meets cost. The standard fix is a subsidy, which lifts private benefit toward social benefit.

> [!success] Answer: a
> Consumers of education could be subsidised to reach the socially optimal quantity.

- **b** is wrong: the market quantity is **smaller** than optimal, not larger.
- **c** is wrong: taxing producers raises cost and reduces quantity even further.

![[PP07_q19_education_externality.png|640]]

*Schematic of the exam graph (not to scale). With MSB above MPB, the market quantity is below the optimum, so a subsidy moves the market toward it.*

See [[Lec_12-Externalities]] and [[Positive Externality]].

## Q20 — Robert and Frank: palm leaves and coconuts

Daily maximum: Robert picks 8 palm leaves or 2 coconuts; Frank picks 10 palm leaves or 5 coconuts.

Opportunity cost of one coconut in palm leaves:

$$
\text{Robert: } 8/2 = 4 \qquad \text{Frank: } 10/5 = 2
$$

Frank gives up fewer leaves per coconut, so Frank has the comparative advantage in coconuts and Robert in palm leaves. Both gain from a trade price strictly between 2 and 4 leaves per coconut.

> [!success] Answer: c
> A price of 3 palm leaves per coconut is inside the range (2, 4).

- **a** is wrong: Robert's advantage is in palm leaves.
- **b** (1 leaf per coconut) is below Frank's cost of 2, so Frank would be worse off.

See [[Lec_02-PPF and Gains from Trade]].
