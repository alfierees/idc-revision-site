## Q1 to Q5 — The apple market

**Setup.** The market for apples. $P$ is the price of 1 kg of apples in USD and $Q$ is the number of kg sold. The diagram shows an upward supply curve and a downward demand curve crossing at $(Q_0, P_0)$. The equations are

$$
D:\ P = 2.25 - 0.03\,Q
$$

$$
S:\ P = 0.02\,Q
$$

![[PP05_q1_apple_market_tax.png|760]]

The left panel is the market before the tax and the right panel is the market after the USD 1 per kg tax, with every area used in Q2 to Q5. All numbers on it come from the solutions of Q1 to Q5.

### Q1 — Equilibrium price and quantity

**Question.** What is the equilibrium price and quantity? A) 45 kg and USD 0.9, B) 90 kg and USD 3.6, C) 85 kg and USD 4, D) none of these.

> [!success] Official answer: A) 45 kg and USD 0.9

Set demand equal to supply, see [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]]:

> [!example] Working
> $2.25 - 0.03Q = 0.02Q$, so $2.25 = 0.05Q$ and $Q = 45$.
>
> $P = 0.02 \times 45 = 0.9$. Check on demand: $2.25 - 0.03 \times 45 = 2.25 - 1.35 = 0.9$.

Options B and C do not lie on both curves. For example at $Q = 90$ demand gives $P = 2.25 - 2.7 < 0$.

### Q2 — Consumer and producer surplus

**Question.** What are the consumer surplus and the producer surplus? A) 30.375 and 20.250, B) 20.245 and 31.125, C) 33.125 and 40.514, D) none of these.

> [!success] Official answer: A) CS = 30.375 and PS = 20.250

Both surpluses are triangles, see [[Lec_09-Welfare Consumer and Producer Surplus]]. The demand intercept is 2.25 and the supply intercept is 0.

> [!example] Working
> Consumer surplus: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 45 \times (2.25 - 0.9) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 45 \times 1.35 = 30.375$.
>
> Producer surplus: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 45 \times (0.9 - 0) = 20.25$.

### Q3 — Supply curve and prices after a USD 1 tax on sellers

**Question.** The government taxes sellers USD 1 per kg. Find the supply curve after the tax. A) buyers pay 1.5, quantity 25 kg, sellers receive 0.5. B) buyers pay 2, quantity 45 kg, sellers receive 1.5. C) buyers pay 2.25, quantity 105 kg, sellers receive 1.25. D) none of these.

> [!success] Official answer: A) buyers pay 1.5, quantity 25 kg, sellers receive 0.5

A tax on sellers raises their cost by the tax on every unit, so the supply curve shifts **up** by USD 1, see [[Lec_11-Taxes]]. The new supply curve is the price the buyer pays:

$$
S_{\text{after tax}}:\ P_{\text{buyer}} = 0.02\,Q + 1
$$

> [!example] Working
> $2.25 - 0.03Q = 0.02Q + 1$, so $1.25 = 0.05Q$ and $Q = 25$.
>
> Buyer pays $P = 2.25 - 0.03 \times 25 = 1.5$.
>
> Seller receives $1.5 - 1 = 0.5$. Check on the old supply curve: $0.02 \times 25 = 0.5$.

Options B and C both keep a quantity of 45 or more, but a tax can only reduce the quantity traded. Option B also has a gap of 0.5 between the buyer price and seller price, not the tax of 1.

### Q4 — Deadweight loss

**Question.** What is the deadweight loss associated with the tax? A) 10, B) 20, C) 30, D) all wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: A) 10

The deadweight loss is the triangle between the lost units (from 25 up to 45) and the gap between demand and old supply. The height at $Q = 25$ is the tax, 1 (1.5 minus 0.5).

> [!example] Working
> $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{tax} \times \Delta Q = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times (45 - 25) = 10$.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Check with the surplus totals. Before the tax, $CS + PS = 30.375 + 20.25 = 50.625$. After the tax, $CS + PS + \text{revenue} = 9.375 + 6.25 + 25 = 40.625$. The difference is 10, the deadweight loss.

### Q5 — Surplus and government revenue after the tax

**Question.** Calculate the consumer surplus, producer surplus and government revenue after the tax. A) CS 9.375, PS 6.250, revenue 25. B) CS 9.523, PS 5.342, revenue 45. C) CS 9.125, PS 6.534, revenue 12.5. D) none of these.

> [!success] Official answer: A) CS = 9.375, PS = 6.250, revenue = 25

Use the new quantity 25, the buyer price 1.5 and the seller price 0.5 from Q3.

> [!example] Working
> Consumer surplus: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 25 \times (2.25 - 1.5) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 25 \times 0.75 = 9.375$.
>
> Producer surplus: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 25 \times (0.5 - 0) = 6.25$.
>
> Government revenue: $\text{tax} \times Q = 1 \times 25 = 25$.

Option B uses a revenue of 45 (the old quantity times the tax), forgetting that quantity falls to 25.

## Q6 — Potatoes: an inferior good and two shocks

**Question.** Potatoes are an inferior good. January 2022: $Q_s = -2 + 4P$ and $Q_d = 13 + P$. January 2023: (I) new picking machines speed up harvesting, and (II) because of Covid-19 many consumers lost their jobs, so overall income fell. Which claim can be made about the January 2023 equilibrium? a. quantity is greater than 18, b. price is greater than 5, c. quantity is less than 18, d. price is less than 5.

> [!success] Official answer: a. equilibrium quantity is greater than 18

See [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]]. First find the 2022 benchmark.

> [!example] Working
> $-2 + 4P = 13 + P$, so $3P = 15$, $P = 5$ and $Q = 13 + 5 = 18$.

Now take each shock:

- **I. Machines:** a technology improvement lowers cost, so supply shifts **right** (more at every price). Price tends down, quantity up.
- **II. Income falls, inferior good:** income and demand move in **opposite** directions for an inferior good, so demand shifts **right** too. Price tends up, quantity up.

Both curves shift right, so quantity clearly rises above 18. The two shifts push the price in opposite directions, so the price change is **ambiguous**. That rules out b and d, and c is the wrong direction for quantity.

![[PP05_q6_potato_two_shifts.png|520]]

> [!tip] Intuition
> The 2022 point (5, 18) is from the equations. The size of each 2023 shift is arbitrary on this figure: only the directions matter. Both shifts go right, so quantity rises whatever the sizes.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> If potatoes were a normal good, lost income would shift demand **left**. Then price would clearly fall but quantity would be ambiguous. The word "inferior" is what flips the answer.

## Q7 — Gasoline supply: the four-event path

**Question.** Graph 1 shows supply curves for the gasoline market (five parallel upward lines, labelled points on them). Start at point A. After the four consecutive events, where are we? Shifts of the curve go to the line directly above or below, movements along the curve go to the point directly above or below, and there is no demand in this question. Events: 1) technology for producing gasoline improves, 2) the price of gasoline rises, 3) producers expect prices to fall in the future, 4) the price of hydrocarbons used to produce gasoline rises. Options: a) I, b) F, c) K, d) M.

> [!success] Official answer: a) I

On the graph the points sit on five parallel supply lines. Reading from the left, line 1 holds M and N, line 2 holds B, A and C, line 3 holds I, H and G, line 4 holds L, K and J, and line 5 holds F, E and D. Within each line the points go top to bottom, from a high price to a low price. A moves to the next line to the **right** when supply increases, and to the line on the left when supply decreases. See [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]] for the determinants of supply.

| Step | Event | What moves | Move | Point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | | | | A (line 2, middle price) |
| 1 | Technology improves | Supply increases (shift right) | A to the line on the right | H (line 3) |
| 2 | Price of gasoline rises | Movement along the curve (price up, quantity supplied up) | H to the point directly above | I (line 3, top) |
| 3 | Producers expect lower future prices | Supply increases now (sell today rather than later): shift right | I to the line on the right | L (line 4, top) |
| 4 | Input price (hydrocarbons) rises | Supply decreases: shift left | L to the line on the left | I (line 3, top) |

The final point is **I**, option a.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> Step 2 is a change in **price**, so it is a movement **along** the curve, not a shift. Step 3 is an expectation: expecting a lower price later makes sellers offer more today, so supply shifts right. If you read step 3 backwards (supply falls), you end at F or M instead.

## Q8 — Petrol cars: four demand events

**Question.** Market for petrol cars. Each shift or movement has the same magnitude. Events: 1) the country lowers the legal driving age from 16 to 14, 2) consumers expect car prices to rise in the next years, 3) consumers receive a government subsidy for electric cars, 4) overall consumer income rises. Which is correct after the four events? a) demand for petrol cars increased, b) supply increased, c) demand decreased, d) supply decreased.

> [!success] Official answer: a) the demand for petrol cars increased

All four events move **demand**, not supply, see [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]].

| Event | Determinant of demand | Effect on demand for petrol cars |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Driving age 16 to 14 | Number of buyers | Right (more potential buyers) |
| 2. Expect higher future prices | Expectations | Right (buy earlier, today) |
| 3. Subsidy for electric cars | Price of a related good (electric cars are a substitute) | **Left** (cheaper substitute) |
| 4. Income rises | Income (petrol cars are a normal good) | Right |

Three shifts go right and one goes left, all of the same size, so the net effect is one shift to the right. Demand increased. b and d are wrong because none of the events changes the cost of producing petrol cars, and c has the wrong direction.

## Q9 — David's tutoring business

**Question.** David leaves a job as a math tutor at Chegg (salary USD 4,000 per month) to start his own tutoring business. He charges USD 200 per hour. He teaches in a workspace he owns, which he could rent out for USD 1,500 per month. His annual costs (electricity, materials, advertising and so on) are USD 50,000 per year. How many hours must he teach in a year to reach zero economic profit? A) 580, B) 530, C) 500, D) 480.

> [!success] Official answer: A) 580

Zero economic profit means revenue equals **explicit plus implicit** costs, see [[Lec_05-Supply and Costs of Production]].

> [!example] Working
> Explicit costs: 50,000 per year.
>
> Implicit costs: forgone salary $4{,}000 \times 12 = 48{,}000$, plus forgone rent $1{,}500 \times 12 = 18{,}000$.
>
> Total economic cost: $50{,}000 + 48{,}000 + 18{,}000 = 116{,}000$.
>
> Hours: $116{,}000 / 200 = 580$.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> Dropping the owned workspace gives $98{,}000 / 200 = 490$ (not an option). Dropping the salary gives 340. The rent on a space he owns is still an opportunity cost.

## Q10 — Milk, unit-elastic demand

**Question.** Price elasticity of demand for milk is unit elastic. The original price was USD 6 and the original quantity was 8,000. The price falls to USD 4. What is the new quantity? a) 12,000, b) 4,000, c) 10,000, d) 6,000.

> [!success] Official answer: a) 12,000

Unit elasticity means total revenue does not change when the price changes, see [[Lec_08-Elasticity]].

> [!example] Working
> Revenue before: $6 \times 8{,}000 = 48{,}000$.
>
> New quantity: $48{,}000 / 4 = 12{,}000$.

b and d are lower quantities (a price fall cannot reduce quantity demanded). c would give revenue of 40,000, a fall, which means inelastic demand.

> [!tip] Intuition
> A fall from 6 to 4 is a one-third cut in price. Unit elasticity needs the quantity to rise to a level that exactly offsets it: 8,000 times 6/4 equals 12,000.

## Q11 — Gladword opens to trade, then a tariff

**Question.** Gladword has two goods. Before trade (2016), maple syrup: domestic price USD 4 per litre, world price USD 6. Honey: domestic price USD 15, world price USD 10. In 2017 the government imposes a tariff on all imports. Which statement is accurate?

- a) 2016: export maple syrup and import honey. Consumer surplus falls in the maple syrup market, producer surplus falls in the honey market. 2017: the tariff gives a lower consumer surplus and higher producer surplus in the honey market than in 2016.
- b) 2016: export honey and import maple syrup. Producer surplus in the maple syrup market falls, consumer surplus in the honey market falls. 2017: a higher consumer surplus and lower producer surplus in the honey market.
- c) 2016: export maple syrup and import honey. Producer surplus in the maple syrup market falls, consumer surplus in the honey market falls. 2017: a lower consumer surplus and higher producer surplus in the maple syrup market.
- d) 2016: export honey and import maple syrup. Consumer surplus in the maple syrup market falls, producer surplus in the honey market falls. 2017: a lower consumer surplus and higher producer surplus in the maple syrup market.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_13-Public Goods and International Trade]]. A country **exports** a good when the world price is above the domestic price, and **imports** it when the world price is below.

- **Maple syrup:** world price 6 is above the domestic price 4, so Gladword **exports** it. The domestic price rises to 6, so consumers lose (consumer surplus falls) and producers gain.
- **Honey:** world price 10 is below the domestic price 15, so Gladword **imports** it. The domestic price falls to 10, so consumers gain and producers lose (producer surplus falls).
- **2017 tariff on imports:** the only imported good is honey. A tariff raises the domestic honey price above 10. Relative to free trade (2016), consumer surplus **falls** and producer surplus **rises**, and the government collects revenue. Maple syrup is exported, not imported, so the tariff does not touch it.

Why the others fail: b and d reverse who exports and who imports. c gets the 2016 trade pattern right but then claims the tariff affects the maple syrup market, which it does not.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> Option c is tempting because its 2016 half is correct and its tariff half sounds plausible. The error is in the last clause: a tariff is paid on imports, and maple syrup is an export, so its market is not the one that changes.

## Q12 — Rental apartments (cancelled)

**Question.** Group A believes rents are too low and must be higher to encourage investment in rental space. Group B believes rents are too high and hurt renters. Which statement is most correct? Options A to D (printed in the paper) mix up price floor and ceiling and the surplus and shortage each causes.

> [!success] Official: question cancelled; the intended answer was A (corrected wording)

The examiners cancelled it because the printed option A said "shortage if the **price floor** is binding" in its second half, instead of "price ceiling". The corrected option A reads:

> Group A will advocate for a **price floor**, which will cause a rental apartment **surplus** if the price floor is binding. Group B will advocate for a **price ceiling**, which will cause a rental apartment **shortage** if the price ceiling is binding.

See [[Lec_10-Price Controls]]:

- A **price floor** (a minimum rent) helps those who want a higher price. It binds only when it is **above** equilibrium, and then quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a **surplus** (vacant apartments).
- A **price ceiling** (a maximum rent) helps those who want a lower price. It binds only when it is **below** equilibrium, and then quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a **shortage**.
- B, C and D each swap which group wants which control, or which condition causes which result.

## Q13 — Falafel and shakshuka (cancelled)

**Question.** Steven owns a restaurant with two employees and must decide who makes falafel and who makes shakshuka. Hours needed:

| Employee | Hours for 1 kg falafel | Hours for 1 kg shakshuka |
|---|---|---|
| Josh | 8 | 2 |
| Henry | 2 | 1 |

Options a) Henry makes falafel, his opportunity cost of 1 kg falafel is 4 kg shakshuka. b) Josh makes falafel, his opportunity cost is 1/4 kg shakshuka. c) Henry makes falafel, his opportunity cost is 1/2 kg shakshuka. d) Josh makes falafel, his opportunity cost is 4 kg shakshuka.

> [!success] Official: question cancelled, no right answer among the options

> [!warning] No official answer letter: my own check, not from the key
> The solution file only says the question was cancelled because of a cut-and-paste error. This working is my own, not official.

See [[Lec_02-PPF and Gains from Trade]]. Opportunity cost of falafel is the shakshuka given up:

> [!example] Working
> Josh: in 8 hours he could make 1 kg falafel or $8 / 2 = 4$ kg shakshuka, so 1 kg falafel costs **4 kg shakshuka**.
>
> Henry: in 2 hours he could make 1 kg falafel or $2 / 1 = 2$ kg shakshuka, so 1 kg falafel costs **2 kg shakshuka**.

Henry has the lower opportunity cost of falafel, so Henry should make falafel (and Josh shakshuka, where his cost of 1/4 kg falafel is lower than Henry's 1/2 kg). The true statement is "Henry, opportunity cost 2 kg shakshuka". None of the options says that: a has 4 for Henry, c has 1/2 for Henry, and b and d assign Josh. That is why it was cancelled.

## Q14 — Average fixed cost

**Question.** When company A produces 20 units the average total cost is USD 250 and total variable cost is USD 4,000. What is the average fixed cost when 30 units are produced (round to a whole number)? a) 33, b) 35, c) 43, d) 45.

> [!success] Official answer: a) 33

See [[Lec_05-Supply and Costs of Production]] and [[Lec_14-Production Costs Worksheet]]. Fixed cost does not change with output, so find it at 20 units and reuse it.

> [!example] Working
> Total cost at 20 units: $250 \times 20 = 5{,}000$.
>
> Fixed cost: $5{,}000 - 4{,}000 = 1{,}000$.
>
> [[Average Fixed Cost]] at 30 units: $1{,}000 / 30 = 33.3 \approx 33$.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> Option d (45) is not obtainable. Option b (35) and c (43) would come from using the wrong fixed cost. The key idea: total fixed cost is constant, so only the divisor changes.

## Q15 — Why the US government does not provide health care

**Question.** In Israel every citizen pays a health tax that gives much cheaper health care than the private market (as in the US). Which statement explains why health care is not provided by the government in the US? a) excludable and rival, b) excludable and non-rival, c) non-excludable and non-rival, d) non-excludable and rival.

> [!success] Official answer: a) health care is excludable and rival

See [[Lec_13-Public Goods and International Trade]]. The official explanation, in my words: health care is a **private good**.

- **Excludable:** a provider can deny access.
- **Rival:** one doctor's hour used by one patient cannot be used by another.

| | Rival | Not rival |
|---|---|---|
| **Excludable** | Private good (a) | Club good (b) |
| **Not excludable** | Common resource (d) | Public good (c) |

Israel pays for health care because of a policy view that every citizen should have access, not because health care is a public good. In the US it is neither a public good nor something the government has chosen to give to everyone. A public good (c) is the kind the market is unlikely to supply because of the **free rider** problem, which health care does not have.

## Q16 — Under-investment in vaccines

**Question.** The passage says the social costs of the pandemic in the US are estimated at 16 trillion dollars and the vaccines give a large global benefit (17.4 trillion dollars, over USD 5,800 per course), but manufacturers capture only a modest portion: the three vaccine makers' US revenue totalled only USD 14 billion by the end of 2021. Private firms are likely to under-invest in development and capacity. Which statement is correct? a. social benefit exceeds private benefit, so the government should award a subsidy to reach the efficient production point. b. social benefit is lower than private benefit, so the government should levy a Pigouvian tax. c. social and private benefit are the same, so the government should stay out. d. all wrong.

> [!success] Official answer: a.

See [[Lec_12-Externalities]]. The firms capture only a small part of the benefit, so the **social benefit exceeds the private benefit**: a **positive externality**. The market quantity is below the efficient quantity. A **subsidy** per unit raises the private benefit to the social one and moves production to the efficient point.

b is wrong because a Pigouvian tax fixes a negative externality (private benefit above social). c contradicts the passage.

## Q17 — Pollution from the fashion industry

**Question.** The fashion industry causes 10% of humanity's carbon emissions, which could rise to 26% by 2050. Which policies would reduce the pollution from the fashion industry? a. subsidising special vouchers to buy clothes at second-hand stores, b. an import tariff on all imported fashion items, c. a higher sales tax on clothes, d. all of the answers are correct.

> [!success] Official answer: d. all the answers are correct

See [[Lec_12-Externalities]], [[Lec_11-Taxes]] and [[Lec_13-Public Goods and International Trade]]. All three policies cut the quantity of **new** clothing produced and bought, which cuts the emissions that come with producing it:

- **a. Second-hand vouchers:** subsidise a substitute for new clothes, so demand for new clothes shifts left.
- **b. Import tariff:** raises the price of imported clothes, so the quantity bought falls.
- **c. Higher sales tax:** raises the price buyers pay, so the quantity bought falls.

> [!tip] Intuition
> The question asks which policies "incentivize reducing pollution", not which is the most efficient. Every option pushes the quantity of new clothing down, so every option qualifies.

## Q18 — Burger restaurant: raising the price

**Question.** Christopher wants to raise revenue by raising the price. The price elasticity of demand is 1.5 in absolute value. Which is correct? a) The plan will not be successful because the decrease in quantity will be greater than the increase in price. b) The plan will be successful because the decrease in quantity will be less than the increase in price. c) The plan will not be successful because the decrease in quantity will be less than the increase in price. d) Not enough information.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_08-Elasticity]]. $|\varepsilon| = 1.5 > 1$, so demand is **elastic**: the percentage fall in quantity is **larger** than the percentage rise in price.

> [!example] Working
> $\%\Delta Q = -1.5 \times \%\Delta P$. For a 10% price rise, quantity falls about 15%. Revenue is $P \times Q$, which falls (about $1.10 \times 0.85 = 0.935$ of the old revenue).

For elastic demand, revenue moves **opposite** to price, so raising the price loses revenue. b has the right idea but for inelastic demand. c is self-contradictory (a smaller quantity fall than the price rise would be a success). d is wrong because the elasticity alone is enough.

## Q19 — The tree company (Haba)

**Question.** Haba sells large trees to gardeners at USD 5 per tree. The table gives total cost by quantity:

| Quantity | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost | 9 | 10 | 12 | 15 | 19 | 24 | 30 | 45 |

Which statement is correct?

- a) The optimal level is 5 trees. This would not be a long-run equilibrium because average total cost is lower than marginal cost and the firm earns positive economic profit.
- b) 5 trees, a long-run equilibrium, because ATC is lower than marginal cost and economic profit is zero.
- c) 6 trees, not a long-run equilibrium, because ATC is higher than marginal cost and profit is positive.
- d) 6 trees, a long-run equilibrium, because ATC equals marginal cost and economic profit is zero.

> [!success] Official answer: a)

See [[Lec_06-Firms in Competitive Markets]]. A price-taker produces where price equals [[Marginal Cost]], as long as the next unit's marginal cost does not exceed the price.

> [!example] Working
> Marginal cost (the change in total cost from one more tree): 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 15 for trees 1 to 7.
>
> Price 5: produce up to the fifth tree (MC = 5, not above price). The sixth tree has MC = 6, above 5, so stop. **Optimal q = 5.**
>
> Revenue $5 \times 5 = 25$. Total cost 24. **Profit = 25 - 24 = 1 > 0.**
>
> [[Average Total Cost]] at 5 trees: $24 / 5 = 4.8$, below MC of 5 and below the price of 5.

![[PP05_q19_tree_firm_mc_atc.png|520]]

The figure comes from the cost table. In a long-run equilibrium economic profit is zero (price equals the minimum of ATC), and here profit is positive, so new firms would enter and push the price down: **not** a long-run equilibrium. That is a. b says zero profit, which is false (profit is 1). c and d choose 6 trees, but the sixth tree costs 6 and sells for 5. d also says ATC equals MC at 6: ATC at 6 is $30 / 6 = 5$ while MC is 6.

## Q20 — Car subsidy for immigrants

**Question.** The Israeli government considers a subsidy for buying new cars (first hand) for immigrants. Analyse three markets: (1) cars for immigrants, (2) cars for non-immigrants, (3) cars for all groups together. Which is correct?

- A) Immigrants pay less and buy more. Non-immigrants pay **more** and buy **fewer**. Overall **more** cars are sold.
- B) Immigrants pay less and buy more. Non-immigrants pay **less** and buy **fewer**. Overall more cars are sold.
- C) Immigrants pay less and buy more. Non-immigrants pay the same and buy the same. Overall **fewer** cars are sold.
- D) Immigrants pay **more** and buy **fewer**. Non-immigrants pay more and buy fewer. Overall fewer cars are sold.

> [!success] Official answer: A)

See [[Lec_11-Taxes]] for subsidies and [[Lec_04-Supply and Market Equilibrium]]. Immigrants and non-immigrants buy from the **same** sellers, so the two groups compete for the same cars. The sellers face one supply curve for all new cars, and it slopes upward.

> [!example] Working through the three markets
> **Immigrants:** the subsidy makes buying cheaper, so their demand curve shifts up (right). They buy more, and the price **they pay** (net of the subsidy) falls.
>
> **Total market:** total demand rises, so the equilibrium quantity of new cars rises. This is the answer to (3): more cars sold overall.
>
> **Non-immigrants:** the sellers' price (the market price before subsidy) rises along the upward-sloping supply curve. Non-immigrants have no subsidy, so they pay more per car and buy fewer.

B is wrong because it has a lower price for non-immigrants alongside a smaller quantity, which is a contradiction on a downward-sloping demand curve. C contradicts the rise in total demand. D has the subsidised group paying more.

> [!tip] Intuition
> A subsidy to one group is a demand shock in the shared market. Non-immigrants lose out because they are competing with the subsidised buyers for the same supply.
