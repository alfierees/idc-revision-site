# Lec 03: Perfect Competition and Demand

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Demand Function]], [[Demand Schedule]], [[Demand Curve]], [[Law of Demand]], [[Substitution Effect]], [[Income Effect]], [[Diminishing Marginal Utility]], [[Quantity Demanded]], [[Market Demand]], [[Change in Quantity Demanded]], [[Change in Demand]], [[Normal Good]], [[Inferior Good]], [[Substitutes]], [[Complements]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> The consumer side of a market. The lecture builds a demand curve from a classroom survey about coffee, explains why it slopes down, shows what shifts it, and adds individual curves into a market curve. Source: *Presentation #3 - Demand* (22 slides, 2.12.2024) and the *Answers to Class Activity* version of the same deck (11 slides, which fills in the survey numbers).

> [!warning] Title versus content
> The deck is titled "Demand and Supply" and is about demand only. It does not define [[Perfect Competition]] anywhere. The one-line definition in section 1 is added context, not from the slides (marked below).

## 1. Setting the scene: price takers

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides never state this, but the whole demand analysis assumes a **competitive market**: many buyers and many sellers of an identical good, so no single buyer can move the price. Each buyer just decides how much to buy at the going price. That is why we can ask "would you pay 20 NIS?" and treat the answer as the individual's choice, taking price as given.

## 2. Building a demand function from a survey

The lecture asks how a consumer decides whether to buy a cup of coffee in the university cafeteria. The tool is the **demand function**: it connects the price of a good to the number of units consumers are willing to buy.

**The experiment.** For each price in turn (20, 15, 10, 7, 5 NIS) students answer yes or no: "Are you willing to pay this for a cup?" Counting the yeses gives the quantity demanded at that price.

> [!warning] Assumption: quality is held fixed
> The cup of coffee is the same quality at every price. The lecturer notes that in the past, students said that at a lower price they assumed worse quality. That would break the experiment, because the price would then be changing the product, not just the cost.

### Demand schedule (class result)

| Price (NIS per cup) | Quantity demanded (cups) |
|---|---|
| 20 | 6 |
| 15 | 27 |
| 10 | 55 |
| 7 | 60 |
| 5 | 62 |

A table like this is a [[Demand Schedule]]. Plotting it gives the [[Demand Curve]].

![[Lec03_coffee_demand_curve.png]]

> [!tip] Graph convention: "Ps before Qs"
> Price determines quantity, yet economists draw **price on the Y axis** and **quantity on the X axis**. The hint on the slide: "Ps before Qs". Here $P$ is the price per cup and $q$ is the number of cups demanded.

> [!info] Ceteris paribus
> Many things affect demand. The experiment **holds all of them constant and changes only the price**. This is what makes the curve a statement about price alone.

## 3. Why the demand curve slopes down

Three reasons are given.

1. **[[Substitution Effect]].** When the price of the good goes up, consumers switch to other goods that can substitute for it.
2. **[[Income Effect]].** When the price goes down, the same income buys more units. (The converse also holds: a price rise leaves buyers feeling poorer.)
3. **[[Diminishing Marginal Utility]].** The first unit of a good usually gives a lot of utility, and each extra unit gives less. So we are willing to pay less for additional units.

> [!info] Law of demand
> The [[Law of Demand]]: the quantity demanded of a good **falls when the price of the good rises, other things equal**, and vice versa.

The slide stresses that here the quantity changes *because the good's own price changed*. Keep this in mind for section 5.

## 4. A shift of the whole curve: the bad-news experiment

The survey is repeated after a (hypothetical) study claims that **coffee is bad for you**. Same prices, new counts:

| Price (NIS per cup) | Before the study | After the study |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 6 | 10 |
| 15 | 27 | 17 |
| 10 | 55 | 24 |
| 7 | 60 | 32 |
| 5 | 62 | 38 |

![[Lec03_coffee_demand_shift.png]]

The lecturer's reading (answers slide): the finding that coffee is bad for one's health **shifts the whole demand curve to the left**. At every price level fewer cups are bought, because some consumers who would have bought a cup before the study now choose not to.

> [!warning] Check the first row
> At 20 NIS the "after" count (10) is *higher* than the "before" count (6), so the two plotted lines cross at the top. Every other row falls, which is what the leftward-shift story needs. The answers deck draws the new curve fully to the left, so the 10 may be a slip in the slide. Take the shift to be leftward and do not read anything into the crossing.

> [!tip] Why this is a shift, not a movement
> Price did not change between the two surveys. A non-price factor (information about health, which changes **tastes**) changed, so the whole curve moves. This is the idea formalised in section 7.

## 5. Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| [[Quantity Demanded]] | The amount of a good or service buyers are ready, willing and able to purchase at a given price |
| [[Law of Demand]] | Quantity demanded falls when the price rises, other things equal |
| [[Demand Curve]] | A downward-sloping line showing quantity demanded for each given price |

> [!note]
> "Ready, willing **and able**": wanting a good is not enough, the buyer must also be able to pay for it.

## 6. Market demand versus individual demand

[[Market Demand]] is the **horizontal addition** of individual demands: at each price, sum the quantities demanded by all buyers.

**Classroom exercise (laptops).** Split the class into two groups, treated as two separate markets, and assume nobody owns a laptop. Group 1 counts how many would pay 4500 NIS for a school laptop. Group 2 counts how many would pay 4000 NIS. The market demand of the class is the total laptops bought by all students at a given price. (The slides give the method, not the counts.)

**Worked example: movie tickets, young and old.** Two groups of buyers behave differently, and the market is their sum.

| Price (NIS) | Young | Old | Whole market |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65 | 32 | 45 | 77 |
| 47 | 42 | 50 | 92 |

$$
Q_{total}(P) = Q_{young}(P) + Q_{old}(P)
$$

For example at a price of 47: $42 + 50 = 92$.

![[Lec03_market_demand_sum.png]]

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Add **quantities at each price** (horizontally). Do not add the prices at a given quantity. Note also that the old group's curve is steeper: its quantity responds less to the price drop (45 to 50 versus 32 to 42).

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slide shows each curve as a straight line with two marked points; the figure here draws only those two points per group, using the slide's values.

## 7. Determinants of demand: movement along versus shift of the curve

| | [[Change in Quantity Demanded]] | [[Change in Demand]] |
|---|---|---|
| What moves | A point **along** one demand curve | The **whole curve** shifts to a new position |
| Cause | A change **only in the good's own price** | A change in one or more **non-price determinants** |
| At a given price | Same curve, new price, new quantity | The quantity demanded at any given price changes |

![[Lec03_movement_vs_shift.png]]

### The non-price determinants

Four are listed: **income**, **prices of related goods**, **tastes**, and **expectations**.

| Determinant | Type | Effect on demand |
|---|---|---|
| Income | [[Normal Good]] | Income up, demand up. Income down, demand down |
| Income | [[Inferior Good]] | Income up, demand **down**. Income down, demand up |
| Price of a related good | [[Substitutes]] | Price of A up, demand for B **up** |
| Price of a related good | [[Complements]] | Price of A up, demand for B **down** |
| Tastes | | Preferences change (the coffee health study) |
| Expectations | | Beliefs about the future |

In the slide's notation: for normal goods $I \uparrow \rightarrow D \uparrow$; for inferior goods $I \uparrow \rightarrow D \downarrow$; for substitutes $P_A \uparrow \rightarrow D_B \uparrow$; for complements $P_A \uparrow \rightarrow D_B \downarrow$.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides list tastes and expectations without examples. Short illustrations: a health study that lowers the taste for coffee (section 4); expecting a higher price next month pushes buyers to purchase more now. Substitutes are goods used in place of each other (tea for coffee); complements are used together (coffee and sugar).

> [!tip] Exam habit
> Ask first: did the **good's own price** change? If yes, move along the curve. If something else changed, shift the curve. Own price never shifts the demand curve.

## Summary

- The demand function links the price of a good to the quantity buyers will purchase. A demand curve plots it with **P on the Y axis, Q on the X axis**.
- It slopes down by the **substitution effect**, the **income effect** and **diminishing marginal utility** ([[Law of Demand]]).
- A price change causes a **change in quantity demanded** (movement along the curve). A change in income, related prices, tastes or expectations causes a **change in demand** (shift).
- Normal goods: demand rises with income. Inferior goods: demand falls with income. Substitutes: demand rises when the other's price rises. Complements: it falls.
- [[Market Demand]] is the horizontal sum of individual demands, for example 32 + 45 = 77 tickets at a price of 65 NIS.
