# Lec 13: Public Goods and International Trade

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Excludability]], [[Rivalry]], [[Public Goods]], [[Common Resources]], [[Club Goods]], [[Free-Rider Problem]], [[Tragedy of the Commons]], [[World Price]], [[Exporting Country]], [[Importing Country]], [[Tariff]], [[Import Quota]], [[Deadweight Loss]]

> [!info] What this lecture covers
> Two topics close the course. **Part 1** (public goods) asks which goods markets can supply well. It classifies goods by two questions, "can I be stopped from using it?" and "does my use leave less for you?", and shows that the two awkward cells (**public goods** and **common resources**) are the ones where government can help. **Part 2** (international trade) is a Review application of surplus analysis: it uses the consumer and producer surplus tools from earlier lectures to show who wins and who loses when a country opens to trade, and then what a **tariff** and an **import quota** do. Slide content is partly adapted from Mankiw, *Principles of Economics*.

# Part 1: Public goods and common resources

## 1. Different kinds of goods

Every good can be sorted using two properties.

> [!info] The two properties
> - **[[Excludability]]**: someone can prevent me from using the good (typically because I have not paid). A good is **excludable** if people can be kept from using it, **not excludable** if no one can stop me.
> - **[[Rivalry]]**: if I am using the good, someone else cannot use it. A good is **rival** if one person's use reduces what is left for others, **not rival** if others can use it at the same time.

Crossing the two properties gives four types of good:

| | **Rival** (my use leaves less for you) | **Not rival** (we can both use it) |
|---|---|---|
| **Excludable** (I can be stopped) | **[[Private Goods]]**: pizza, cars, massages | **[[Club Goods]]**: natural monopolies (cable TV) |
| **Not excludable** (no one can stop me) | **[[Common Resources]]**: clean air, fish in the ocean | **[[Public Goods]]**: national defence, a tsunami siren |

> [!tip] A quick way to sort a good
> Ask two questions in order. (1) If I do not pay, can I be kept out? No means *not excludable*. (2) If I use it, is there less for the next person? Yes means *rival*. A pizza is a private good (you can be stopped from taking it, and once eaten it is gone). A tsunami siren is a public good (you cannot be kept from hearing it, and your hearing it does not stop your neighbour).

> [!note] Cells the lecture only labels
> Private goods are the markets of the earlier lectures, where price and quantity are set by supply and demand. Club goods get one line on the slides (natural monopolies such as cable TV: excludable through subscription, but not rival). The lecture's attention is on the two **not excludable** rows, summed up on the slide "Two issues that government can help with": **free-rider issue** for public goods, **tragedy of the commons** for common resources.

## 2. Public goods

A **public good** is **not excludable and not rival**.

The slides list three linked problems.

**(a) Non-excludability and the free-rider problem.**

> [!info] Definition
> The **[[Free-Rider Problem]]** arises when a person receives the benefit of a good but avoids paying for it.

If people cannot be excluded, everyone has the incentive to let someone else pay and enjoy the good anyway. Each person's payment is small relative to the whole, so each one waits for the others. Nobody pays, and the good is not provided.

**(b) A positive externality.** People cannot be excluded and can benefit from consuming the good. The benefit spills over to everyone, much like the positive externalities in [[Lec_12-Externalities]] but in the extreme: the whole benefit is external to whoever might pay for it.

**(c) A market quantity problem with a marginal cost of zero.**

- The marginal cost of one more person consuming the good is **zero** (the siren sounds just as loudly for one more listener).
- So who pays? The good is **not produced under market conditions without intervention**, even if buyers collectively value it more than the cost of providing it.
- Decision rule from the slides: **if the benefit of a public good exceeds the cost of providing it, the government should provide the good and pay for it with a tax.**
- **Issue**: measuring the benefit is usually difficult. A private firm sees sales; for a public good there is no price signal that reveals how much people value it.

> [!example] Illustration: a tsunami siren
> Suppose a coastal town values a siren more than it costs to build and run. No single resident wants to pay the full cost, and because no one can be kept from hearing the siren, a resident who refuses to pay still benefits. A private seller cannot charge for it. The tax solution makes everyone contribute and delivers the siren. The hard part, as the slide says, is knowing whether the benefit really exceeds the cost.

## 3. Common resources

A **common resource** is **not excludable but rival**.

- **Non-excludability** brings the same free-riding issue as above: anyone can use it without paying.
- **Rivalry** adds a **negative consumption externality**: each person's use reduces others' ability to use it.

> [!info] Definition
> The **[[Tragedy of the Commons]]** occurs when private incentives (for example, catching and selling fish) outweigh social incentives (keeping the fish population large enough to reproduce). People neglect the external cost of their own use, and as a result everyone is worse off.

The slides link a video on the tragedy of the commons (a YouTube link); it is a class illustration and is not reproduced here.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Compare a siren and an ocean. The siren is not excludable and not rival, so the problem is that **too little** gets provided. The ocean is not excludable but rival, so the problem is that **too much** gets used up: every boat catches fish for its own profit and ignores the fact that each fish taken reduces the stock for everyone else (and for future breeding). The policy goal is therefore the opposite: government should **ensure common resources are not overused**.

## 4. Public goods vs common resources

| | [[Public Goods]] | [[Common Resources]] |
|---|---|---|
| Excludable? | No | No |
| Rival? | No | **Yes** |
| Examples | National defence, tsunami siren | Clean air, fish in the ocean |
| Externality | Positive (everyone benefits) | Negative consumption externality (use reduces others' use) |
| Market problem | [[Free-Rider Problem]]: **under-provided** | [[Tragedy of the Commons]]: **over-used** |
| Government role | Provide it and fund it with a tax if benefit exceeds cost | Ensure it is not overused |
| Hard part | Measuring the benefit | (not stated on slides) |

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Free-riding appears in **both** non-excludable cells (the slides list it under both public goods and common resources). The two cells differ in **rivalry**, and that is what determines whether the outcome is under-provision or over-use. Do not mix up "not rival" (public goods) with "rival" (common resources).

# Part 2: Application, international trade

> [!info] Setting
> The slides use a single example throughout: the market for **steel**. Start with a country that does not trade, then open it to trade and ask who gains and loses. This uses [[Consumer Surplus]] and [[Producer Surplus]] from [[Lec_09-Welfare Consumer and Producer Surplus]] and the deadweight loss logic from [[Lec_11-Taxes]].

## 5. Equilibrium without international trade

With no trade, the domestic market for steel clears where **domestic supply** meets **domestic demand**, at the **equilibrium price** and **equilibrium quantity**. **Consumer surplus** is the area under the demand curve and above the price. **Producer surplus** is the area above the supply curve and below the price. These are the surpluses before trade.

## 6. Trade decides on the world price

A country that opens to trade faces the **[[World Price]]**, the price of steel prevailing in the world market. The country is assumed to be a **price taker**: it can buy or sell as much as it wants at the world price (drawn as a horizontal line). Compare it with the price that would prevail without trade:

| If the world price is... | Then the country becomes... | And it... |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher** than the no-trade price | an **[[Exporting Country]]** | sells steel abroad |
| **Lower** than the no-trade price | an **[[Importing Country]]** | buys steel from abroad |

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides state the figures and their labels but do not spell out the "price taker" assumption or the exporter/importer rule in words. The rule above is read off Figures 2 and 4: in the exporting country the world price line sits above the no-trade price, in the importing country it sits below. The two figures below use illustrative numbers I chose (demand $P = 10 - Q$, supply $P = 2 + Q$, so the no-trade price is 6); the slides use no numbers.

## 7. Free trade in an exporting country

At the higher world price, domestic producers want to supply **more** than domestic consumers demand. The gap is **exports**: **quantity supplied minus quantity demanded** at the world price. Domestic consumers buy less than before, because the price rose.

![[Lec13_exporter_welfare.png]]

Area labels (as on the slide): **A** is the triangle above the world price under the demand curve, **B** is the strip between the two prices under the demand curve, **C** is the area above supply and below the no-trade price, and **D** is the triangle between the curves, below the world price, with exports as its base.

| | Before trade | After trade | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B | A | $-B$ |
| Producer surplus | C | B + C + D | $+(B + D)$ |
| **Total surplus** | A + B + C | A + B + C + D | **$+D$** |

> [!info] Reading the table
> The exporting country's **producers gain** and its **consumers lose** (the price rose). The gain to producers (B + D) exceeds the loss to consumers (B), so **total surplus rises by D**, the **gains from trade**. B is simply a transfer from consumers to producers; D is a net gain.

## 8. Free trade in an importing country

At the lower world price, domestic consumers want to buy **more** than domestic producers supply. The gap is **imports**: **quantity demanded minus quantity supplied** at the world price. Domestic producers sell less than before, because the price fell.

![[Lec13_importer_welfare.png]]

Here **A** is the triangle above the no-trade price under demand, **B** is the strip between the two prices (between demand and supply on the left), **C** is the area above supply and below the world price, and **D** is the triangle between the curves, with imports as its base.

| | Before trade | After trade | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A | A + B + D | $+(B + D)$ |
| Producer surplus | B + C | C | $-B$ |
| **Total surplus** | A + B + C | A + B + C + D | **$+D$** |

> [!info] Reading the table
> In the importing country the signs flip: **consumers gain** (a lower price), **producers lose**, and again the winners' gain (B + D) exceeds the losers' loss (B), so **total surplus rises by D**.

> [!tip] The punchline of both cases
> Free trade makes the pie bigger (area **D**) in both exporting and importing countries. The winners could in principle compensate the losers and still be better off, but trade does **not** make everyone better off by itself: the losers (domestic producers in an importing country, domestic consumers in an exporting country) are the ones who lobby for trade barriers.

## 9. Comparison: exporter vs importer

| | [[Exporting Country]] | [[Importing Country]] |
|---|---|---|
| World price vs no-trade price | Higher | Lower |
| Trade volume | Exports = $Q^S - Q^D$ | Imports = $Q^D - Q^S$ |
| Domestic consumers | Lose (surplus falls by B) | Gain (surplus rises by B + D) |
| Domestic producers | Gain (surplus rises by B + D) | Lose (surplus falls by B) |
| Total surplus | Rises by D | Rises by D |

## 10. The effects of a tariff

> [!info] Definition
> A **[[Tariff]]** is a tax on goods produced abroad and sold domestically (here, on imported steel).

Start from the importing country in free trade at the world price. The tariff **raises the domestic price** of steel above the world price by the amount of the tariff, and the effects are:

- Domestic **quantity supplied rises** (producers respond to the higher price): from $Q^S_1$ to $Q^S_2$.
- Domestic **quantity demanded falls**: from $Q^D_1$ to $Q^D_2$.
- **Imports fall**: from $Q^D_1 - Q^S_1$ (imports without tariff) to $Q^D_2 - Q^S_2$ (imports with tariff). The tariff moves the market **back towards** the no-trade equilibrium.

![[Lec13_tariff_effects.png]]

The figure uses illustrative numbers (world price 4, tariff 1, so the price with tariff is 5). The slides' areas are labelled **A** to **G**:

- **A + B**: consumer surplus with the tariff.
- **C**: the strip between the two prices to the left of the supply curve (extra producer surplus). **G**: producer surplus that remains without the tariff.
- **D** and **F**: the two small triangles at either end of the imports rectangle.
- **E**: the rectangle with the tariff and imports with the tariff as sides, which is **tariff revenue** (tariff per unit multiplied by the quantity imported).

| | Before tariff | After tariff | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B + C + D + E + F | A + B | $-(C + D + E + F)$ |
| Producer surplus | G | C + G | $+C$ |
| Government revenue | None | E | $+E$ |
| **Total surplus** | A + B + C + D + E + F + G | A + B + C + E + G | **$-(D + F)$** |

> [!info] Deadweight loss of the tariff
> The area **D + F** is the fall in total surplus and is the **[[Deadweight Loss]]** of the tariff. The tariff does two things to resources: **D** is lost because the higher price makes domestic producers supply units that foreign producers could make more cheaply (overproduction by domestic firms), and **F** is lost because consumers who would have bought at the world price are priced out (underconsumption). **C** is a transfer from consumers to producers and **E** is a transfer from consumers to the government.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides give the areas and the table but no wording for what D and F represent. The overproduction and underconsumption reading above is the standard explanation of those triangles, added to help you remember them.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Do not say the tariff "raises total surplus because the government gets E." The government revenue E is already counted in the "after" total, and the net result is still a **loss of D + F**.

## 11. The effects of an import quota

> [!info] Definition
> An **[[Import Quota]]** is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be produced abroad and sold domestically.

In the slides' example the quota restricts imports to the same level as the tariff. The slides picture **domestic supply + import supply**: the market supply curve is domestic supply plus the quota quantity, which cuts the demand curve at the equilibrium with the quota. The domestic price (the price "with quota") rises above the world price, just as with a tariff.

![[Lec13_quota_effects.png]]

The same illustrative numbers are used as for the tariff (quota of two units, domestic price 5). The areas **A, B, C, D, F, G** have the same meaning as for the tariff; the rectangle that was **E** (government revenue under a tariff) is now **E' + E''** (written E' and E'' on the slide). This is the **license-holder surplus**: whoever holds the licences to import at the world price can sell in the domestic market at the higher price and keeps the gap.

| | Before quota | After quota | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B + C + D + E' + E'' + F | A + B | $-(C + D + E' + E'' + F)$ |
| Producer surplus | G | C + G | $+C$ |
| License-holder surplus | None | E' + E'' | $+(E' + E'')$ |
| **Total surplus** | A + B + C + D + E' + E'' + F + G | A + B + C + E' + E'' + G | **$-(D + F)$** |

> [!info] Deadweight loss of the quota
> **D + F** is again the fall in total surplus: the **deadweight loss of the quota**.

## 12. Tariff vs quota

| | [[Tariff]] | [[Import Quota]] |
|---|---|---|
| What is restricted | Imports taxed (price rises) | Imports capped by quantity |
| Effect on domestic price | Up | Up |
| Effect on imports | Down | Down |
| Producers | Gain C | Gain C |
| Consumers | Lose C + D + E + F | Lose C + D + E' + E'' + F |
| Who gets the rectangle (E or E' + E'') | **Government** (tariff revenue) | **License holders** |
| Deadweight loss | D + F | D + F |

> [!tip] Why this comparison matters
> For the same level of imports a tariff and a quota look identical to consumers and producers and have the **same deadweight loss**. The difference is who collects the rectangle: with a tariff the government collects it, with a quota the holders of the import licences do (the license-holder surplus). With a tariff the revenue can be spent on something useful, but under a quota the same money goes to private parties.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides present the tariff and the quota separately and never put them side by side. The comparison table and the "same imports" assumption in the figure are my construction from the two surplus tables. If an exam question gives a quota and a tariff that limit imports to different levels, the areas will differ, so recompute rather than reuse this table.

## Summary

- Goods are classified by **excludability** and **rivalry**: private (both), club (excludable, not rival), common resources (rival, not excludable), public (neither).
- **Public goods** suffer the **free-rider problem**: no one can be excluded, marginal cost of another user is zero, the market under-provides them. If benefits exceed cost, the government should provide them and tax to pay. Measuring the benefit is the difficulty.
- **Common resources** suffer the **tragedy of the commons**: rival plus non-excludable, so private use ignores the external cost and the resource is over-used. Government should prevent overuse.
- **Free trade** raises total surplus (by area D) in both an exporting country (price rises, producers gain, consumers lose) and an importing country (price falls, consumers gain, producers lose).
- A **tariff** raises the domestic price, shrinks imports, helps producers, raises government revenue and **costs consumers more than it gives to others**: deadweight loss **D + F**.
- An **import quota** has the same effects on price, quantity and deadweight loss (**D + F**), but the rectangle goes to **license holders**, not the government.
