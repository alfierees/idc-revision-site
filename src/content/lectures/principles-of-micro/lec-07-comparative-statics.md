# Lec 07: Comparative Statics

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Comparative Statics]], [[Shift of the Supply Curve]], [[Shift of the Demand Curve]], [[Movement Along a Curve]], [[Market Equilibrium]], [[Shortage]], [[Surplus]], [[Substitute Goods]], [[Simultaneous Shifts]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> The method of **[[Comparative Statics]]**: start from a market in equilibrium, change one outside factor, shift the right curve, and compare the old and the new equilibrium. The lecture (Presentation #8, a class exercise with solutions) works through two real markets, **vanilla** and **cucumbers**. A short follow-up deck (Presentation #8.1) covers the special case where **both curves move at once**, using apples, where the price change is certain but the quantity change is **ambiguous**.

## 1. The method: four steps

Comparative statics compares two equilibria. It does not describe the journey between them. Every question in this lecture is solved with the same recipe.

1. **Draw the starting equilibrium**: demand $D_0$, supply $S_0$, price $P_0$, quantity $Q_0$.
2. **Identify the event** and ask which curve it affects, supply (the cost or ability of firms to produce) or demand (what buyers want at each price).
3. **Shift that curve** in the right direction. Shift one curve at a time.
4. **Read off the new equilibrium** and say what happened to price and quantity. At the old price there is now a [[Shortage]] or a [[Surplus]], and market forces push the price to the new equilibrium.

> [!warning] Shift versus movement
> Only a change in something **other than the good's own price** shifts a curve. The change in price that follows is a [[Movement Along a Curve]] of the curve that did **not** shift. The class exercise asks students to use an arrow for a shift of a whole curve and a different mark for a movement along a curve.

| | Shift of a whole curve | Movement along a curve |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | A change in a non-price factor (input costs, tastes, price of a substitute, ...) | A change in the good's own price |
| Picture | The curve moves left or right (or up or down) | Slide along the same curve to a new point |
| Example here | Ban on foreign workers moves the cucumber supply curve | Cucumber demand falls as the price rises, along $D_0$ |

> [!tip] Reading a supply shift
> A supply shift to the **left** is the same as a shift **up**: for every quantity, the cost of producing it is higher. A shift to the **right** is a shift **down**. This is the wording the slides use ("shifts to the left (up)").

## 2. Class exercise 1: the market for vanilla

### The data

The class is given two charts to read, and a news article on the history of vanilla prices. The first shows the price in euros per kg from 1990 to 2017. The second compares the vanilla price with the silver price (USD per kg) over 2015 to 2018.

![[Lec07_data_vanilla_price_history.png]]

![[Lec07_data_vanilla_vs_silver.png]]

> [!note] Reading the charts
> The slides give no figures beyond the charts. Visually, the price is low and flat in the 1990s, has a spike in the early 2000s, falls back, and then rises steeply from about 2013 to 2017. On the second chart the vanilla price climbs through 2015 to 2017 until it is close to the silver price. These are read off the pictures, not quoted data.

### Supply: what happened to the supply of vanilla?

Vanilla is native to Mexico, but deforestation there greatly reduced its natural habitat. In Madagascar, where over 80% of the world's vanilla is produced, the flowers must be pollinated by hand and the pods need several months to cure after harvest. The process is slow and labour-intensive, so supply cannot expand easily.

This is a **fall in supply**: the supply curve shifts **left (up)** from $S_0$ to $S_1$. The slide first analyses supply on its own ("for simplicity") and adds demand later.

### Demand: two shifts

| Period | What happened | Why | Shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980s | Consumers moved to **artificial** vanilla flavouring | It is a [[Substitute Goods]] for real vanilla | Demand falls: $D_0 \to D_1$ (left) |
| Since 2011 | Big companies joined the "all natural" trend and pledged to drop artificial flavourings | Demand for real vanilla rises | Demand rises: $D_1 \to D_2$ (right) |

### Putting it together: 1980 to 2000

Supply shifts left and demand shifts left. Taking them one at a time:

- Supply alone ($S_0 \to S_1$) would push the price **up** and quantity **down**.
- Demand alone ($D_0 \to D_1$) would push the price **down** (to $P_1$) and quantity **down**.

![[Lec07_vanilla_1980_2000.png]]

Quantity falls under both shifts, so it clearly falls ($Q_0 \to Q_2$). The price effect is a tug of war between the two shifts. On the slide's diagram the new price $P_2$ lies between $P_1$ (demand effect only) and $P_0$ (the start), so the price ends below $P_0$, but that depends on how far each curve moves (see section 5).

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slide shows the two shifted curves and the labels $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_2$, $Q_0$, $Q_1$, $Q_2$ but gives little commentary. The reading above (which effect pushes which way, and that the final price depends on the size of the shifts) is my reconstruction.

### Putting it together: 2011 to 2017

Starting from $S_1$ and $D_1$ (equilibrium $P_2$, $Q_2$), demand now shifts **right** to $D_2$. The price rises to $P_3$ and the quantity rises to $Q_3$. On the slide, $P_3$ is above $P_0$ and $Q_3$ is above $Q_0$, which matches the huge rise in the price chart.

![[Lec07_vanilla_2011_2017.png]]

> [!tip] Why the price rose so much
> Supply was already shifted left and is hard to expand (a slow, labour-intensive crop). Demand then shifts right against this tight supply, so the price rises sharply, matching the price chart.

## 3. Class exercise 2: the market for cucumbers

The exercise starts from a Haaretz newspaper article, "Farmers Blame Gov't Policy as Cucumbers Double in Price". The article itself is not reproduced in the deck, so the reasoning below follows the slides' own answers only.

**Step 1: starting point.** Draw demand and supply and mark the equilibrium price and quantity. The slide labels the starting price $P_0$ = 3.99 and quantity $q_0$. No other numbers are needed.

### Shift 1: ban on foreign workers (a supply shift)

The farmers claim that the government ban on foreign workers caused the price rise.

> [!example] Slide answer, in steps
> 1. Foreign workers come to work in Israel because the wage in Israel is high relative to their country of origin, but they are still much cheaper than native-born workers.
> 2. Banning foreign workers reduces the supply of workers, so the wage farmers must pay **rises**.
> 3. For every quantity of cucumbers, the cost of production rises, so the **supply curve shifts to the left (up)**.
> 4. At the original price $P_0$ there is a **shortage**, so the price rises from $P_0$ to $P_1$.
> 5. The quantity produced falls from $q_0$ to $q_1$.

![[Lec07_cucumber_supply_shift.png]]

The price rise from $P_0$ to $P_1$ and the fall in quantity is a **movement along the demand curve** $D_0$. Demand itself did not shift.

### Shift 2: allowing imports from Jordan (a demand shift)

The second question asks what happens if the government **does** allow imports from Jordan. The slide assumes that Jordanian cucumbers differ from Israeli ones in taste and size, so they are a [[Substitute Goods]], and that they are **cheaper**. The slide adds a footnote: this assumption is added by the lecturer even though it is not in the article, because the special shape of the supply curve with imports has not been taught yet.

> [!example] Slide answer, in steps
> 1. A cheaper substitute is available, so Israeli consumers buy **fewer** Israeli cucumbers at every price: the **demand curve shifts to the left**.
> 2. At the old price $P_1$ there is a **surplus**.
> 3. The surplus forces suppliers to **cut the price** from $P_1$ to $P_2$.
> 4. Equilibrium quantity falls from $q_1$ to $q_2$ (the slide marks the surplus at $P_1$).

![[Lec07_cucumber_demand_shift.png]]

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slide's picture starts the import question from the already-shifted supply curve $S_1$ at price $P_1$ (the labels are $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_2$ and $q_0$, $q_1$, $q_2$). My diagram above shows the demand shift starting from the original equilibrium for clarity, so its labels are simpler. The direction of every change is the same.

## 4. Summary table: one curve moves

Use this as a lookup. "Up" and "down" are for price and quantity at the new equilibrium compared with the old one.

| Event | Curve | Shift | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supply falls (input cost up, ban on workers, deforestation) | Supply | Left (up) | Up | Down |
| Supply rises | Supply | Right (down) | Down | Up |
| Demand falls (cheaper substitute, change in taste) | Demand | Left | Down | Down |
| Demand rises (health study, "all natural" trend) | Demand | Right | Up | Up |

## 5. Special case: both curves shift at once

> [!info] Source
> This section comes from Presentation #8.1, "Both Curve Move Example".

When **both** curves shift in the same event, one of the two effects (price or quantity) is **certain** and the other is **ambiguous**. The lecture example is the market for apples, with two simultaneous events:

- A **drought** reduces the quantity supplied at every price: supply shifts left.
- A new study proves "an apple a day keeps the doctor away": the quantity demanded rises at every price: demand shifts right.

The method: analyse each shift separately, then combine them.

### Supply alone (drought)

1. The supply curve shifts left (at each price the quantity supplied is lower).
2. At the original price there is a shortage.
3. Market forces lead to a new equilibrium with a **higher price** ($P_1^*$) and a **lower quantity**.

### Demand alone (health study)

1. The demand curve shifts right.
2. At the earlier price $P_1^*$ there is a shortage.
3. The new equilibrium has a higher price $P_2^*$, above both $P_0^*$ and $P_1^*$.
4. For quantity, the slides then show that the answer depends on the sizes of the shifts.

### Combined result

![[Lec07_apples_ambiguity.png]]

| | Example 1 (supply moves more) | Example 2 (demand moves more) |
|---|---|---|
| Price versus $P_0^*$ | Increases | Increases |
| Quantity versus $Q_0^*$ | **Decreases** | **Increases** |

> [!tip] What we know for sure
> The market price rose. The quantity could increase, stay the same or decrease, depending on **how far each curve moves**. So the quantity change is ambiguous. In general, if supply and demand shift in **opposite** directions in this way (supply left, demand right), price is certain and quantity is ambiguous. The lecture slides only treat this one pairing.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides draw the two examples and state the conclusion for apples. The general rule for other pairings (for example both curves shifting the same way gives a certain quantity but an ambiguous price) is **not on the slides** directly, but it is what the vanilla 1980 to 2000 case shows (both curves left: quantity certain, price ambiguous). I use it here as a reconstruction.

| Both shift | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Supply left, demand right (apples) | Up (certain) | Ambiguous |
| Supply left, demand left (vanilla 1980 to 2000) | Ambiguous | Down (certain) |

## Summary

- **[[Comparative Statics]]** compares equilibria before and after a change in a factor outside the market. Draw the start, decide which curve moves, shift it, compare.
- A **supply shift** comes from production costs and ability to produce (input prices, worker supply, a destroyed habitat). A **demand shift** comes from tastes and the prices of [[Substitute Goods]].
- A shift of a whole curve is different from a [[Movement Along a Curve]]: the latter happens along the curve that did not shift.
- At the old price a shift creates a [[Shortage]] (supply left or demand right) or a [[Surplus]] (supply right or demand left), and price adjusts.
- Vanilla: supply left (deforestation, hard to farm), demand left in the 1980s (artificial flavouring), then demand right from 2011 (all-natural trend), giving a steep price rise.
- Cucumbers: ban on foreign workers is a left supply shift (price up, quantity down). Cheaper imported substitutes are a left demand shift (price down, quantity down).
- When both curves move, one effect is certain and the other is ambiguous. Apples: price up for sure, quantity depends on which shift is bigger.
