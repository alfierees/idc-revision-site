# Lec 09: Welfare: Consumer and Producer Surplus

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Consumer Surplus]], [[Producer Surplus]], [[Total Surplus]], [[Willingness to Pay]], [[Marginal Benefit]], [[Marginal Cost]], [[Efficient Production]], [[Efficient Allocation]], [[Efficient Quantity]], [[Market Efficiency]], [[Gains from Trade]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> Supply and demand tell us the price and quantity. **Welfare economics** asks the next question: *who gains, and how much?* The lecture measures the gain to buyers ([[Consumer Surplus]]) and to sellers ([[Producer Surplus]]) as areas on the supply and demand graph, adds them into [[Total Surplus]], and shows that the free-market equilibrium produces the **largest possible** total surplus. The class activities (consumer surplus at a price, what happens when the price rises, producer surplus and what happens when the price falls) are folded into the sections below. Taxes and price controls (later lectures) are judged against this benchmark.

All money amounts in the shoe example are in USD per pair, and quantities are in thousands of pairs.

## 1. Warm-up: how much would you pay?

The lecture opens with a classroom exercise: **how much are you willing to pay for a Samsung Galaxy A55 5G phone?** Each student writes down a maximum price, and the class builds a demand curve from the answers (the slide holds a blank table: name, willingness to pay, consumer surplus). The point is that every buyer has a personal maximum, their [[Willingness to Pay]] (WTP), and the demand curve is just those maximums lined up from highest to lowest.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The table was filled in live in class, so no numbers are in the deck. The takeaway is: sort the WTP values from high to low, that is the demand curve; the market price then decides who buys and what each buyer's surplus is.

## 2. Consumer surplus

> [!info] Definition
> **Consumer surplus** is the economic surplus a buyer gets from buying something: the benefit minus what they pay. A buyer's marginal benefit is their willingness to pay.

$$
\text{Consumer surplus} = \text{Marginal benefit} - \text{Price}
$$

You gain consumer surplus whenever you buy for a price **lower** than the marginal benefit you get from the good.

> [!example] Jeans
> You are willing to pay 80 for a pair of jeans, but the price is only 50. Your surplus is 80 - 50 = 30.

### 2.1 Total consumer surplus: a triangle

With many buyers, the demand curve is each buyer's marginal benefit, so the total consumer surplus is the **area under the demand curve and above the market price**, out to the quantity bought.

| Part of the triangle | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Height | Where demand hits the price axis, minus the market price: $P(q=0) - P^*$ |
| Base | The quantity demanded at the market price, $q^*$ |
| Area | $\tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ |

$$
CS = \frac{\left(P(q=0) - P^*\right) \times q^*}{2}
$$

> [!example] Textbook example (Stevenson and Wolfers)
> Demand starts at 120 and the price is 50, with 200 units sold. Total consumer surplus is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 200 \times (120 - 50) = 7000$. The same figure marks one buyer's marginal benefit of 80 against the price of 50 (surplus of 30 from that single purchase).

### 2.2 Two facts about the last unit

- The **last unit bought** earns zero consumer surplus: there the marginal benefit equals the price.
- Every unit bought **before** the last has a **positive** surplus, because earlier units are worth more to buyers than the price.

> [!tip] Intuition: water
> We pay very little for water, but our marginal benefit of the first glass is huge (imagine no water at all). The gap between that and the tiny price is a lot of consumer surplus, which is why "we enjoy a lot of consumer surplus in our lives".

### 2.3 Why consumer surplus measures the gains from trade

Supply and demand push the price to the market level, and that price is **always lower than (or equal to, at the last unit) the marginal benefit** of every buyer who chooses to buy. So the surplus exists *because of trade*: it is what buyers gain by being able to trade at all.

### 2.4 Class activity 1: shoe market

Demand for shoes runs from 60 at $Q=0$ down to 0 at $Q=30$, so each extra 5 (thousand) pairs lowers the marginal buyer's WTP by 10. The equilibrium price is 30.

- At $Q = 5$ the marginal buyer is willing to pay 50, so their own surplus is 50 - 30 = **20**.
- Total consumer surplus: height = 60 - 30 = 30, base = 15.

$$
CS = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \times 30 = 225
$$

![[Lec09_consumer_surplus.png]]

### 2.5 Class activity 2: what happens when the price rises?

The price rises from 30 to 40. At 40 the buyers' quantity falls to $Q = 10$ and the height of the triangle is 60 - 40 = 20.

$$
CS = \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 20 = 100
$$

Consumer surplus falls from 225 to 100, a loss of 125, for **two reasons**:

1. **Remaining buyers pay a higher price** on units they still buy (the orange rectangle).
2. **Some buyers leave the market** altogether and lose all their surplus (the red triangle).

![[Lec09_cs_price_rise.png]]

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The solution slide gives only the new surplus (100) and the two reasons. The split of the 125 loss is my calculation from the same curve: the 10 remaining buyers each pay 10 more (10 x 10 = 100, rectangle), and the buyers between $Q = 10$ and $Q = 15$ lose $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 10 = 25$ (triangle). 100 + 25 = 125.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> A higher price does not only hurt through lost sales. Even the people who keep buying are worse off. Always think of **both** effects when the price moves.

## 3. Producer surplus

> [!info] Definition
> **Producer surplus** is the economic surplus a seller gets from selling something: the price received minus the marginal cost of producing that unit.

$$
\text{Producer surplus} = \text{Price} - \text{Marginal cost}
$$

> [!example] Jeans
> The price of a pair of Levi's jeans is 50 but the marginal cost to produce it is 35. The producer's surplus is 50 - 35 = 15.

Producer surplus is the gain from selling at a higher price than the seller needs in order to be willing to supply: that minimum is the marginal cost.

> [!tip] Reminder
> The **supply curve is the sellers' marginal cost curve**. Reading it upward, each seller (or unit) appears at its own marginal cost.

### 3.1 Total producer surplus: a triangle

The mirror image of consumer surplus: the area **above the supply curve and below the market price**, out to the quantity sold.

| Part of the triangle | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Height | The market price minus where supply hits the price axis: $P^* - P(q=0)$ |
| Base | The quantity supplied at the market price, $q^*$ |
| Area | $\tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ |

$$
PS = \frac{\left(P^* - mc\right) \times q^*}{2}
$$

As with consumer surplus, the **last unit sold earns zero** producer surplus (price equals marginal cost there) and every earlier unit earns a positive surplus. The assumption behind this: sellers keep selling until price equals marginal cost.

### 3.2 Class activity 3: shoe supply at P = 40

The supply curve starts at 15 at $Q = 0$ and rises by 1 for every extra thousand pairs, so at $P = 40$ sellers supply $Q = 25$.

- Marginal seller at $Q = 15$: cost 30, price 40, producer surplus **10**.
- Total producer surplus: height = 40 - 15 = 25, base = 25.

$$
PS = \frac{1}{2} \times 25 \times 25 = 312.50
$$

![[Lec09_producer_surplus.png]]

### 3.3 Class activity 4: what happens when the price falls?

The price falls from 40 to 30 (the equilibrium price): quantity supplied drops to $Q = 15$ and the height is 30 - 15 = 15.

$$
PS = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \times 15 = 112.50
$$

Producer surplus falls from 312.50 to 112.50, a loss of 200, for **two reasons** (mirror of the consumer case):

1. Sellers who stay in the market receive a **lower price** on every unit (orange rectangle: 15 x 10 = 150).
2. Some sellers **leave the market** and lose all their surplus (triangle: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 10 = 50$).

![[Lec09_ps_price_fall.png]]

### 3.4 Comparing the two

| | Consumer surplus | Producer surplus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | Buyers | Sellers |
| Formula per unit | Marginal benefit - price | Price - marginal cost |
| Curve involved | Demand (marginal benefit) | Supply (marginal cost) |
| Area | Below demand, above price | Above supply, below price |
| Triangle height | $P(q=0) - P^*$ | $P^* - P(q=0)$ |
| Last unit | Zero | Zero |
| Price rises | Falls | Rises |
| Price falls | Rises | Falls |

## 4. Voluntary trade creates surplus for both sides

Voluntary exchange generates surplus for **both** the buyer and the seller. Trade is not a zero-sum game where one side's win is the other's loss. Following the cost-benefit principle:

- You buy only if marginal benefit is at least the price, so buying yields consumer surplus.
- A supplier sells only if the price is at least marginal cost, so selling yields producer surplus.
- So neither side takes part in a trade that makes it worse off.

> [!tip] Markets facilitate cooperation
> We often picture markets as competition, but voluntary exchange makes them also a form of **cooperation**. This does **not** mean the gains are shared equally. But as long as both sides are well informed and follow the cost-benefit principle, nobody makes a trade that leaves them worse off.

## 5. Total surplus (social welfare)

Put both surpluses on one graph.

$$
\text{Total surplus} = CS + PS = \left(MB - P\right) + \left(P - MC\right) = MB - MC
$$

The price cancels: it only **splits** the gain between buyers and sellers. What matters for the size of the pie is the gap between marginal benefit and marginal cost. On the graph, total surplus is the area **between the demand and supply curves, out to the quantity traded** ($q^*$ in equilibrium).

> [!example] Jeans
> A pair of jeans is worth 80 to the buyer and costs Levi's 35 in denim, cotton and labour. The transaction creates 80 - 35 = **45** of surplus, whatever price between 35 and 80 they agree on.

In the shoe market (price 30, quantity 15): $CS = 225$, $PS = 112.50$, so total surplus is 337.50.

## 6. Market efficiency: markets answer three questions

The lecture argues that the free market delivers the **maximum** possible surplus, unlike centrally planned systems. The motivating contrast is the Soviet Union: the slides show bread lines and queues, because most consumer goods (food, clothes, shoes, books) were in short supply when the state set prices and output. Today's centrally planned economies mentioned are Cuba, North Korea and, to an extent, China; most countries and most economists prefer market-based economies because markets yield more efficient outcomes.

Markets determine what is made, how much, who makes what, who gets what, and at what price (and, through income, what you can afford to buy). Efficiency is checked with three questions.

### 6.1 Question 1: who makes what? (efficient production)

> [!info] Efficient production
> Producing a given quantity of output at the **lowest possible cost**, which requires producing each good at the lowest marginal cost.

At the market price (30 in the example): every seller whose marginal cost is **at most 30** produces, and every seller whose cost is above 30 does not. So the **lowest-cost sellers** are the ones who produce.

### 6.2 Question 2: who gets what? (efficient allocation)

> [!info] Efficient allocation
> Allocating goods to create the largest economic surplus, which requires each good to go to the person who gets the **highest marginal benefit** from it.

At the market price: every buyer whose WTP is **at least 30** buys, and every buyer whose WTP is below 30 does not. So the buyers who **value the good most highly** are the ones who consume it. As each buyer pursues their own self-interest, the market gives each tomato to the person with the largest marginal benefit, as measured by willingness to pay.

### 6.3 Question 3: how much is bought and sold? (efficient quantity)

> [!info] The rational rule for markets
> To increase economic surplus, produce more of an item if the marginal benefit of one more is **greater than or equal to** its marginal cost. Keep going until they are equal. The efficient quantity is where

$$
\text{Marginal benefit} = \text{Marginal cost}
$$

which is exactly where demand crosses supply: the [[Market Equilibrium]]. It is as if the economy were directed by an "invisible hand".

Why equilibrium beats any other quantity (shoe example, equilibrium $Q = 15$):

| Quantity | Cost of marginal unit (MC) | Value to consumers of marginal unit (MB) | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $Q = 20$ | 35 | 20 | MC above MB, so total surplus **rises if Q falls**. True for any Q above 15. |
| $Q = 10$ | 25 | 40 | MB above MC, so total surplus **rises if Q rises**. True for any Q below 15. |
| $Q = 15$ | 30 | 30 | MB equals MC: no gain from moving. Total surplus is maximised. |

![[Lec09_efficient_quantity.png]]

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides state the verdict ("can increase surplus by reducing/increasing Q") but not the size of the gain. In the figure the triangles are the surplus recovered: moving from $Q=20$ back to 15 recovers $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 15 = 37.5$, and moving from $Q=10$ up to 15 gains the same 37.5. These are my arithmetic, not slide figures.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> "Efficient" does not mean "fair". The market maximises the **size** of total surplus, but the price decides how it is **split** between buyers and sellers. Efficiency says nothing about equality.

## Summary

- **Consumer surplus** = marginal benefit - price, per buyer. Total CS = $\tfrac{(P(q=0) - P^*) \times q^*}{2}$, the triangle between the demand curve and the price.
- **Producer surplus** = price - marginal cost, per seller. Total PS = $\tfrac{(P^* - mc) \times q^*}{2}$, the triangle between the price and the supply curve.
- The last unit traded earns zero surplus for each side; every earlier unit earns a positive amount.
- **A higher price** lowers CS (remaining buyers pay more, some leave) and raises PS; **a lower price** does the reverse. Always split the change into the "same units, new price" part and the "units lost" part.
- **Total surplus (welfare)** = CS + PS = area between demand and supply out to the quantity traded.
- Voluntary trade creates surplus for **both** sides, though not necessarily equally.
- The free-market equilibrium gives **efficient production** (lowest-cost sellers produce), **efficient allocation** (highest-value buyers consume) and the **efficient quantity** ($MB = MC$), so it maximises total surplus.
- Worked numbers (shoe market, $P^* = 30$, $Q^* = 15$): CS = 225, PS = 112.50 (from the price-30 case), total = 337.50.
