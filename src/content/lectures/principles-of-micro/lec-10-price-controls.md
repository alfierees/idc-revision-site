# Lec 10: Price Controls

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Price Control]], [[Price Ceiling]], [[Price Floor]], [[Binding Constraint]], [[Shortage]], [[Surplus]], [[Rent Control]], [[Minimum Wage]], [[Consumer Surplus]], [[Producer Surplus]], [[Deadweight Loss]], [[Market Equilibrium]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> Presentation #11 (Dr. Hadass). The government overrides the market price with a legal **maximum** (price ceiling) or **minimum** (price floor). The lecture asks when such a rule matters (**binding** vs not), what it does to quantities (shortage or surplus), and then, using the hotel-room class exercise, what it does to **consumer surplus**, **producer surplus** and **total surplus** (the **deadweight loss**). It builds on [[Lec_07-Comparative Statics]] and [[Lec_08-Elasticity]]; surplus areas are the same triangles as in the welfare lecture.

## 1. Opening: the Classroom Parliament

The lecture opens with three proposals the class "votes" on. They are discussion prompts; the slides give no answers, and the rest of the lecture supplies the tools to decide.

| Proposition | Policy | Type |
|---|---|---|
| #1 Rent ceiling | No flat within 5 miles of Tel Aviv may charge more than NIS 3,000 plus NIS 1,000 per additional room per month | [[Price Ceiling]] |
| #2 Minimum wage | Minimum wage rises from NIS 30 to NIS 35 per hour | [[Price Floor]] |
| #3 Tax burden | A NIS 5 tax per pizza: should sellers or buyers pay it? | Tax (next lecture, [[Lec_11-Taxes]]) |

> [!tip] Why start here
> Each proposal sounds kind (help renters, help low-wage workers). The lecture's point is to check the **side effects**: who gets left out, and how much total surplus is destroyed.

## 2. Government policies that alter market outcomes

- **[[Price Control]]s** fix the price by law.
  - **[[Price Ceiling]]**: a legal **maximum** price. Example: [[Rent Control]].
  - **[[Price Floor]]**: a legal **minimum** price. Example: [[Minimum Wage]].
- **Taxes**: the government makes buyers or sellers pay a set amount on each unit (covered in [[Lec_11-Taxes]]).

> [!warning] Easy to mix up
> A ceiling is a cap, so it is **above** you and stops the price rising. A floor is a bottom, so it stops the price falling. Neither word says anything about whether the rule bites. That is the next idea.

## 3. Binding and non-binding constraints

A constraint on behaviour may matter at some times and not at others. The slide's picture is a speed limit sign: a limit of 80 is irrelevant on a mountain track where nobody can go that fast, but it matters on an open road. When it matters, the constraint is **binding**.

> [!info] Binding Constraint
> A price control is **binding** when it forbids the free-market equilibrium price, so it changes the outcome. It is **not binding** when the equilibrium price is already legal, so it has no effect.

| | Price ceiling | Price floor |
|---|---|---|
| **Binding when** | ceiling is **below** the equilibrium price | floor is **above** the equilibrium price |
| **Not binding when** | ceiling is above equilibrium | floor is below equilibrium |
| **Result if binding** | [[Shortage]] ($Q_D > Q_S$) | [[Surplus]] / excess supply ($Q_S > Q_D$) |
| **Quantity traded** | the short side: $Q_S$ | the short side: $Q_D$ |
| **Example** | rent control | minimum wage |

> [!tip] Rule of thumb: the short side rules
> When the price is stuck away from equilibrium, only the **smaller** of $Q_D$ and $Q_S$ actually trades. Under a ceiling that is $Q_S$; under a floor it is $Q_D$. Nobody can be forced to buy or sell more than they want.

## 4. Example 1: the market for one-bedroom flats (price ceiling)

Free-market equilibrium: rent USD 1,500 per month, 300 flats. The slide's dollar figures are illustrative.

![[Lec10_price_ceiling_apartments.png]]

- **Not binding.** A ceiling of USD 2,000 is above the equilibrium rent of 1,500. The market still settles at 1,500 and 300 flats: **no effect**.
- **Binding.** A ceiling of USD 1,000 is below equilibrium, so the equilibrium price is now illegal. At 1,000, landlords supply only **250** flats but tenants demand **400**: a **shortage of 150**.
- **Short run vs long run.** In the long run supply and demand are **more price-elastic** (landlords can stop building or maintaining; tenants can restructure where they live). The same ceiling then gives quantity supplied 150 and demanded 450: a **larger shortage of 300**.

> [!example] Why the long run is worse (see [[Lec_08-Elasticity]])
> A flatter curve responds more to the same price gap, so the quantity gap between $Q_D$ and $Q_S$ grows with time.

## 5. Example 2: the market for unskilled labour (price floor)

Here the "price" is the **wage**, buyers are **firms** (labour demand) and sellers are **workers** (labour supply). Equilibrium: USD 12 per hour, 500 workers.

![[Lec10_minimum_wage.png]]

- **Not binding.** A minimum wage of USD 8 is below 12: no effect.
- **Binding.** A minimum wage of USD 15 is above equilibrium. Firms want only **400** workers but **550** want jobs: a **labour surplus of 150**, i.e. **unemployment**.

> [!warning] Who gains, who loses
> Workers who keep jobs earn more; the 100 who lose jobs (500 down to 400) and the extra 150 who would now like a job get nothing. The floor helps some low-wage workers and hurts others.

## 6. Class exercise: the hotel-room market

The exercise uses straight-line curves read off a grid. Free market: **P = 100, Q = 100**.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slides give the curves only as a graph. From the plotted points, supply is $P = Q$ and demand is $P = 150 - 0.5\,Q$ (so $Q_D = 300 - 2P$ and $Q_S = P$). I use these only to compute the numbers below; the slides themselves state only the quantities.

Three cases, each answering: quantity demanded, quantity supplied, market price, shortage or surplus?

![[Lec10_hotel_rooms_three_cases.png]]

| Case | Binding? | Market price | $Q_D$ | $Q_S$ | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Ceiling at 90 | Yes (90 < 100) | 90 | 120 | 90 | Shortage of 30 |
| B. Floor at 90 | **No** (90 < 100) | 100 | 100 | 100 | Neither: free equilibrium |
| C. Floor at 120 | Yes (120 > 100) | 120 | 60 | 120 | Surplus of 60 |

Check by substitution: at price 90, $90 = 150 - 0.5Q \Rightarrow Q_D = 120$, and $Q_S = 90$. At price 120: $120 = 150 - 0.5Q \Rightarrow Q_D = 60$, $Q_S = 120$.

> [!warning] Slide error to be aware of
> The class "solution" file lists case A as "quantity demanded 90, quantity supplied 120". That is reversed: a ceiling **below** equilibrium cuts supply and raises demand. The lecture's own later slides (and the graph) give $Q_D = 120$, $Q_S = 90$, with a shortage of 30, and that is what the table uses.

> [!tip] Exam habit
> Always ask first: is the control binding? Compare it with the equilibrium price. If not, the answer is just the free-market equilibrium.

## 7. Price controls and social surplus

Recall: **[[Consumer Surplus]]** is the area under demand and above the price; **[[Producer Surplus]]** is the area above supply and below the price; total surplus is their sum. In the free market both are shown below.

### 7.1 Binding price ceiling

The deck builds this in three pieces: who loses consumer surplus, who loses producer surplus, and what happens to the total.

![[Lec10_ceiling_surplus.png]]

**Consumer surplus.** Two opposing effects:
1. Some buyers lose out entirely: with only $Q_S$ available, some cannot find any seller at the controlled price (the shortage).
2. Buyers who **do** get a unit pay **less per unit**, so their surplus per unit rises.

**Producer surplus.** Falls sharply, for two reasons:
1. Sellers who leave the market (those between $Q_S$ and the old $Q^*$) lose their small surplus (the triangle).
2. Sellers who stay now sell at a **lower price**, so they lose the rectangle between the old and new price.

**Total surplus.** The rectangle sellers lose from the lower price is not destroyed: it is **transferred** to buyers as consumer surplus, so it does not change the total. The surplus of the traders who left the market, on both sides, **goes nowhere**: it just disappears. This lost triangle is the **[[Deadweight Loss]] (DWL)**.

> [!info] Deadweight Loss
> The fall in total surplus caused by a distortion: gains from trade that would have been enjoyed, but are not, because some mutually beneficial trades no longer happen.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides: the numbers
> The slides label the hotel ceiling case only with areas, not values. Using the straight lines above (free market: CS = 2,500, PS = 5,000, total 7,500) and assuming the 90 rooms go to the buyers who value them most, a ceiling of 90 gives CS = 3,375, PS = 4,050, total 7,425, so **DWL = 75**. These are my calculations from the plotted curves, not figures from the lecture.

### 7.2 Binding price floor

The mirror image. With the floor above equilibrium, buyers cut back to $Q_D$ and there is **excess supply**.

![[Lec10_floor_surplus.png]]

**Consumer surplus** falls: (1) buyers leave because the price is higher, and (2) those who remain pay more per unit.

**Producer surplus** has two effects: sellers sell **fewer units** (lose the triangle) but receive a **higher price** on the units they do sell (gain a rectangle). The slide stresses there are always these two effects.

**Total surplus.** The rectangle buyers lose from the higher price becomes producer surplus (a transfer, no effect on the total). The surplus of traders who disappear is the **deadweight loss**.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides: the numbers
> For the hotel floor of 120 (60 rooms sold), the same assumptions give CS = 900, PS = 5,400, total 6,300 and **DWL = 1,200**. Again my calculation, assuming the 60 rooms sold are the lowest-cost ones; the cost of producing unsold rooms is ignored.

### 7.3 Side-by-side summary

| | Binding ceiling | Binding floor |
|---|---|---|
| Price vs equilibrium | below | above |
| Quantity traded | $Q_S$ (short side) | $Q_D$ (short side) |
| Gap | shortage | excess supply |
| Consumer surplus | loses units, gains lower price | loses units, pays higher price |
| Producer surplus | loses units, receives lower price | loses units, receives higher price |
| Transfer | sellers to buyers (rectangle) | buyers to sellers (rectangle) |
| Total surplus | falls: **DWL** | falls: **DWL** |

> [!warning] Common mistake
> A control always creates a deadweight loss when binding, but one group can still be better off. A ceiling can raise consumer surplus (if the transfer outweighs the lost triangle) and a floor can raise producer surplus. It cannot raise **total** surplus above the free-market level, which is the efficient benchmark.

## Summary

- **Price ceiling** = legal maximum; **price floor** = legal minimum.
- A control only matters if it is **binding**: ceiling below equilibrium, floor above equilibrium. Otherwise the free-market outcome stands.
- Binding ceiling: **shortage**, with $Q_S$ traded. Binding floor: **surplus** (unemployment in the labour market), with $Q_D$ traded.
- Gaps grow in the long run because curves are more elastic.
- Surplus accounting: one rectangle is **transferred** between buyers and sellers; the triangle of lost trades is the **deadweight loss**.
- Hotel exercise: ceiling 90 gives $Q_D = 120$, $Q_S = 90$; floor 90 is not binding; floor 120 gives $Q_D = 60$, $Q_S = 120$.
