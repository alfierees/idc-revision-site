## 2. What is economics?

**[[Scarcity]]** is the limited nature of society's resources. **[[Economics]]** is the study of how society manages its scarce resources. Economics is the art of solving the problem of **unlimited needs with scarce resources**, for individuals, firms and society as a whole.

| Decision maker | Example decision | What is scarce |
|---|---|---|
| Individuals | What to buy, how much to work, save and spend | Time and money |
| Firms | How much to produce, how many workers to hire | Capital, materials, money |
| Society | How to split resources between defence, consumer goods, the environment | Money, people, time |

**Two branches:**

| Branch | Studies | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| [[Microeconomics]] | How households and firms make decisions and interact in markets | Prices, demand, firm output |
| [[Macroeconomics]] | Economy-wide phenomena | Inflation, unemployment, growth |

This course is microeconomics. Principles 8-10 are the macro principles.

> [!example] Managing scarcity: the library
> Libraries face rising demand for hot new titles (the slide's illustration is a library stack). Two real strategies were shown:
> - **Cambridge Public Library, "speed read":** new books can be borrowed for **7 days** instead of the usual 21.
> - **Waltham Public Library, "rental":** a small fee of **USD 0.50 per day**, with a shortened 14-day loan.
>
> The slide asks which is best and gives no answer. The point is that both are rules for allocating a scarce good, one by shortening time, the other by using a price.

## 3. Group one: how people make decisions

### Principle 1: People face tradeoffs

A **[[Tradeoff]]** is the sacrifice we make when we decide. Every decision involves one.

- Partying before a midterm leaves less time for studying.
- More money to spend means longer working hours and less leisure.
- Behavioural economics: people tend to prioritise the **short term over the long term**.
- Workforce: **job security vs opportunity**.
- Government regulation: **protection vs freedom**.

**Society's big tradeoff: [[Efficiency]] vs [[Equality]].**

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Efficiency** | Society gets the most from its scarce resources |
| **Equality** | Prosperity is distributed uniformly among society's members |

Redistributing income from rich to poor moves towards equality, but it weakens the incentive to work and produce, so the economic **pie** shrinks. Recognising tradeoffs is the first step to good decisions, not a reason to avoid choosing.

### Principle 2: The cost of something is what you give up to get it

The **[[Opportunity Cost]]** of an item is whatever must be given up to obtain it. Decisions compare the costs and benefits of the alternatives, and opportunity cost is the **relevant cost** for that comparison.

> [!example] Slide examples
> - A year of travelling costs not only the trip but also the wages forgone by not working that year.
> - A movie costs not only the ticket but also the value of the time spent in the cinema.
> - You have USD 30,000 and either buy shares in Company XYZ or fund a graduate degree. You buy the shares. The opportunity cost is the **higher lifetime earnings** the degree might have brought.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Opportunity cost is the value of the **best forgone alternative**, not the sum of every alternative, and not only the money price.

### Principle 3: Rational people think at the margin

A **[[Rational Decision Maker]]** systematically and purposefully does the best they can to reach their objectives. They decide by weighing **[[Marginal Change]]s**: small, incremental adjustments to an existing plan.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| [[Marginal Benefit]] | The additional benefit from the change |
| [[Marginal Cost]] | The additional cost of the change |

**Decision rule:** act if

$$
\text{Marginal Benefit} > \text{Marginal Cost}
$$

> [!example] Are we rational? The hotel puzzle
> A basic hotel room costs USD 437 and you are comfortable paying it. A luxury room costs USD 484 and you feel uncomfortable. Yet you would happily spend USD 47 on sneakers. The USD 47 *upgrade* is the same money. The real decision is the **incremental difference** between the two options, the "upgrade value".

> [!example] Is business class expensive? A three-step test
> 1. **Marginal cost:** how much extra does the upgrade cost? (Slide: an extra USD 1,000.)
> 2. **Marginal benefit:** what do I get over economy? (A lie-flat bed and better food.)
> 3. **Compare:** is that worth USD 1,000 to me?
>
> Different people reach different answers because of different preferences and incomes. That is not irrationality: the rule is the same, the inputs differ.

The deck also shows a seven-item "have you ever..." list of everyday quirks (pressing an elevator button repeatedly, abandoning a call-centre queue, choosing the middle-priced quote). They are discussion prompts for whether we always behave rationally. The slides do not draw a formal conclusion from them.

### Principle 4: People respond to incentives

An **[[Incentive]]** is something that induces a person to act, such as the prospect of a reward or punishment. Rational people's decisions respond to **external rewards**, positive or negative. Because costs and benefits drive choices, changing them changes behaviour.

> [!example] Slide examples
> - Attendance counts for 10% of the grade, so people come to class.
> - Petrol prices rise, so consumers buy more hybrids and fewer SUVs.
> - Cigarette taxes rise, so teen smoking falls.
> - Insurance that fits a device tracking your driving rewards safe driving.

## 4. Active learning: the Mustang problem

> [!example] Question (slide)
> You are selling your 1996 Mustang and have **already spent USD 1,000 on repairs**. At the last minute the transmission dies. Repairing it costs **USD 600**, or you can sell as is. Should you repair it if:
> **A.** book value is USD 6,500 with a working transmission and USD 5,700 without;
> **B.** book value is USD 6,000 with a working transmission and USD 5,500 without?
> Hint: rational people think at the margin.

**Solution.**

| | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| Value if repaired | 6,500 | 6,000 |
| Value if not repaired | 5,700 | 5,500 |
| **Marginal benefit** (the difference) | **800** | **500** |
| **Marginal cost** (repair) | **600** | **600** |
| Verdict | 800 > 600: **repair** | 500 < 600: **do not repair** |

![[Lec01_mustang_repair.png]]

> [!tip] Two observations from the slides
> 1. The **USD 1,000 already spent** is irrelevant. It is gone whichever you choose. Only the cost and benefit of the *marginal* repair matter (this is the sunk-cost idea, though the slides do not use that name).
> 2. The change in **incentives** from A to B (a smaller gain from repairing) changed the decision. Principles 3 and 4 working together.

## 5. Group two: how people interact

### Principle 5: Trade can make everyone better off

Rather than being self-sufficient, countries (and people) can trade. This is **[[Gains from Trade]]** and the topic of the next lectures.

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Specialisation | Some people's slice of the pie gets **smaller** (the slide's example: China opening to the world in the 1980s) |
| Larger markets | |
| More productive firms | |
| Political cooperation | |

The slide's suggested answer to the cons: empower people through education, ease mobility, and provide welfare out of the **bigger pie**.

Trade also works at the personal level. The slide's example is **Fiverr**, an Israeli startup and online platform for freelancing.

### Principle 6: Markets are usually a good way to organise economic activity

A **[[Market]]** is a group of buyers and sellers (they need not be in one place). "Organising economic activity" means deciding:

- **what** goods to produce,
- **how** to produce them,
- **how much** of each,
- **who gets** them.

| | Centrally planned economy | Market economy |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | Government officials, on the belief that only they can organise activity for everyone's benefit | Millions of firms and households |
| Firms | Told what to make | Decide whom to hire and what to make |
| Households | Told what is available | Decide where to work and what to buy |
| Guide | Plan | **Prices and self-interest** |

This is puzzling: nobody looks after society as a whole, everyone pursues their own well-being, yet market economies have proved the most successful at promoting overall well-being.

> [!info] The Invisible Hand (Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*, 1776)
> Households and firms act as if "led by an **[[Invisible Hand]]**" to promote general economic well-being. It works through the **price system**: buyers look at price to decide how much to buy, sellers look at price to decide how much to sell, so price reflects both the **value of the good to society and the cost of producing it**. Prices adjust to guide buyers and sellers to outcomes that maximise well-being.

### Principle 7: Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes

Governments are essential for enforcing **property rights**, so people can own and control scarce resources. Beyond that, markets sometimes fail and governments can intervene for two reasons:

| Goal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Promote **efficiency** | Create a larger pie |
| Promote **equality** | Change how the pie is divided |

**[[Market Failure]]** is when the market fails to allocate society's resources efficiently. Two causes:

| Cause | Definition | Example on the slide |
|---|---|---|
| [[Externality]] | Production or consumption of a good affects bystanders not part of the transaction | Pollution |
| [[Market Power]] | A single buyer or seller has substantial influence on the market price | Monopoly |

Even when the invisible hand is efficient it may leave large disparities, so governments may use tax and welfare policy to promote equity. Studying economics helps you judge when an intervention is justified on efficiency or equality grounds and when it is not.

> [!example] Discussion question
> Principles 6 and 7 together: markets are usually best, but government can sometimes improve them, and the challenge is knowing **when and how**. The slide asks: *Should Israel raise its minimum wage? What are the benefits and the consequences?* No answer is given; it is an open discussion.

## 6. Group three: how the economy works as a whole

The last content slide only names the three **macroeconomic principles**, which belong to macroeconomics and are not developed in this lecture:

- **Principle 8:** a country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services.
- **Principle 9:** prices rise when the government prints too much money.
- **Principle 10:** society faces a short-run tradeoff between inflation and unemployment.

## Summary

| # | Principle | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | People face tradeoffs | Choosing one thing means giving up another; efficiency vs equality |
| 2 | Cost = what you give up | Use opportunity cost, not just the money price |
| 3 | Think at the margin | Act when marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost; ignore sunk costs |
| 4 | Respond to incentives | Change costs or benefits and behaviour changes |
| 5 | Trade helps everyone | Specialisation enlarges the pie, but some lose out |
| 6 | Markets organise activity | Prices guide decisions, the invisible hand |
| 7 | Government can help | Property rights, market failure (externalities, market power), equity |
| 8-10 | Macro | Productivity, money printing and inflation, inflation-unemployment tradeoff |
