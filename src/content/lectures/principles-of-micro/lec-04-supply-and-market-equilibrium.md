# Lec 04: Supply and Market Equilibrium

> Part of: [[Principles of Micro]]
> Key concepts: [[Supply]], [[Law of Supply]], [[Supply Curve]], [[Market Supply]], [[Change in Quantity Supplied]], [[Change in Supply]], [[Determinants of Supply]], [[Price Taker]], [[Perfect Competition]], [[Surplus]], [[Shortage]], [[Market Equilibrium]], [[Equilibrium Price]], [[Equilibrium Quantity]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> The lecture opens with a recap of demand (including two odd demand curves), then builds the **supply side** from the producer's costs, and finally puts supply and demand together to find the **market equilibrium**. The running example is the market for cups of coffee. Next lecture proves the cost link formally.

## 1. Recap: demand

The [[Law of Demand]]: when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls, and the reverse. The consumer's decision about how many units to buy depends on five things.

| Determinant | Effect on the demand curve |
| --- | --- |
| The good's own price | **Movement along** the curve |
| Tastes | Shift |
| Income | Shift |
| Price of related goods (complements, substitutes) | Shift |
| Expectations (if the price is expected to rise, I buy more today) | Shift |

Only the good's own price moves you along the curve. The other four are non-price determinants and shift the whole curve.

### Two curves that break the law of demand

Both are upward sloping: with income held constant, a higher price leads to a higher quantity demanded.

| | [[Giffen Good]] | [[Veblen Good]] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type of good | A non-luxury **inferior good** | A **luxury** good |
| Why quantity rises with price | Poor households spend so much on the staple that a price rise eats their budget, so they cut dearer foods and buy more of the staple | Bought mainly to show off wealth, so a higher price makes it more attractive |
| Examples (slides) | Potatoes, rice (Marshall's 1895 bread example, credited to Sir Robert Giffen) | Diamonds, yachts (after Thorstein Veblen) |

> [!tip] Don't confuse the two
> Both violate the law of demand, but for opposite reasons: Giffen goods because the buyer is poor, Veblen goods because the buyer wants status.

## 2. The market assumptions

The lecture works in the [[Perfect Competition]] model. Each good has a **market price**. The "baby steps" assumptions are:

1. **Many sellers and buyers** (unlike monopoly or oligopoly).
2. **Perfect information**: it costs nothing to find the price at every selling point.
3. Each seller and buyer is **small relative to the market** and cannot dictate or change the price.

## 3. How a producer decides: the supply side

### The producer is a price taker

Because the producer is too small to move the market price, the price is **given**:

- Sell above the market price and nobody buys from you, since buyers go elsewhere.
- Sell below it and some lucky buyers get a bargain, but you are too small to produce enough to serve everyone, so you simply lose revenue.

So a [[Price Taker]] does not choose the price. **The only decision is the quantity to produce.**

A producer wants first to **cover costs**, and then to make a profit, so costs drive the quantity choice. For a takeaway coffee the slides show a cost breakdown: wages are the biggest item (34.6%), then rent, operating costs and GST (32%), with coffee beans and milk about 11.9% each, and smaller shares for equipment and the cup and lid (about 4.8% each).

### Why cost per cup rises with output

The lecture states an assumption it will prove next class: **the cost of producing each extra unit rises as more units are produced.** The reason is [[Diminishing Marginal Product]]. Worked example with a wage of 30 NIS per hour:

| Workers | Cups per hour | Extra cups from the last worker | Total wage cost (NIS) | Cost per cup (NIS) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10 | 0 | 30 | 30/10 = 3.00 |
| 2 | 18 | 8 | 60 | 60/18 = 3.33 |
| 3 | 24 | 6 | 90 | 90/24 = 3.75 |
| 4 | 28 | 4 | 120 | 120/28 = 4.29 |
| 5 | 30 | 2 | 150 | 150/30 = 5.00 |
| 6 | 31 | 1 | 180 | 180/31 = 5.81 |

Each added worker costs the same 30 NIS but adds fewer cups (8, 6, 4, 2, 1), so the wage bill per cup climbs.

$$
\text{Cost per cup} = \frac{\text{Total wage cost}}{\text{Number of cups}}
$$

> [!note] Slide 14 lists the first worker's contribution as 0
> The table shows 0 in the "additional cups" column for the first worker, even though that worker produces the first 10 cups. Read the column as "extra cups beyond the first row".

### From the cost curve to the supply curve

Assuming for now that the only cost is labour, the cost-per-cup curve slopes upward. The producer should sell a cup only if the price covers the cost of producing it:

> If the price is above the cost of producing the **last unit**, it makes sense to produce it.

So the rising cost curve *is* the supply curve: at price $p$, the producer supplies the quantity $q$ where the cost of the last unit equals $p$. The slides say the formal proof comes next week, and that the curve can be drawn curved or as a straight line.

## 4. Supply: definitions

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| [[Supply]] / quantity supplied | The amount of a good or service sellers are **willing and able** to sell |
| [[Law of Supply]] | Quantity supplied **rises** when the price rises, other things equal |
| [[Supply Curve]] | Upward sloping line showing quantity supplied at each price |

### Market supply versus individual supply

[[Market Supply]] is the total quantity supplied by all sellers. It is built by **horizontal addition**: at each price, add up the quantities each seller would supply. The slides use the notation $Q^S$ for market supply and $q^S$ for an individual seller.

> [!example] Reconstructed from the slides
> If at a price of 3 one café supplies 10 cups and another supplies 5, the market supplies 15 at that price. Add at each price along the horizontal (quantity) axis, never along the price axis. (Illustrative numbers, not from the slides.)

### Movement along vs shift of the curve

| | [[Change in Quantity Supplied]] | [[Change in Supply]] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Movement **along** one supply curve | **Shift** of the whole curve |
| Caused by | A change in the good's own **price** only | A change in a **non-price determinant** |

## 5. Determinants of supply (what shifts the curve)

The lecture lists three, with input prices worked in detail:

- **Input prices** (raw materials, wages): when they **fall**, producers offer more at every price and supply shifts **right**; when they **rise**, supply shifts **left**.
- **Technology**.
- **Expectations**.

Shorthand on the slide: price of inputs down means supply up.

### Worked example: the wage rises from 30 to 35 NIS

| Workers | Cups per hour | Total wage cost at 35 | Cost per cup at 35 | Cost per cup at 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10 | 35 | 3.50 | 3.00 |
| 2 | 18 | 70 | 3.89 | 3.33 |
| 3 | 24 | 105 | 4.38 | 3.75 |
| 4 | 28 | 140 | 5.00 | 4.29 |
| 5 | 30 | 175 | 5.83 | 5.00 |
| 6 | 31 | 210 | 6.77 | 5.81 |

At every output level the cost per cup is higher at the higher wage. Because the cost per unit is what the producer must cover when selling at price $P$, a higher cost curve means a **leftward (upward) shift of supply**: less is offered at each price.

> [!warning] Typo on the slide
> On slide 21 the cups produced for workers 5 and 6 are printed as 26 and 27, but the cost-per-cup figures (5.83 and 6.77) only work out with 30 and 31 cups (175/30 and 210/31). The table above uses 30 and 31, matching the 30 NIS table.

![[Lec04_supply_shift.png]]

> A fall in the good's **own price** never shifts the supply curve: it moves you down along it. Only non-price determinants shift it.

## 6. Equilibrium

The slides use one linear example. Reading off the diagrams: demand passes through (price 5, quantity 9) and (price 1, quantity 21), supply through (price 1, quantity 5) and (price 5, quantity 25).

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> Those points fit $Q^D = 24 - 3P$ and $Q^S = 5P$. The slides do not state these equations; they are read from the graphs and match every quantity the slides give.

### Surplus (excess supply)

Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded. At $P = 5$: $Q^D = 9$, $Q^S = 25$, so the surplus is **16 cups**.

![[Lec04_surplus.png]]

What happens: facing unsold stock, sellers **cut the price** to increase sales. That makes $Q^D$ **rise** and $Q^S$ **fall**, shrinking the surplus. Prices keep falling until the market reaches equilibrium.

### Shortage (excess demand)

Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. At $P = 1$: $Q^D = 21$, $Q^S = 5$, so the shortage is **16 cups**.

![[Lec04_shortage.png]]

What happens: sellers **raise the price**. That makes $Q^D$ **fall** and $Q^S$ **rise**, shrinking the shortage, until the market reaches equilibrium. (Slide 31 says "prices continue to fall" here, a slip: the price is rising towards equilibrium.)

### Market equilibrium

[[Market Equilibrium]]: the situation with **one price** at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. In the example this is $P^E = 3$ and $Q^E = 15$, where the two curves cross. Check: $24 - 3(3) = 15$ and $5(3) = 15$.

![[Lec04_equilibrium.png]]

| Price relative to equilibrium | Market state | Sellers respond by | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Above $P^E$ | Surplus: $Q^S > Q^D$ | Cutting the price | $Q^D$ up, $Q^S$ down |
| At $P^E$ | $Q^D = Q^S$ | No pressure | Stable |
| Below $P^E$ | Shortage: $Q^D > Q^S$ | Raising the price | $Q^D$ down, $Q^S$ up |

> [!tip] Intuition
> Equilibrium is the only price with no pressure to change. Above it, unsold goods push the price down; below it, queues push it up. The market is pulled to the crossing point without anyone planning it.

## Summary

- Demand shifts with tastes, income, related prices and expectations; own price moves you along it. Giffen and Veblen goods are the exceptions that slope upward.
- In perfect competition the producer is a **price taker** and only chooses quantity.
- Cost per unit rises with output because of diminishing marginal product, so the **supply curve slopes upward** (law of supply).
- Market supply is the **horizontal sum** of individual supplies.
- Own price changes cause **movement along** the supply curve; input prices, technology and expectations **shift** it.
- A price above equilibrium creates a surplus and pushes the price down; below creates a shortage and pushes it up. Equilibrium is where $Q^D = Q^S$.
