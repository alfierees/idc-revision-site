# Demand

> Part of: [[Micro-Economics I]]
> Key concepts: [[Demand Function]], [[Law of Demand]], [[Demand Curve]], [[Normal Good]], [[Inferior Good]], [[Substitutes and Complements]], [[Market Demand]]

This lecture moves from the consumer's optimal choice ([[Optimal Choice]]) to the object the whole later course uses: **demand**, the relationship between a good's price and the quantity a household wants to buy.

## 1. Who decides: firms and households

- A **firm** turns inputs (resources) into outputs; an **entrepreneur** organises the firm and bears its risk.
- A **household** (consumer) is the consuming unit. Demand in a product market is the household side.

## 2. What drives a household's demand

A household's choice for one product depends on six things:

| Factor | Role |
|---|---|
| Own price of the product | Moves you **along** the demand curve |
| Income (a **flow**: earnings per period) | Shifts demand |
| Wealth (a **stock**: assets minus debts) | Shifts demand |
| Prices of other goods | Shifts demand |
| Tastes and preferences | Shifts demand |
| Expectations about future income, wealth, prices | Shifts demand |

> [!info] Quantity demanded vs demand
> **Quantity demanded** is one number: how much is bought in a period at the current price. **Demand** is the whole price-quantity relationship (the schedule or curve). Only a change in the **own price** changes quantity demanded; a change in any other factor changes demand itself.

In the language of [[Optimal Choice]], the [[Demand Function]] is just the optimal bundle written as a function of prices and income; the factors above are its arguments (plus tastes and expectations, which sit outside the pure budget-and-preferences model).

## 3. The law of demand

A **demand schedule** lists quantity at each price; a **demand curve** plots it. The lecture's running example is Alex's weekly gasoline demand: at $8 he buys 0 gallons, at $5 he buys 5, at $3 he buys 10, at $1 he buys 20, and at $0 he buys 26 (the schedule has these points and more).

![[Lec05_demand_movement.png]]

The **law of demand**: holding everything else fixed (*ceteris paribus*), a higher price lowers quantity demanded and a lower price raises it, so the curve slopes down.

Three properties of an individual curve:

1. **Negative slope** (the law itself).
2. It **hits the quantity axis**: even at a price of zero, demand is finite, because time is limited and extra units give diminishing marginal satisfaction (Alex stops at 26 gallons).
3. It **hits the price axis**: at a high enough price demand is zero, because income and wealth are limited (Alex buys nothing at $8).

How steep or curved it is depends on the household's tastes. The course texts' point that even "required" goods obey the law: when a required textbook is expensive, students find substitutes (used copies, sharing, going without), so sales fall below enrolment.

> [!warning] Not guaranteed by the model
> The law of demand is an empirical regularity. In the consumer-choice model a negative slope needs the substitution effect to win; see [[Income Effect]] and [[Substitution Effect]] in the next lectures.

## 4. Movement along vs shift of the curve

| Cause | What changes | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| Own price changes | Quantity demanded | **Movement along** the curve |
| Related prices, income, tastes, expectations change | Demand | **Shift** of the curve |

![[Lec05_demand_shift.png]]

In the schedule, income rising from 500 to 700 per week raises Alex's gasoline demand at every price (at $5 from 5 to 10 gallons, at $0 from 26 to 30), so the whole curve moves right. Gasoline is a [[Normal Good]] for him.

> [!tip] Test before you draw
> Ask "what moved?". If the answer is the good's own price, slide along the curve. If it is anything else, redraw the curve.

## 5. The shifters in detail

### Prices of related goods

| Relation | Definition | If good A's price rises |
|---|---|---|
| [[Substitutes and Complements\|Substitutes]] | Can replace each other (perfect substitutes: identical) | Demand for B **rises** (shifts right) |
| [[Substitutes and Complements\|Complements]] | Go together | Demand for B **falls** (shifts left) |

Lecture example: dearer hamburger lowers hamburger quantity demanded (movement), raises demand for chicken (substitute, shift right) and lowers demand for ketchup (complement, shift left). Compare [[Perfect Substitutes]] and [[Perfect Complements]] from the preference lectures.

### Income and wealth

- [[Normal Good]]: demand rises when income rises (the curve shifts right).
- [[Inferior Good]]: demand falls when income rises (the curve shifts left). The lecture's example contrasts hamburger (inferior) with steak (normal).

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> "Normal" and "inferior" describe the sign of the response of demand to income, not quality. The same good can be normal at low incomes and inferior at high ones; the slide only gives the simple definition.

### Tastes and expectations

Tastes are **volatile** and **idiosyncratic**, so the model cannot say much in general. Expectations matter because buying today depends on what you expect about future prices and income (expecting a higher price tomorrow raises demand today).

## 6. From household to market demand

**Market demand** is the sum of all households' demands: at each price, add up the individual quantities. Graphically this is a **horizontal sum**.

![[Lec05_market_demand.png]]

The lecture's coffee example has three households. At $3.50 they buy 4, 0 and 4 pounds (market: 8); at $1.50 they buy 8, 3 and 9 (market: 20).

> [!warning] Add quantities, not prices
> Sum horizontally (quantities at a given price). Adding prices at a given quantity is a different object and is not market demand.

## Summary

- Demand is the whole price-quantity relationship; quantity demanded is one point on it.
- The law of demand gives a downward-sloping curve that meets both axes.
- Own price: **move along**. Related prices, income, wealth, tastes, expectations: **shift**.
- Substitutes move together with a rival's price; complements move opposite. Normal goods rise with income; inferior goods fall.
- Market demand is the horizontal sum of household demands.
