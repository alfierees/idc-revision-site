# Intertemporal Choice (Consumption over Time)

> Part of: [[Micro-Economics I]]
> Key concepts: [[Intertemporal Choice]], [[Intertemporal Budget Constraint]], [[Interest Rate]], [[Budget Constraint]], [[Indifference Curve]], [[Optimal Choice]], [[Income Effect]], [[Substitution Effect]]

The deck is five slides. It reuses the two-good consumer model with one twist: the two "goods" are **consumption now** and **consumption later**.

## 1. Setting up the problem

A person decides how much of income to consume now and how much to save for the future. Treat this as a choice of a bundle:

- Good 1: consumption **when young** (today).
- Good 2: consumption **when old** (the future).
- The **relative price** between them is set by the [[Interest Rate]].
- The solution is the same as before: the best affordable bundle, found where the [[Budget Constraint]] touches an [[Indifference Curve]] ([[Optimal Choice]]).

## 2. The intertemporal budget constraint

Saving one dollar today gives $1+r$ dollars tomorrow. So giving up a dollar of young consumption buys $1+r$ of old consumption: the budget line has slope $-(1+r)$.

![[Lec09_consumption_saving_optimum.png]]

The slide's figure has intercepts of **$100,000** on the young axis and **$110,000** on the old axis, and the optimum at **$50,000 when young and $55,000 when old**. The ratio of intercepts is $110/100 = 1.1$, which corresponds to an interest rate of 10 percent. The picture above redraws the same geometry with illustrative curve shapes.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The slide does not state income or the interest rate. I inferred an endowment of 100,000 in the young period (the young-axis intercept) and $r = 10\%$ from the two intercepts. Optimal saving when young is then 100,000 minus 50,000 = 50,000, giving 55,000 when old.

> [!tip] Why the optimum is where it is
> At the optimum the slope of the indifference curve (the [[Marginal Rate of Substitution]]) equals the slope of the budget line, so the MRS equals $1+r$: the person's willingness to give up future consumption for current consumption matches the market's rate.

## 3. What a higher interest rate does to saving

A higher rate makes future consumption cheaper in terms of today's, so the budget line **rotates outward** and becomes **steeper**, pivoting around the point where all income is consumed when young. Consumption when old rises either way; the question is what happens to consumption when young, hence saving.

| Effect | Mechanism | Consumption when young | Saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Substitution Effect]] | Future consumption is cheaper, so shift toward it | falls | rises |
| [[Income Effect]] | The person is effectively richer, so buys more of everything | rises | falls |

![[Lec09_interest_rate_effects.png]]

- **Panel (a): substitution dominates.** Young consumption falls, old consumption rises, saving **rises**.
- **Panel (b): income dominates.** Consumption rises in both periods, saving **falls**.

The theory alone does not decide which wins; it depends on preferences.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Do not assume a higher interest rate must raise saving. The two effects pull in opposite directions on young consumption, and only the income effect can push saving down.

> [!note] Reconstructed from the slides
> The figure shows the two outcomes, with indifference curves in the original. My redraw marks only the before and after bundles on the two budget lines, whose numbers are illustrative and not from the deck.

## Summary

- Intertemporal choice is ordinary consumer choice with present and future consumption as the two goods.
- The interest rate is the relative price; the budget line has slope $-(1+r)$.
- A higher rate rotates the line outward; the net effect on saving is ambiguous: substitution raises it, income lowers it.
