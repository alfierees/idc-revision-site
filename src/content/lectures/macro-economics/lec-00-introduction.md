## ❓ The Big Questions of Macro

The deck frames macro through a handful of open questions, each backed by data on the slides.

### 1. What determines long-run growth?

- Why did **real GDP per capita** rise so much during the 20th century?
- Why are some countries much richer than others? (The slides show regional paths for 1860–2022 and a cross-country picture for 2022.)

→ Taken up in [[Lec_04-Production]] (development accounting) and [[Lec_11-Productivity]].

### 2. What causes business cycles?

- Fluctuations in activity around a trend. Each data point splits into a **trend component** (expected growth) and a **cyclical component** (actual minus expected).
- Is there a common cause? The slides contrast the financial crisis and the Covid-19 crisis: the **causes are heterogeneous**.

→ See [[Lec_01-Data Review]] and [[Real Business Cycles]].

### 3. What affects employment and unemployment?

- Why does it fluctuate so much? Why is unemployment high even during expansions?
- Slides: US employment-population ratio, unemployment rate and labor-force participation, 1948–2025.

→ [[Lec_07-Labor Market]], [[Lec_08-Labor Market Data, Participation & Unemployment]].

### 4. Inequality (income and wealth)

- Slides: income inequality in the US and other advanced economies (Hoffmann, Lee and Lemieux, 2020), the Gini coefficient of disposable income across countries, and inequality during recent recessions.
- Recessions can have **very different implications for low-earnings workers**. The deck flags this as suggestive only: "a more systematic analysis is required".

→ [[Lec_09-Inequality & Polarization]].

### 5. Technological progress and automation

- "Disappearing jobs", and the question: is technology a **replacement or a helper** for workers?
- **Polarization** along occupational characteristics (Jaimovich and Siu, 2020).
- Covid-19 again: differential effects by **risk of automation** (Frey and Osborne index; Ding and Molina, 2020).

→ [[Lec_09-Inequality & Polarization]] (task-based model, routinization).

### 6. Inflation and deflation

- Slides: annual US inflation 1914–2024 and Israeli inflation (2021–2025, and annual since 1952).

→ [[Lec_01-Data Review]].

### 7. Fiscal and monetary policy

- Slides: US **debt-to-GDP ratio**, the US Federal Funds rate, and conventional vs. unconventional monetary policy (balance-sheet expansion, with the mix of Treasuries moving towards longer maturities). The deck notes US GDP is around 20 trillion USD for scale.

→ [[Lec_10-Fiscal Policy]] (this course has **no role for monetary policy**, see the assumptions below).

## 🔬 How We Study These Questions

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Data** | Study the data we are interested in |
| **2. Theory** | Develop a **model** that explains regularities in the data |
| **3. Test** | Calibrate / estimate / test: does it account for the facts? If not, which assumptions should change? |
| **4. Use** | Use the model **with caution** to (i) design and evaluate policies, (ii) make forecasts |

## 🧱 Why Micro-Foundations?

The deck quotes Shimer (2009): modern macro differs from micro **only in its focus on aggregation and general equilibrium**. Virtually all modern macro models rest on two foundations:

1. **Households maximize (expected) utility** subject to a budget constraint.
2. **Firms maximize (expected) profits.**

> [!tip] Agreeing on the foundations is not agreeing on the answers
> Whether and how the economy reacts to monetary or fiscal policy, and what optimal policy looks like, depends on more controversial issues (the nature of shocks, how beliefs form and evolve).

Macro and micro "are not that distinct": most models in this course are **micro-founded**. Macro simply focuses on **aggregate phenomena**, and we can (and will) add government to the mix.

## 📐 The Plan of the Course

- **Review** data measurement and definitions ([[Lec_01-Data Review]]).
- **Build a model "from the ground up"** with **three participants**: households (consume, save, work), firms (produce, sell, invest, hire) and government (imposes policies); and **two markets**: goods and labor.
- A money market could be added, but **in our model money is neutral** and has no effect on the real economy, so there is **no room for monetary policy**.
- **Apply** the model as a theory of growth and business cycles.
- **Detours** that extend specific models: **labor-market frictions**, **fiscal policy (debt and taxation)**, and **productivity (data and potential sources)**.
- Keep the data in mind: can the model account for macro data, and which assumptions does that require?

## 🧩 What Is a Model? The Assumptions of "Our" Model

A model is a **simplified version of reality** where many details are excluded; it is a set of assumptions that lead to a mathematical and/or graphical representation.

> [!example] The assumptions of the course model
> - Three participants (households, firms, government) and two markets (labor, goods)
> - **All individuals are identical** (representative household)
> - **All firms are identical**
> - All goods are identical; all assets are identical
> - Firms maximize profits; individuals maximize utility
> - **Prices (wages and the interest rate) are flexible**
> - **Everything is real**: nominal prices, money, inflation and monetary policy are irrelevant

Some of these assumptions are at the heart of the **neoclassical growth model**, a major workhorse of macro.

### Why use models at all?

- **No single model addresses every issue.** Example: a model with identical households can match some aggregate regularities but **cannot be used to study inequality**.
- Models give useful **insights** into economic behavior.
- **Alternative assumptions give different implications.** For instance, swap flexible prices for **sticky prices (or wages)** and money is **not neutral**, so there is room for monetary policy (the next macro course).
- Ideally assumptions are based on data, "but it is not as easy as it sounds".
- Recommended reading: Chapters 2 (very short) and 3 of the textbook.

> [!quote] The importance of assumptions (Solow, 1956, as quoted in the deck)
> Theory depends on assumptions that are not quite true; the art is to make the simplifying assumptions so that the results are not very sensitive to them. A **crucial** assumption is one on which the conclusions do depend sensitively, so it must be reasonably realistic. If a result flows from a special crucial assumption that is dubious, the result is suspect.

## 🎯 Summary

1. Macro studies **aggregates**, but builds them from **household utility maximization and firm profit maximization**.
2. The course asks about **growth, cycles, employment, inequality, automation, inflation and fiscal/monetary policy**.
3. Method: **data → model → test/calibrate → use with caution**.
4. Our model: **households, firms, government; goods and labor markets; identical agents; flexible prices; everything real, so money is neutral**.
5. Different assumptions (e.g. sticky prices, heterogeneous households) answer different questions, so know **which assumption a conclusion depends on**.
