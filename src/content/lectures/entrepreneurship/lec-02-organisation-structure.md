## 📚 Session Overview

This class asks a deceptively simple question: **does the way you draw the org chart help or hurt innovation?** We cover four things:

1. What organisational structure *is* (and the dimensions that define it).
2. **Why** structure matters — especially for innovation.
3. The four canonical **structure types** (hierarchical, functional, flat, matrix).
4. How to design an **innovation structure** (centralized, decentralized, hybrid).

## 🧱 What Is Organisational Structure?

> [!info] Definition ^def-org-structure
> ==Organisational structure== is the arrangement of **roles, responsibilities, and relationships** within an organisation. It dictates how activities are **coordinated and directed** toward achieving organisational goals.

The definition packs in three coordinated things. It is worth separating them, because innovation depends on all three:

| Element | What it fixes | Innovation-relevant question |
|---------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **Roles** | Who does what (division of labour) | Is there space/mandate for people to *explore*, not just execute? |
| **Responsibilities** | Who is accountable / who decides | Are decision rights close enough to the work for fast experiments? |
| **Relationships** | Who reports to / talks to whom | Can information cross boundaries so ideas can *recombine*? |

> [!tip] The mental model: structure is the org's "operating system"
> Structure quietly sets three things that make or break innovation: **decision rights** (who can say yes), **information flow** (who hears about what), and **accountability** (who owns the outcome). Innovation is fundamentally the *recombination of information under uncertainty* — so a structure that throttles cross-boundary communication or centralises every "yes" will starve it, even if the strategy and talent are excellent.

Structures are usually described along a few underlying dimensions — the "type" names below are just common *bundles* of these settings:

- ==Centralisation== — how high up decisions are made (concentrated at the top vs. pushed down).
- ==Formalisation== — how much behaviour is governed by rules/procedures.
- ==Span of control== — how many reports each manager has (wide span → flatter org).
- ==Departmentalisation== — how work is grouped (by function, by product/division, or both).

## ⚙️ Why Structure Matters for Innovation

Different structures **impact decision-making, communication, and innovation** in predictable ways. The trade-off almost always reduces to **control vs. speed/creativity**:

- Structures built for **control & efficiency** (tall, centralised, formalised) give you consistency and scale — but slow decisions and silo information, which suffocates novelty.
- Structures built for **flexibility** (flat, decentralised, fluid) give you speed and cross-pollination — but can lack the discipline to commercialise and scale what they invent.

> [!info] Enrichment — Mechanistic vs. Organic (Burns & Stalker, 1961)
> A classic contingency result: **mechanistic** structures (rigid, hierarchical, rule-bound) perform well in *stable* environments, while **organic** structures (flexible, decentralised, fluid roles) perform well in *fast-changing, uncertain* environments — exactly the conditions innovation lives in. The lesson isn't "flat is always better"; it's that the *right* structure is contingent on **how fast your environment and technology are changing**.

## 🏛️ The Four Types of Organisational Structure

Structures range from **centralised & skill-focused** (prioritise clear control) to **decentralised & collaborative** (prioritise quick decisions). The four canonical types:

| Type | Shape / chain of command | Decision-making | Grouped by | Innovation tendency |
|------|--------------------------|-----------------|------------|---------------------|
| **[[Hierarchical Structure\|Hierarchical]]** | Tall pyramid: CEO → layers of management → base employees | Centralised, top-down | Rank / authority | ⬇ Low — slow, siloed |
| **[[Functional Structure\|Functional]]** | Departments led by functional heads | Centralised within each function | **Expertise** (marketing, R&D, finance…) | ⬇ Low–medium — deep skill but isolated |
| **[[Flat Structure\|Flat]]** | Few or no management layers | Decentralised, fast | Minimal hierarchy | ⬆ High — fast, autonomous |
| **[[Matrix Structure\|Matrix]]** | Dual reporting: functional **and** project manager | Shared / negotiated | Function **×** project | ⬆ High — cross-functional |

### Hierarchical

A traditional **pyramid**: the CEO sits at the top, with descending management layers down to base-level employees. Authority and information flow vertically.

### Functional

Divides the organisation into **departments by function** (e.g. R&D, marketing, operations), each led by a functional head. People are organised by **expertise rather than by decision-making hierarchy** — deep specialists, but each in their own lane.

### Flat

**Management layers are limited or eliminated.** The point is to *increase the pace of decision-making* and *cut the cost* of maintaining many management layers. Common in startups and small innovation teams.

### Matrix

Employees **report vertically to a functional manager and horizontally to a project manager**. This deliberately encourages **cross-functional collaboration** while preserving a chain of command — but it also gives everyone "two bosses."

```
              CEO
               │
   ┌───────────┼───────────┐        ← Functional managers (vertical)
  R&D        Marketing   Finance
   │            │           │
   ├───────────┼───────────┤  Project A  ← Project managers (horizontal)
   └───────────┼───────────┘  Project B
        each cell = one person with TWO bosses
```

## ⚖️ Pros & Cons (and the *reasoning* behind them)

> [!example] Consolidated comparison
>
> | Structure | Pros | Cons |
> |-----------|------|------|
> | **Hierarchical** | Clear chain of command · easy to implement · clear advancement path · time-tested | Slow decisions · limited cross-collaboration · base staff **disconnected from leadership** |
> | **Functional** | Flexible decisions *within* a function · grouped by expertise · easier to measure & develop people | **Internal isolation** (silos) · **less innovation** |
> | **Flat** | Leaner org · faster decisions · higher job satisfaction · **more innovation** | Hard to implement/scale · less guidance & mentoring · unclear advancement |
> | **Matrix** | Interdisciplinary problem-solving · better communication · personal development | **Power struggles** between managers · time-consuming reporting · higher salary/coordination cost |

Why these patterns show up:

- **Hierarchical is slow because every decision climbs the ladder.** The same vertical chain that gives clarity also adds a queue in front of every "yes," and keeps front-line staff (who often *see* the innovation opportunity) far from the people who can fund it.
- **Functional silos suppress innovation because breakthroughs are usually *recombinations across* domains** (e.g. hardware × software × design). Grouping strictly by expertise optimises depth but starves the cross-domain contact that novelty needs.
- **Flat structures innovate because decision rights sit next to the work** — a team can try something today without a sign-off chain. The flip side is thin mentoring and murky career ladders, and it gets fragile as headcount grows.
- **Matrix buys cross-functional collaboration at the price of ambiguity.** Two-boss reporting is exactly what forces R&D and marketing to talk — but it also creates the power struggles and reporting overhead that make matrix organisations tiring to run.

> [!warning] Common exam/essay trap
> Don't say "flat structures are the best for innovation" full stop. The honest answer is a **fit** argument: flat/organic designs *generate* more ideas, but without some hierarchy or matrix discipline they struggle to *commercialise and scale* them. The strongest answers name the trade-off and the environment.

## 🔀 How Structure Supports **or** Constrains Innovation

Slide question: *"In what ways does the design of an innovation structure support or constrain innovation?"* The answer runs through the three things structure controls:

| Structure sets… | Supports innovation when… | Constrains innovation when… |
|-----------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Decision rights** | Pushed down → fast, cheap experiments | Centralised → every idea waits for the top |
| **Information flow** | Cross-boundary → ideas recombine | Siloed → domains never meet |
| **Accountability** | Tolerates smart failure → risk-taking | Punishes any failure → people play safe |

> [!tip] The deeper tension: exploration vs. exploitation
> Innovation ("**exploration**" — search, variation, new bets) and running the core business ("**exploitation**" — efficiency, reliability, scale) reward *opposite* structures. Trying to do both with one structure usually means one crowds out the other. The classic resolution is the ==ambidextrous organisation==: run a protected, flat, organic **innovation unit** alongside the hierarchical, efficient **core** — with senior leadership integrating the two. This is why big firms spin up "labs," "skunkworks," or venture arms rather than asking the main hierarchy to innovate.

## 🎨 Designing the Innovation Structure

Beyond the whole-org chart, firms choose *where* innovation lives. Three archetypes:

| Model | Where innovation sits | Structure combo | Exemplar | Key trade-off |
|-------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|---------------|
| **[[Centralized Innovation Design\|Centralized]]** | Strategy set at the **centre**; resources concentrated in one **innovation department** | Functional | **Apple** | Coherent, focused bets — but distant from local markets |
| **[[Decentralized Innovation Design\|Decentralized]]** | **Each business unit** fully owns its innovation; tasks distributed, open communication & collaboration | Divisional | **Johnson & Johnson** | Close to the customer, many parallel experiments — but duplication & weak corporate coherence |
| **[[Hybrid Innovation Design\|Hybrid]]** | Central **corporate strategy** connected to **each BU's** innovation effort | Functional **+** divisional | **Procter & Gamble** | Balances coherence with local responsiveness — but adds coordination cost |

> [!info] Reading the exemplars
> **Apple → centralized:** a tightly functional org with innovation strategy owned at the top, enabling a small number of coherent, high-conviction product bets. **Johnson & Johnson → decentralized:** many operating companies, each running its own innovation close to its market. **Procter & Gamble → hybrid:** a matrix-style blend that ties each business unit's innovation back to a shared corporate strategy (its "Connect + Develop" open-innovation model is a well-known expression of this). *(The slide's company labels are column-aligned to Centralized/Decentralized/Hybrid respectively.)*

## ✅ Summary

- ==Organisational structure== = the arrangement of **roles, responsibilities, and relationships** that coordinates activity toward goals. It quietly sets **decision rights, information flow, and accountability** — the three things innovation depends on. ^summary-core
- Four canonical types trade **control** against **speed/creativity**: **hierarchical** (clear but slow), **functional** (deep expertise but siloed), **flat** (fast & innovative but hard to scale), **matrix** (cross-functional but two-boss friction).
- Structure **constrains** innovation when decisions are centralised, information is siloed, and failure is punished; it **supports** innovation when the opposite holds.
- There is **no universally best structure** — fit depends on environmental change (mechanistic for stable, organic for turbulent). Big firms reconcile the **exploration vs. exploitation** tension via **ambidextrous** designs.
- Innovation-design archetypes: **centralized** (Apple), **decentralized** (J&J), **hybrid** (P&G) — a spectrum from coherence to local responsiveness.
