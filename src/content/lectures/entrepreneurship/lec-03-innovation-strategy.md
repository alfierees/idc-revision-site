## 🧭 Strategy vs. Business Model

These two terms are constantly confused, so pin the distinction down first.

> [!info] Definitions ^def-strategy-model
> A ==business model== is **how a business produces, offers and captures value** — the mechanics of value creation and value capture.
> A ==business strategy== is **the strategic initiatives a company pursues to create value for the organisation and gain a competitive advantage in the market** (Harvard Business School).

| | Business **Model** | Business **Strategy** |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | *How* do we make, deliver, and get paid for value? | *How* do we build a durable **advantage** over rivals? |
| Time horizon | Operating logic (how the engine runs today) | Directional choices (where we compete & how we win) |
| Focus | Value **creation + capture** mechanics | **Competitive advantage** & positioning |
| Example | Netflix charges a monthly subscription for streamed content | Netflix invests in exclusive original content to lock in subscribers and out-position rivals |

> [!tip] The intuition
> Two firms can share the same **model** (e.g. both run ad-supported social feeds) yet pursue very different **strategies** (one chases scale and network effects, the other chases a premium niche). The model is the machine; the strategy is the *choice of race* and the *plan to win it*. ==Competitive advantage== is the goal that strategy exists to produce.

## 🌊 Blue Ocean vs. Red Ocean Strategy

> [!info] Definitions ^def-oceans
> ==Red Oceans== are **all the industries in existence today — the known market space.** Industry boundaries are defined and accepted, and the competitive rules of the game are known. *(Ex: airline industry, fashion retail.)*
> ==Blue Oceans== are **all the industries not yet in existence — the unknown market space, untainted by competition.** Here demand is *created* rather than fought over. *(Ex: AI technology opens multiple blue oceans.)*

The core contrast is between **fighting over existing demand** and **creating new demand**:

| | 🔴 Red Ocean | 🔵 Blue Ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Market space | Known, existing industries | Unknown, not-yet-created space |
| Boundaries & rules | Defined and accepted | Undefined — you set them |
| Demand | **Fought over** (fixed pie) | **Created** (grow the pie) |
| Competition | Head-to-head; crowded | Irrelevant — no rivals yet |
| Typical outcome | Bloody price/feature wars, thin margins | Uncontested space, high margin (until imitated) |
| Examples | Airlines, fashion retail | New AI-enabled categories |

> [!tip] Why the metaphor works
> In a **red** ocean, competitors "bloody the water" fighting for the same customers, so profits get competed away. In a **blue** ocean the strategist makes competition *irrelevant* by opening a market that did not exist — so for a while there is simply no one to fight. The strategic prize is not out-competing rivals but **removing the need to compete at all**.

> [!warning] Common misconception
> Blue Ocean is **not** "just invent a brand-new technology." It is about creating **uncontested market space** — which can come from a new *combination* of value (e.g. Cirque du Soleil merging circus + theatre) as much as from raw invention. And blue oceans do not stay blue: once demand is proven, imitators arrive and the water reddens, so the advantage is temporary unless defended.

## ⚔️ Porter's Five Forces

> [!info] What it is ^def-five-forces
> **"The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy"** (Michael E. Porter, *HBR* 1979) is one of the most widely used tools for analysing an **industry's competitive environment**. It identifies **five forces** that shape every industry and market; together they determine the **intensity of competition** and hence the **profitability and attractiveness** of an industry.

The key insight: profitability is not just about your own firm — it is set by the *structure of the industry you sit in*. Five forces jointly squeeze (or protect) the profit available to everyone in that industry.

| Force | What it captures | Pressure on profit when force is **high** |
|---|---|---|
| **Threat of New Entrants** | Possibility that new companies enter and add capacity | High → prices/margins competed down; incumbents must keep barriers up |
| **Bargaining Power of Suppliers** | Powerful suppliers can drive up input costs | High → input costs rise, squeezing margins |
| **Bargaining Power of Buyers** | Buyers push for lower prices / better quality & service | High → prices pushed down, service costs pushed up |
| **Threat of Substitutes** | Alternative products/services meeting the same need | High → caps the price you can charge |
| **Rivalry Among Existing Competitors** | Intensity of competition between current players | High → price wars, heavy marketing, thin margins |

> [!tip] How to read the forces
> Each force is a **question about who holds power**. Strong forces drain profit *out* of the industry (toward suppliers, buyers, entrants, or substitutes); weak forces let incumbents *keep* it. An "attractive" industry is one where the five forces are mostly **weak** — high barriers to entry, dependent suppliers, captive buyers, few substitutes, and disciplined rivalry. Strategy is partly about **choosing** favourable industries and partly about **shifting** the forces in your favour (e.g. raising entry barriers, locking in buyers).

> [!warning] Don't confuse the two buyer/supplier forces
> **Suppliers** sit *upstream* (they sell inputs *to* you); **buyers** sit *downstream* (they buy outputs *from* you). Both squeeze your margin, but from opposite ends of the value chain. Mixing them up is the most common Five-Forces exam error.

### Worked example — Fast Food Industry

> [!example] Applying the five forces to fast food
>
> | Force | Rating | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | Threat of New Entrants | **Medium** | Franchising makes entry relatively easy, *but* brand recognition, economies of scale, and real-estate access are significant barriers. |
> | Bargaining Power of Suppliers | **Low** | Large chains buy ingredients in bulk with many supplier options → little supplier leverage. |
> | Bargaining Power of Buyers | **High** | Customers have many choices and switch easily on taste, price, service, or convenience. |
> | Threat of Substitutes | **High** | Home-cooked meals, meal kits, casual dining, food trucks, and delivery (Uber Eats) all compete. |
> | Competitive Rivalry | **Very High** | McDonald's, Burger King, KFC, Wendy's, etc. compete hard on price, speed, location, and menu. |
>
> **Read-out:** thin structural profitability — powerful buyers, easy substitutes, and brutal rivalry mean margins are defended mainly through **scale and brand**, not pricing power.

### Worked example — Streaming Services Industry

> [!example] Applying the five forces to streaming
>
> | Force | Rating | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | Threat of New Entrants | **Medium** | Entry is possible with capital, *but* brand, content libraries, and tech infrastructure are major barriers. |
> | Bargaining Power of Suppliers | **High** | Content creators/studios can demand premium deals — or launch their own platforms. |
> | Bargaining Power of Buyers | **High** | Viewers switch services or cancel subscriptions easily. |
> | Threat of Substitutes | **High** | YouTube, gaming, TikTok, and live TV all compete for screen time & attention. |
> | Competitive Rivalry | **Very High** | Many players compete on content, pricing, and global reach. |
>
> **Read-out:** a structurally *hard* industry — **suppliers** (studios) hold power at the top while **buyers** churn at the bottom, so platforms are squeezed from both ends and must spend heavily on original content to differentiate.

> [!tip] Compare the two examples
> Both industries share **high buyer power, high substitutes, and very high rivalry**. The decisive difference is **supplier power**: *low* in fast food (commodity inputs, many suppliers) but *high* in streaming (scarce, powerful content owners). That single force explains why streaming margins are structurally harder to defend — and why platforms fight to own their content and turn suppliers into employees.

## ✅ Summary

- A ==business model== is *how you create and capture value*; a ==business strategy== is *the initiatives that build competitive advantage*. Same model ≠ same strategy. ^summary-core
- **Blue Ocean vs. Red Ocean:** red oceans *fight over* existing demand in known markets (airlines, retail); blue oceans *create* new, uncontested demand (AI-enabled categories). The strategic prize is making competition **irrelevant** — but blue oceans redden as imitators arrive.
- **Porter's Five Forces** (Porter, 1979) diagnoses industry *attractiveness/profitability* via: threat of new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes, and rivalry. Strong forces drain profit; an attractive industry has mostly **weak** forces.
- Worked cases: **fast food** (low supplier power but high buyer power, substitutes & rivalry) vs. **streaming** (high supplier *and* buyer power — squeezed from both ends). The differentiator is **supplier power**.
- Strategy sits alongside structure, culture, and leadership as the levers of [[Organizational Innovation]]: it chooses *where* the firm competes and *why* an advantage there would hold.
