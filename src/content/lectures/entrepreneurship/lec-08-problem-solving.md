# Problem Solving

> Part of: [[Entrepreneurship]] | Key concepts: [[Componential Model of Creativity]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[Psychological Safety]], [[Progress Principle]], [[Brainstorming]], [[Lateral Thinking]]

---

## 📚 Session Overview

The four previous classes built the *organisational levers* of innovation — structure ([[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]]), strategy ([[Lec_03-Innovation Strategy]]), culture ([[Lec_06-Culture of Innovation]]) and leadership ([[Lec_07-Innovation Leadership]]). Those explain how a firm is *set up* to innovate. This class zooms in one level: **once you have the right conditions, how do individuals and teams actually generate ideas and solve problems?** In other words, it moves from *the innovative organisation* to *the act of creativity itself*.

The class has two halves:

1. **What drives creativity** — Amabile & Pratt's *dynamic componential model*: the mix of skills, processes and motivation, and the enabling conditions (intrinsic motivation, meaningful work, psychological safety, the progress principle).
2. **How to generate ideas on demand** — two classic ideation techniques: **Osborn's Brainstorming** and **De Bono's Lateral Thinking / Six Thinking Hats**.

> [!info] Where this sits in the course
> Structure/strategy/culture/leadership are the *stage*; problem-solving is the *performance*. You can build the perfect innovation ecosystem and still get nothing if the people inside it don't know how to think divergently and aren't motivated to try. This class supplies the missing micro-mechanics — the psychology of the creative individual and the concrete tools a team uses in the room.

## 🎭 Two Ways to Understand Creativity

Before any technique, the lecture draws a distinction that governs everything after it.

> [!info] Process vs. Product ^def-process-product
> - ==Creativity as a *process*== — the **activity of generating ideas and solving problems**. It is a *verb*: the messy, iterative journey from a blank page to a candidate solution.
> - ==Creativity as a *product*== — the **final, tangible outcome**. It is a *noun*: the thing you can point at and evaluate.

| | Creativity as **process** | Creativity as **product** |
|---|---|---|
| **Grammatically** | A verb — an activity | A noun — an outcome |
| **What you manage** | The *conditions* (motivation, safety, techniques) | The *result* (novelty, usefulness) |
| **When it matters** | While ideas are being generated | When ideas are judged/shipped |
| **Class metaphor** | Tim Brennan (Apple): *"From Chaos to Clarity"* | The polished, evaluable output |

> [!tip] Why the distinction matters for a manager
> You cannot directly *command* a creative product ("be brilliant by Friday"). You can only manage the **process** — the inputs and conditions — and trust that a good process raises the odds of a good product. Almost everything else in this class (motivation, psychological safety, brainstorming rules) is about **engineering the process** so that valuable products become more likely. Confusing the two leads managers to demand outcomes while starving the conditions that produce them.

## ⚖️ The Management Tension: Control vs. Freedom

> [!info] The core managerial tension ^def-control-freedom
> Managing creativity means living with a permanent tension between **==control==** (structure, deadlines, accountability, resource discipline) and **==freedom==** (autonomy, experimentation, permission to wander).

> [!warning] Neither extreme works
> - **All control, no freedom** → people execute efficiently but never explore; novelty dies. This is the trap of a [[Hierarchy Culture]].
> - **All freedom, no control** → energy scatters, nothing ships, resources are wasted.
>
> The manager's job is not to pick a side but to *hold the tension* — enough structure to make progress visible and resources available, enough freedom for genuine divergence. This is exactly why the enabling conditions below (psychological safety, meaningful goals) matter: they are how you grant *freedom* without losing *direction*.

## 🧩 Amabile & Pratt — The Dynamic Componential Model

The theoretical backbone of the class.

> [!info] Source
> **Amabile, T. M., & Pratt, M. G. (2016).** *The dynamic componential model of creativity and innovation in organizations: Making progress, making meaning.* **Research in Organizational Behavior, 36, 157–183.**

The central claim: individual creativity is not a single trait but the product of **three components that must all be present and work together**. If any one is missing, creativity collapses — they are multiplicative, not additive.

> [!info] The three individual components ^def-three-components
> 1. ==Domain-Relevant Skills== — *what you know*: technical expertise, knowledge and ability in a specific field. This is the raw material; you cannot innovate in a domain you don't understand.
> 2. ==Creativity-Relevant Processes== — *how you think*: cognitive styles and thinking techniques — generating novel ideas, tolerating ambiguity, taking risks, "thinking outside the box." (These are the *techniques* the second half of the class teaches.)
> 3. ==Task Motivation== — *why you do it*: being driven by genuine interest and enjoyment in the task itself (intrinsic motivation).

> [!tip] Why all three are required — the "AND" logic
> Think of them as a product, not a sum. Deep expertise **without** creative processes gives you a competent but conventional expert. Creative processes **without** domain skills give you novel ideas that don't work. Both **without** motivation give you someone perfectly capable who simply won't bother. Creativity appears only where **skills × processes × motivation** all clear a threshold — which is why the class treats motivation (below) as seriously as talent.

### From the individual to the organisation

The model scales up: the same three-part logic has an **organisational mirror**, and the two levels feed each other.

| Level | Skills / Resources | Process | Motivation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | Domain-relevant skills (expertise) | Creativity-relevant processes (cognitive style, techniques) | Task motivation (intrinsic vs. extrinsic) |
| **Organisational** | Resources in the task domain (time, money, information, expertise) | Innovation-management skills (collaboration, feedback, project management) | Organisational motivation to innovate (leaders set a culture valuing creativity & risk-taking) |

> [!info] The dynamic loops that make the model "dynamic" ^def-progress-loops
> The 2016 model adds *feedback loops* over time — this is the "dynamic" in the name:
> - **Progress loops** — *organisational progress increases motivation to innovate.* Even **partial success enhances creativity**: small wins feed back into willingness to keep trying.
> - **Learning from failure** — failure does **not** necessarily end the creative process. Under **[[Psychological Safety|psychological safety]]**, people re-engage and *learn* from setbacks rather than disengaging.
> - **Meaningful work** — leaders reinforce the *meaningfulness* of tasks by linking them to broader organisational values and goals, which sustains motivation across the loops.

> [!tip] Why leaders are the hinge
> Notice how the organisational row maps onto [[Lec_07-Innovation Leadership]]: leaders **set the tone** (organisational motivation to innovate), **allocate resources**, and **link work to meaning**. The componential model is therefore the mechanism *underneath* the leadership lever — it explains *how* transformational leadership actually produces innovation: by feeding all three components at the organisational level.

## 🔥 Motivation for Creativity: Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic

The single most important lever inside "task motivation."

> [!info] Two sources of motivation ^def-intrinsic-extrinsic
> - ==Intrinsic motivation== — driven by **interest, enjoyment and meaning in the task itself.** You'd do it even without a reward.
> - ==Extrinsic motivation== — driven by **outside factors**: pay, deadlines, evaluation, competition, rewards and punishments.

| | Intrinsic motivation | Extrinsic motivation |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of drive** | The task itself — interest, enjoyment, meaning | Outside the task — pay, rewards, deadlines, evaluation |
| **Effect on creativity** | **Sustains and deepens** it | Can support *or* undermine it |
| **Behaviour it produces** | Persistence, exploration, discretionary effort | Compliance; sometimes narrowing/shortcutting |
| **Best for** | Novel, open-ended, creative work | Routine, well-defined execution |

> [!info] The meaning requirement ^def-meaningful-work
> For **both individuals and organisations, work must be *meaningful* to sustain creativity.** Employees who find their work significant are more likely to be **intrinsically motivated** and to **persist** in creative effort — especially through the setbacks that any real creative project involves.

> [!warning] The classic mistake — bribing creativity
> Managers instinctively reach for **extrinsic** levers (bonuses, tighter deadlines, competitions) to "boost" creativity. But creative, open-ended work runs on **intrinsic** motivation; piling on extrinsic pressure can *crowd it out* — people optimise for the reward or the deadline rather than for the interesting problem, and exploration narrows. The lever that actually sustains creativity is **meaning**, not money. Leaders should first make the work *matter*, then add resources — not substitute rewards for purpose.

## 🛡️ Psychological Safety

The enabling condition that lets the failure-and-learning loop function.

> [!info] Definition — Psychological Safety ^def-psych-safety
> ==Psychological safety== is a climate in which team members feel **safe to take risks, share unconventional ideas, and experiment without fear of ridicule or punishment.**

> [!tip] Why it is the precondition for divergent thinking
> Every creative technique in the second half of this class — especially brainstorming's "defer judgment" and "go for wild ideas" — **requires** that people are willing to say something that might be wrong or strange *out loud*. In a low-safety room, self-censorship kills the wild ideas *before they are spoken*, and the best raw material never enters the process. Psychological safety is therefore not a "nice-to-have" morale factor; it is the **structural precondition** that makes ideation possible at all.

> [!info] Safety reframes failure
> In high-safety environments, **failure does not lead to disengagement.** Instead, individuals and teams **view failures as learning opportunities rather than setbacks** — which is precisely what activates the "learning from failure" loop in the componential model. Low safety breaks that loop: a single punished failure teaches everyone to stop taking risks.

## 📈 The Progress Principle

> [!info] Definition — The Progress Principle ^def-progress-principle
> The ==progress principle== states that **creativity is fuelled by perceived progress in meaningful work.** Concretely:
> - **Small wins boost intrinsic motivation**, which in turn enhances creative engagement.
> - **Setbacks reduce motivation** — *but* can still support creativity **if psychological safety is present** (the setback becomes a lesson, not a stop).

> [!tip] The virtuous cycle — why "small wins" are disproportionately powerful
> The progress principle explains the *dynamics* of motivation over a project's life. It creates a self-reinforcing loop:
> $$\text{meaningful work} \rightarrow \text{small win} \rightarrow \uparrow\text{intrinsic motivation} \rightarrow \uparrow\text{creative engagement} \rightarrow \text{more progress}$$
> The managerial implication is counter-intuitive: rather than chasing one giant breakthrough, **engineer frequent visible small wins.** Each win refuels motivation, which sustains the effort that eventually produces the big result. This is also why psychological safety matters so much — it prevents the *setback* half of the cycle from turning into a downward spiral.

## 💡 Ideation Techniques

The class now turns from *conditions* to *tools*. Given a motivated team in a psychologically safe room, how do you actually generate ideas?

> [!info] Definition — Ideation ^def-ideation
> ==Ideation== is a **relaxed, judgment-free session where team members share ideas freely**, fostering creativity. The two canonical techniques below both operationalise the same principle: *separate idea-generation from idea-evaluation.*

---

### 🧠 Brainstorming (Alex Osborn)

> [!info] Origin ^def-brainstorming
> ==Brainstorming== — part of **CPS (Creative Problem Solving)** — was invented by **Alex Osborn in the 1950s** to help the creative teams at his advertising agency (**BBDO**) generate better ideas. He named the technique "brainstorming."

> [!info] Osborn's four rules ^def-osborn-rules
> 1. **Defer judgment** — no criticism during idea generation. Evaluation comes *later*.
> 2. **Go for quantity, not quality** — more ideas ⇒ higher chance a great one is among them.
> 3. **Go for wild ideas** — the stranger the better; wild ideas can be tamed into workable ones.
> 4. **Build on others' ideas** — "yes, and…": combine and extend rather than compete.

> [!tip] Every rule attacks one specific killer of creativity
> The rules aren't arbitrary etiquette — each disables a known enemy of divergent thinking:
> - **Defer judgment** kills *premature criticism*, the main reason people self-censor. (This is why [[Psychological Safety]] is the precondition — the rule only works if people trust it.)
> - **Quantity over quality** kills *perfectionism*: you can't be precious about idea #47 when the goal is 100.
> - **Wild ideas** kills *functional fixedness* — the tendency to stay inside the obvious solution space.
> - **Build on others** kills *ego/ownership*: ideas become shared property, so combination beats competition.
> Together they enforce the golden rule of ideation: **generate first, judge later.** Mixing the two phases is the single most common way brainstorming fails.

---

### 🎩 Lateral Thinking (Edward de Bono)

> [!info] Who ^def-de-bono
> **Edward de Bono** — a Maltese physician, psychologist, author and philosopher — is best known for coining **lateral thinking** and for his broader work on creative thinking and problem-solving.

> [!info] Definition — Lateral Thinking ^def-lateral-thinking
> ==Lateral thinking== aims to **break away from traditional, linear ("vertical") thinking patterns** and to explore **new and non-obvious ways** of approaching a problem. Where linear thinking digs the *same hole deeper*, lateral thinking asks *whether to dig somewhere else entirely.*

> [!tip] Lateral vs. vertical thinking
> Vertical (logical) thinking moves **step-by-step from the current idea**, each step justified by the last — excellent for *refining* a solution, useless for *escaping* a bad frame. Lateral thinking deliberately makes *discontinuous* jumps — provocations, reframes, random entry points — to reach ideas that no logical chain from the starting point could have produced. The two are complementary: lateral thinking to *find* a new frame, vertical thinking to *develop* it.

#### The Six Thinking Hats

De Bono's most famous tool: a structured way to look at a problem from six distinct angles **one at a time**, so a group thinks in *parallel* rather than arguing across purposes.

> [!info] The six hats ^def-six-hats
> | Hat | Mode | What the wearer does |
> |---|---|---|
> | ⚪ **White** | Facts & information | Gather **cold, hard facts** and data for evidence-based decisions — the "detective." |
> | 🔴 **Red** | Emotions & feelings | Voice **gut instincts and emotions**, with *no justification required*. |
> | ⚫ **Black** | Critical thinking | Play the **skeptic**: point out risks, weaknesses, what could go wrong. |
> | 🟡 **Yellow** | Positive thinking | Focus on **benefits, value and possibilities** — the optimist. |
> | 🟢 **Green** | Creative thinking | Generate **new ideas, alternatives and solutions** — the imagination zone. |
> | 🔵 **Blue** | Meta-thinking | **Manage the process**: set the agenda, sequence the hats, keep the group on track — the "conductor." |

> [!tip] Why wearing *one hat at a time, together* is the whole trick
> In a normal meeting everyone wears **all hats at once**: one person is excited (yellow) while another attacks risks (black) while a third is emotional (red) — so they talk *past* each other and it feels like conflict. The Six Hats forces the **whole group into the same mode simultaneously**: everyone does facts (white), *then* everyone does risks (black), etc. This does two things: it **separates generation from evaluation** (like Osborn's rules), and it lets even naturally critical people contribute *green* creative ideas because the hat gives them permission. The **Blue hat** is meta — it decides the order and running of the session, which is why it's the "conductor."

> [!warning] Common misuse
> The Six Hats fails when people treat a hat as a *personality label* ("you're the black-hat pessimist"). The point is that **everyone wears every hat** — the hats are *modes of thinking the group cycles through*, not roles assigned to individuals. Assigning hats permanently just recreates the cross-purposes argument the method exists to prevent.

## ✅ Summary

- Creativity has **two faces**: a **process** (generating ideas — the verb) and a **product** (the outcome — the noun). Managers can only steer the *process*; good products follow from good conditions. ^summary-core
- Managing creativity means holding the **control ⇄ freedom** tension — enough structure to make progress and allocate resources, enough autonomy for genuine divergence.
- **Amabile & Pratt's dynamic componential model**: individual creativity = **domain skills × creativity-relevant processes × task motivation** (all three required), mirrored at the organisational level (resources × innovation-management skills × motivation to innovate), linked by **dynamic loops** — progress loops, learning from failure, and meaningful work.
- **Intrinsic motivation** (interest, enjoyment, *meaning*) sustains creativity; **extrinsic** rewards can crowd it out. Make work *meaningful* before adding incentives.
- **[[Psychological Safety]]** is the precondition for divergent thinking and for treating failure as learning; the **[[Progress Principle]]** shows that **small wins** compound intrinsic motivation into a virtuous cycle.
- Two ideation tools operationalise *"generate first, judge later"*: **Osborn's Brainstorming** (defer judgment · quantity · wild ideas · build on others) and **de Bono's Lateral Thinking**, whose **Six Thinking Hats** (white/red/black/yellow/green/blue) make a group think in *parallel* modes one at a time.
