# Practical Innovation 2 — Design Thinking (Session Two)

> Part of: [[Entrepreneurship]] | Key concepts: [[User Interviews]], [[Empathy Map]], [[Persona]], [[Customer Journey Map]], [[Touchpoint]], [[Design Question]], [[Double Diamond]]

---

## 📚 Session Overview

[[Lec_09-Practical Innovation 1]] introduced the **method** ([[Design Thinking]]) and the **map** ([[Double Diamond]]), and named the two first-diamond tools (Empathy Map, Persona). This session is the **workshop**: it shows how to actually *run* the first diamond, end to end, on a live problem. The pipeline practised in class:

$$\boxed{\text{Interviews} \;\to\; \text{Empathy Map} \;\to\; \text{Persona Map} \;\to\; \text{Customer Journey Map} \;\to\; \text{Design Question}}$$

> [!info] What this session covers
> 1. Recap of the **Double Diamond** — where today's work sits (still the *first* diamond: Discover → Define).
> 2. **Starting the design journey with questions** — who are you designing for, and what is the design problem?
> 3. **User interviews** — how to gather inspiration by *listening* (worked framing: homeless people in London).
> 4. The **three analysis tools** that turn raw interviews into structured insight: ==Empathy Map==, ==Persona Map==, ==Customer Journey Map== (and the idea of a ==touchpoint==).
> 5. The in-class exercise: an **AI-simulated interview** (ChatGPT role-plays a bus-commuting Reichman student).
> 6. The output of the whole first diamond: a sharp, well-structured ==Design Question== ("How might we…").
> 7. Team-assignment briefing — frame your design question *before* brainstorming, and tell your project as a Double Diamond story.

> [!tip] The one idea to leave with
> **Tools are only as good as the listening that feeds them.** The empathy map, persona, and journey map are *analysis* devices — they organise what users told you; they cannot replace it. Everything downstream (including the design question you'll brainstorm on) inherits the quality of your interviews. Garbage in → beautifully formatted garbage out.

## 🔷🔷 Recap: Where We Are on the Double Diamond

> [!info] The Double Diamond (recap) ^def-double-diamond-recap
> The ==Double Diamond== (UK Design Council) models a design project as two rounds of **diverge-then-converge**:
> - **Discover** — understand and explore the question *(diverge on the problem)*
> - **Define** — narrow down ideas and choose one problem *(converge on the problem)*
> - **Develop** — ideate and prototype, test and fix *(diverge on solutions)*
> - **Deliver** — refine the selected solution *(converge on solutions)*
>
> It exists to force **wide exploration before narrowing**, so the final design actually addresses the *identified* problem — not the first problem you assumed.

This entire session lives in the **first diamond**. Interviews and the three maps are *Discover*; the design question is the *Define* checkpoint — the narrow point between the diamonds. Only after it may you brainstorm (Develop, next session).

## ❓ Begin With Questions, Not Answers

Every design journey starts from two questions — *before* any solution is allowed in the room:

> [!info] The two opening questions ^def-opening-questions
> 1. **WHO are you designing for?** — identify the user whose life you're trying to improve.
> 2. **WHAT is the design problem?** — what, specifically, about their experience needs improving?

> [!tip] Why this ordering is the whole discipline
> Notice that both questions are about the *user and the problem* — neither asks "what should we build?" This is [[Lec_09-Practical Innovation 1]]'s "fall in love with the problem" made operational: the session's worked example is framed as *"How can we improve the life of homeless people in London?"* — a **who** + a **what**, with zero solution content. If your starting question already contains a product ("how do we build an app for…"), you've skipped the first diamond and smuggled a solution in as a "problem."

## 🎤 Discover, Step 1: User Interviews

> [!info] Interviews — purpose ^def-user-interviews
> Discovery **starts with listening** — gathering inspiration from your customers through ==user interviews==. The **primary goal is to gain a deep understanding of the users' experiences and needs** — not to validate your idea, and not (yet) to collect feature requests.

> [!info] How to conduct the interviews ^def-interview-method
> 1. **Determine what information you need and why** — go in with a learning goal, not a script to confirm.
> 2. **Identify representative interviewees** — people who represent the end-users: current customers, potential users, or stakeholders.
> 3. **Develop open-ended questions** that encourage detailed responses (no yes/no dead ends).
> 4. **Record (with permission) or take detailed notes** — so no information is lost between the interview and the analysis.

> [!warning] The classic interview mistakes
> - **Closed questions** ("Do you like the bus?") produce one-word answers and zero insight; open questions ("Walk me through your morning commute") produce stories, and stories contain needs.
> - **Pitching instead of listening** — the moment you describe your idea, the interviewee starts reacting to *you* instead of describing *their life*.
> - **Not recording** — memory keeps the quotes that flatter your assumptions and drops the ones that would have corrected them.

> [!example] Simulated interviews with ChatGPT (in-class exercise)
> The class problem: *"How might we improve the experience of students commuting to Reichman University by bus?"* Since you can't interview a bus stop mid-class, ChatGPT **role-plays the user**:
>
> *"Hi, I'd like you to role-play as a student who commutes every day by bus to Reichman University in Herzliya. Please answer my questions honestly and in detail, based on what this kind of student might experience."*
>
> Then ask **at least 5 open-ended questions**, e.g.: What does your typical morning commute look like? · What are the most frustrating parts? · How do you feel when you arrive on campus? · Have you ever missed a class because of the bus? · What would make your commute easier or more enjoyable?
>
> The transcript then feeds the empathy map, persona, and journey map exactly as a real interview would. (Note the questions target *experiences and emotions* — routines, frustrations, feelings — not feature preferences.)

## 🗺️ Discover, Step 2: The Three Analysis Tools

After the interviews, three tools **analyse and present the findings**. They answer three different questions about the same user:

| Tool | Question it answers | Output | Grain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[[Empathy Map]]** | What is going on in the user's *head*? | Say / Think / Feel / Do quadrants | One user's mindset |
| **[[Persona]] (Persona Map)** | *Who* are we designing for? | A named, fictional-but-realistic profile | A whole segment, compressed |
| **[[Customer Journey Map]]** | What does the experience look like *over time*? | Stages × actions × emotions × touchpoints | The end-to-end experience |

> [!tip] Why three tools and not one — they slice the same data on different axes
> The empathy map is a **snapshot of a mind**; the persona is a **portrait of a segment**; the journey map is a **film of the experience**. Mindset, identity, and time. Skipping one leaves a blind spot: without the journey map you know *who* the user is but not *where* in their day the pain actually strikes — and it's the journey map that tells you **where to intervene**.

### 1 — Empathy Map (from Class 9, now applied)

> [!info] Empathy Map ^def-empathy-map-applied
> Used **to understand the user's mindset** by categorising what they **say, think, feel, and do** from the interview transcript. The gaps between quadrants (says ≠ thinks, does ≠ feels) are where hidden needs live — see [[Lec_09-Practical Innovation 1]] for the full logic.

### 2 — Persona Map

> [!info] Persona Map ^def-persona-map
> **Create fictional characters, based on the data gathered from the empathy map, to represent a user segment.** The persona is built *from* the interview evidence — not from imagination.

> [!example] The persona fields (in-class template) ^ex-persona-fields
> Using the ChatGPT interview answers, fill in:
>
> | Field | What goes there |
> |---|---|
> | **Name** | Choose a name (e.g., Noa, Amir, Sarah) |
> | **Age & Background** | How old? Where do they live? What do they study? |
> | **Goals** | Why are they commuting to Reichman? What do they want from their student experience? |
> | **Frustrations** | Challenges / pain points during the commute |
> | **Needs** | What would make the commute better, easier, more enjoyable? |
> | **Motivations** | Why do they do what they do? What matters most to them? |
> | **Typical Quote** | A powerful or emotional quote *from the interview* that sums up their mindset |
>
> The **quote field** is the discipline check: it must come from the interview. If no real quote fits your persona, the persona is fiction in the bad sense — you've drawn your assumptions, not your data.

### 3 — Customer Journey Map

> [!info] Definition — Customer Journey Map ^def-customer-journey-map
> A ==Customer Journey Map== is **a visual representation of the steps, interactions, and emotions a customer experiences while engaging with a product, service, or brand across different touchpoints** — before, during, and after the engagement.

> [!info] Definition — Touchpoint ^def-touchpoint
> A ==touchpoint== is **any moment of interaction between a customer and a brand, product, or service** — online, in person, or through communication channels — **before, during, or after a purchase.**

> [!example] Building the journey map from the interview ^ex-journey-map
> For the bus-commute case:
> 1. **Mine the transcript** for: moments of frustration or confusion · routines, habits, tools used (e.g., Moovit) · emotions throughout the journey · opportunities to improve.
> 2. **Identify the key stages** (use judgment based on the interview): *waking up → getting ready → checking the bus schedule → walking to the stop → waiting → riding → arriving on campus…*
> 3. **Mark ONE key moment** where improving the experience would make a big impact.

> [!tip] Why you mark *one* moment — the journey map is a targeting device
> A journey map that ends with "everything could be better" has converged on nothing. Forcing a single highest-impact moment is the **convergent** move (Define!) hiding inside a Discover tool: the emotional low point of the journey is usually where a design intervention pays most, and that chosen moment is what your design question will be aimed at.

> [!warning] Map the *user's* journey, not your intended product flow
> The journey starts at "waking up" — long before any product appears. Mapping only the moments that touch *your* (future) solution re-imports solution-first thinking. Pain that occurs *around* the product experience (the anxious wait, the missed class) is often the real design opportunity.

## 🎯 Define: The Design Question

Once insights are placed on the three maps, the final step of the first diamond is to **sharpen and refine your design question** — the single sentence the whole team will brainstorm against.

> [!info] What a design question is ^def-design-question
> A ==design question== frames the chosen problem as an open, optimistic challenge — classically phrased **"How might we…?"** (HMW). A **well-structured design question ensures that solutions are both relevant and impactful**, because it fixes *who* you're designing for and *what* they need, while leaving *how* completely open.

### The three ingredients

> [!info] Structure of a good design question ^def-dq-structure
> 1. **Customer or User** — *who* the question is for.
> 2. **Need or Desire** — *what* the user needs or desires.
> 3. **Problem or Challenge** — the problem the user faces.

### Optional templates

> [!example] Question templates ^ex-dq-templates
> - **How might we [action] for [user] to [outcome]?**
>   → *"How might we create a more engaging campus experience for students to enhance their learning and social interactions?"*
> - **How could we [action] to help [user] who [problem]?**
>   → *"How could we develop an online platform to help students who struggle with finding study resources?"*

> [!example] Worked examples from the lecture ^ex-dq-examples
> - *"How might we improve the commuting experience for students who travel to Reichman University by bus, so that it feels more reliable, comfortable, and stress-free?"* (the class case, fully framed: user + need + outcome)
> - *"How might we improve the campus dining experience for students who have dietary restrictions?"*
> - *"In what ways can we make public transportation more accessible and user-friendly for people with disabilities in our city?"*
> - *"How can we enhance the online learning experience for students who struggle with maintaining focus during virtual classes?"*
> - Healthcare: *"How can we develop a wearable device that reminds elderly patients to take their medication on time?"*
> - Retail: *"What digital tools can we create to help store managers keep track of products and know when to restock items?"*

> [!warning] The Goldilocks problem — scope your question carefully
> The question must be **broad enough to invite many ideas** but **narrow enough to be actionable**. Compare: "How might we fix public transport?" (too broad — no team can brainstorm that) vs. "How might we add a bench at stop 42?" (too narrow — the solution is already inside the question). The class example threads the needle: a specific user (bus-commuting Reichman students), a specific need (reliable, comfortable, stress-free), no prescribed solution. Note also that some lecture examples (the wearable, the digital tools) already lean toward a solution *category* — acceptable in industry practice, but the purer HMW form keeps the solution space fully open.

## 📣 Team Assignment Briefing

> [!info] The rule ^def-assignment-rule
> For the final team assignment you are **required to frame your design question *before* brainstorming solutions** — the first diamond must close before the second opens. **For next class: begin the FIRST STAGE of the team assignment (first diamond only!).**

> [!example] Presentation tips — "Our Design Innovation Story" (recorded final team presentation)
> - **Describe every step of your design process** — the presentation should read as a logical flow, not a product pitch.
> - **Incorporate short citations from your interviews** to justify your ideas — evidence adds credibility to design choices.
> - **Insert the provided Moodle canvases** (GTM, Empathy Map, Persona, Customer Journey) with digital post-it notes, as practised in class.
> - **Use the Double Diamond as the story framework** — it gives the narrative its structure.
> - **Keep slides simple and visual** — avoid walls of text.

> [!tip] Why "cite your interviews" is the grading criterion that matters
> The instruction to include interview quotes is the assignment-scale version of the persona's *Typical Quote* field: it makes your team **show the evidence chain** — user said X → map shows Y → question asks Z → idea does W. A presentation that can't cite an interview for its idea is confessing the idea didn't come from the user.

## ✅ Summary

- Session Two of the practical-innovation block runs the **first diamond in practice**: interviews → empathy map → persona map → customer journey map → **design question**. ^summary-core
- Every design journey opens with two questions — **who are you designing for?** and **what is the design problem?** — never with a solution.
- **User interviews** are the raw material of Discover: define your learning goal, pick representative users, ask **open-ended** questions, and record everything. In class, ChatGPT role-plays the user (a bus-commuting Reichman student) with 5+ open questions.
- Three tools analyse the interviews on three axes: the **Empathy Map** (mindset: say/think/feel/do), the **Persona Map** (segment identity: name, background, goals, frustrations, needs, motivations, quote), and the **Customer Journey Map** (experience over time: stages, emotions, **touchpoints** — mark *one* high-impact moment).
- The first diamond closes with a **design question** ("How might we…") built from three ingredients — **user + need + problem** — broad enough to invite ideas, narrow enough to act on.
- Team assignment: frame the design question **before** brainstorming; present the project as a Double Diamond story with interview citations and the four Moodle canvases.
