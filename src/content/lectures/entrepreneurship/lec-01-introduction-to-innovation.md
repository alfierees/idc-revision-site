## 🔑 Key Terms and Concepts

### Entrepreneurship and intrapreneurship

> [!info] Definition: Entrepreneurship
> **Entrepreneurship** is the process by which individuals, either on their own or in organizations, pursue opportunities **without regard to the resources they currently control**. (Stevenson, HBS)

The important phrase is the last one. An entrepreneur starts from the **opportunity** and then works out how to get the resources, rather than limiting their ambition to what they already have. The definition also says "or in organizations", so entrepreneurship is not limited to founders.

> [!info] Definition: Intrapreneurship
> **[[Intrapreneurship]]** is acting like an entrepreneur **within an established company**. It is creating a new business or venture within an organization. (MIT Sloan)

| | Entrepreneurship | Intrapreneurship |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Individuals on their own or in organizations | Inside an established company |
| **Core behaviour** | Pursuing opportunity beyond current resources | Acting like an entrepreneur from within |
| **Typical output** | A new venture | A new business or venture inside the organization |

### Creativity

> [!info] Definition: Creativity
> **[[Creativity]]** is the production of **novel and useful** ideas. (Sternberg & Lubart 2007; Csikszentmihalyi 2009)

Both conditions must hold:

- **Novelty**: the idea must be original or new **within its context**.
- **Usefulness**: the idea should be practical or valuable in solving a problem or fulfilling a purpose.

> [!tip] Why "within its context" matters
> An idea does not have to be new to the world to count as novel. It only has to be new where it is being applied. An original idea that solves nothing fails the usefulness test.

### Innovation and invention

> [!info] Definition: Innovation
> **[[Innovation]]** is the **successful implementation** of creative ideas within an organization. (Amabile et al. 1996)

> [!info] Definition: Invention
> Innovation is not the same as **[[Invention]]**. It is perfectly possible to come up with an invention that no one ever actually uses or gains benefit from. (Wilson 2016)

| Concept | The question it answers | Complete when… |
|---------|-------------------------|----------------|
| **Creativity** | Is there a novel, useful idea? | The idea exists |
| **Invention** | Has something new been created? | The thing exists, even if nobody uses it |
| **Innovation** | Has the idea been successfully implemented? | It is implemented and delivers benefit |

> [!warning] Common exam trap
> Do not use creativity, invention and innovation as synonyms. **Creativity produces the idea, and innovation implements it successfully.** An invention nobody uses is not an innovation.

> [!example] Class activities
> - Think of a real-world example of **creativity** and of **innovation**, then note their similarities.
> - In pairs, brainstorm a fun, innovative solution to one everyday problem: misplacing keys or a wallet, forgetting passwords, long grocery lines, food wastage, difficulty waking up in the morning, or keeping plants alive.

## 🏢 What Is an Organization?

> [!info] Definition: Organization
> An **[[Organization]]** is a collection of people involved in pursuing defined objectives. It encompasses the **division of work** among employees and the **alignment of tasks** towards the ultimate goal of the company. (Business Jargons)

The definition has three parts: **people**, **shared objectives**, and **coordinated work**. The third part is the subject of [[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]].

## 🚀 From Startup to Established Organization

The slide frames why organization matters as a shift **from flexibility and efficiency to scalability and coordination**. Each stage adds people and roles:

| Stage | What the organization looks like |
|-------|----------------------------------|
| **Founding** | 1–2 people in the garage |
| **Seed round** | An agile team: product, engineering and design |
| **Series A** | Minimal viable product: more team leaders to scale the product |
| **Series B & C** | Growth and revenue: hiring senior people to lead functions, plus a CFO |
| **Exit (IPO, acquisition)** | Raising equity, moving from private to public: a Chief Communications Officer |

```
Founding → Seed → Series A → Series B & C → Exit
flexibility and efficiency ───► scalability and coordination
```

> [!tip] Reading the progression
> Early on, a few people can coordinate informally. As headcount grows, that stops working, so the company adds team leaders, then functional leaders, then public-facing executives. The structure that makes a company scalable can also reduce the flexibility it started with, which is why later classes ask how established firms stay innovative.

## 🧭 The Four Types of Innovation

The [[Four Types of Innovation]] framework (diagram credited to Jeroen Kraaijenbrink) sorts innovation along two axes:

- **Inward–outward**: innovations targeting the **market** (outward) versus those aimed at the **organization itself** (inward).
- **Operational–strategic**: from **practical, tangible** innovations (operational) to **overarching, value-creating** ones (strategic).

| | **Operational** | **Strategic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Outward** (market) | **[[Product Innovation]]** | **[[Business Model Innovation]]** |
| **Inward** (organization) | **[[Process Innovation]]** | **[[Management Innovation]]** |

| Type | What it changes | Notes from the slide |
|------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **Product** | The organization's products and/or services | The most well-known type: change, improve or renew existing offerings, or create new ones |
| **Process** | The way the organization works day to day | Often efficiency- and quality-driven. Can concern any type of process |
| **Business model** | How the organization creates and captures value | A newer type, often focused on developing new revenue models |
| **Management** | How the organization is organized, managed and led | Less commonly known but critical. Often implies decentralization |

> [!tip] How to classify a scenario
> Ask two questions. **Who is it aimed at**, the market or the organization itself? **What level does it change**, a tangible offering or way of working, or the overall logic of value and management?

> [!example] Class exercise: identify the type
> The slide lists six scenarios and **does not give the answers**. The classifications below are suggested by applying the two axes, so check them against what was said in class.
>
> | Scenario | Suggested type | Reasoning |
> |----------|----------------|-----------|
> | Health-tracking smartwatch | Product | A new offering for the market |
> | AI inventory system | Process | Improves day-to-day internal operations |
> | Software subscription service | Business model | Changes how value is captured |
> | Mobile banking app | Product (arguably business model) | A new customer-facing service, which may also change how the bank delivers value |
> | Eco food packaging | Product (arguably process) | Changes the offering customers receive, and may also change production |
> | Empowered front-line workers | Management | Changes how people are managed, through decentralization |

## ✅ Summary

- **Entrepreneurship** is pursuing opportunity without regard to the resources currently controlled. **Intrapreneurship** is doing so inside an established company.
- **Creativity** is producing **novel and useful** ideas. **Innovation** is their **successful implementation**. An **invention** can exist without anyone using it.
- An **organization** is a collection of people pursuing defined objectives through divided and aligned work.
- Startups move from **flexibility and efficiency** to **scalability and coordination** as they pass from founding through funding rounds to exit.
- The four types of innovation are **product** (outward, operational), **business model** (outward, strategic), **process** (inward, operational) and **management** (inward, strategic).
