## 📚 Session Overview

The practical-innovation block runs the **Double Diamond** end to end. [[Lec_09-Practical Innovation 1]] introduced the method and the map; [[Lec_10-Practical Innovation 2]] ran the **first diamond** (Discover → Define) in practice, closing on a sharp ==Design Question==. This session picks up exactly where that left off and runs the **second diamond** — **Develop & Deliver** — where a chosen problem is turned into a working, tested solution.

$$\boxed{\text{Design Question} \;\to\; \text{Prototype} \;\to\; \text{Test with users} \;\to\; \text{Iterate} \;\to\; \text{Validated product}}$$

> [!info] What this session covers
> 1. **Where we are on the Double Diamond** — this session is the *second* diamond: ==Develop== (ideate, prototype, test, fix) and ==Deliver== (refine the selected solution).
> 2. **Rapid Prototyping** — building the *simplest, cheapest, fastest* model of a solution so it can be tested and improved.
> 3. **Prototyping principles and objectives** — a prototype is a **tool for thinking**, not a finished product.
> 4. The **Plan → Build → Test** loop — the three-step engine of every prototype cycle.
> 5. **Types of prototypes** and the idea of ==fidelity== — wireframes, mockups, and interactive clickable prototypes.
> 6. **User testing & iteration** — test with (at least two) real users and use their feedback to fix the solution.
> 7. **Team-assignment updates** — three maps now required (Empathy, Persona, GTM Canvas; Customer Journey dropped), a Canva prototyping activity, and how to tell the final **"Design Innovation Story."**

> [!tip] The one idea to leave with
> **Build to learn, not to impress.** Rapid prototyping deliberately trades polish for speed: the whole point of the second diamond is to *discover flaws while they are still cheap to fix*. A rough prototype in front of a real user this week beats a beautiful product nobody has tested next month — because the user's reaction, not the team's confidence, is what tells you whether the idea works.

## 🔷🔷 Where We Are: The Second Diamond

> [!info] The Double Diamond (recap) ^def-double-diamond-recap
> The ==Double Diamond== (UK Design Council) models a design project as two rounds of **diverge-then-converge**:
> - **Discover** — understand and explore the question *(diverge on the problem)*
> - **Define** — narrow down ideas and choose one solution *(converge on the problem)*
> - **Develop** — ideate and prototype, test and fix *(diverge on solutions)* ← **this session**
> - **Deliver** — refine the selected solution *(converge on solutions)* ← **this session**
>
> Sessions One and Two lived in the **first diamond**; this session is the **second diamond**. The [[Design Question]] framed in [[Lec_10-Practical Innovation 2]] is the narrow point between the two diamonds — the input this session builds on.

> [!tip] Why Develop is *divergent* even though you already chose a problem
> A common misread is that once the design question is fixed, the work becomes narrow execution. Not yet. **Develop diverges again** — this time on *solutions*: you generate many possible answers to the design question and prototype several before converging. **Deliver** is the convergent close, refining the one solution that survived testing. Divergence then convergence, a second time — same shape as the first diamond, different subject (solutions, not problems).

## ⚡ Rapid Prototyping

> [!info] Definition — Prototyping ^def-prototyping
> ==Prototyping== is **the process of creating an early model or sample of a product to test and evaluate its concepts, design, functionality, and usability.** It exists to make an idea *tangible* so it can be reacted to, rather than merely discussed.

> [!info] Definition — Rapid Prototyping ^def-rapid-prototyping
> ==Rapid prototyping== emphasises creating the **simplest, least costly, and quickest** prototypes, in order to iterate and gather feedback efficiently. The three watchwords:
> $$\text{SHORTEST TIME} \quad+\quad \text{LEAST COST} \quad+\quad \text{SIMPLEST MODEL}$$

> [!tip] Why "simplest, cheapest, fastest" is the entire discipline
> The three constraints aren't a compromise forced by limited resources — they are the *method*. Each one protects the same thing: your willingness to throw the prototype away. A prototype that took months and a large budget becomes something you defend; a prototype you sketched in an afternoon is something you happily kill when the user reveals it's wrong. **Cheapness buys honesty.** The less you've invested, the more truthfully you can hear "this doesn't work."

## 🎯 Prototyping: Principles & Objectives

> [!info] Objectives of prototyping ^def-prototyping-objectives
> 1. **Quickly test ideas** to see if they meet user needs.
> 2. **Identify flaws and opportunities for improvement early** in the design process — while they are cheap to fix.
> 3. **Gather feedback from users** to refine and improve the prototype.

> [!info] Key principles ^def-prototyping-principles
> 1. **Prototypes are tools for thinking and understanding — not final products.**
> 2. **Quickly identify what works and what doesn't** without significant investment.
> 3. **Keep the end-user in focus**, ensuring the prototype addresses their needs and challenges.

> [!warning] The trap: falling in love with the prototype
> If a prototype is treated as a *draft product* rather than a *thinking tool*, the team starts polishing it and defending it instead of interrogating it. Early flaws then get discovered late — exactly when they are most expensive. The principle "prototypes are tools for thinking, not final products" is there precisely to prevent this. Ask of every prototype: *"What did we learn?"* — not *"Isn't it good?"*

> [!tip] "Early and cheap" is a claim about the cost curve
> The cost of fixing a design flaw rises sharply the later it's found — a wrong assumption caught on a paper sketch costs minutes; the same assumption caught after the product ships costs a rebuild. Prototyping's objective ("identify flaws early") is really a bet on this curve: spend a little now, testing rough models, to avoid spending a lot later, reworking a finished one.

## 🔁 The Prototyping Loop: Plan → Build → Test

Every prototype cycle runs the same three steps, then loops back on the feedback.

> [!info] Plan ^def-step-plan
> - **Determine what you aim to learn or test** with the prototype — go in with a specific question.
> - **Choose the most critical aspects of your idea** to prototype (you can't and shouldn't prototype everything).

> [!info] Build ^def-step-build
> - **Use basic materials** (mostly digital tools) to create **low-fidelity** prototypes.
> - **Ensure the prototype demonstrates the key functionality or concept**, even if it's not fully polished.

> [!info] Test ^def-step-test
> - **Test the prototype with real users or stakeholders** to gather insights.
> - **Based on feedback, refine the prototype** — then loop.

| Step | Guiding question | Output | Failure mode to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plan** | *What do we need to learn?* | A single learning goal + the critical feature to test | Prototyping everything at once (learn nothing clearly) |
| **Build** | *What's the cheapest thing that shows this?* | A low-fidelity model of the key concept | Over-polishing before testing |
| **Test** | *What did the user actually do and feel?* | Feedback + a list of fixes | Pitching to the user instead of observing them |

> [!tip] Why Plan comes first — a prototype without a question teaches nothing
> The instinct is to jump to Build. But a prototype built without a learning goal produces feedback you can't act on ("they seemed to like it"). Planning what you want to *learn* turns testing into an experiment: you know in advance what a pass or fail looks like. The **Plan step is the difference between a demo and an experiment** — a demo shows off, an experiment resolves an uncertainty.

> [!example] The loop in practice (bus-commute case)
> Continuing [[Lec_10-Practical Innovation 2]]'s design question about improving the Reichman bus commute:
> - **Plan** — *Learning goal:* will students actually use a live "seats-available + arrival" screen at the stop? Critical feature: the real-time display.
> - **Build** — a clickable Figma mockup of the screen (no real data feed; buttons just simulate states). Low-fidelity, an afternoon's work.
> - **Test** — two commuting students tap through it; note where they hesitate, what they expect that isn't there, how they *feel* about it.
> - **Iterate** — feedback ("I care more about *when* the next bus leaves than how full it is") reshapes the next build.

## 🎚️ Types of Prototypes & Fidelity

Prototypes differ in ==fidelity== — how closely the prototype resembles and behaves like the finished product. Low fidelity = rough, fast, cheap; high fidelity = detailed, interactive, closer to real.

> [!info] The three types (low → high fidelity) ^def-prototype-types
> 1. **Wireframes** — *digital sketches of the user-interface layout* without detailed design elements. The skeleton: boxes and structure, no styling.
> 2. **Mockups** — *static representations of the product with more detail than wireframes* but **without interactive functionality**. The look, but it doesn't respond.
> 3. **Interactive prototypes** — *digital prototypes with clickable elements that simulate the user experience*, built with tools like **Figma, InVision, or Adobe XD**. The feel: the user can actually click through a simulated flow.

| Type | Fidelity | Has visual detail? | Is it interactive? | Best for testing… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wireframe** | Low | No (skeleton only) | No | Layout, information hierarchy, flow structure |
| **Mockup** | Medium | Yes (looks real) | No | Visual design, look-and-feel, branding |
| **Interactive prototype** | High | Yes | Yes (clickable) | Actual user experience, navigation, usability |

> [!tip] Why start low-fidelity and climb — match fidelity to the question
> Fidelity should follow your *learning goal*, not your ambition. Testing whether the *flow* makes sense? A wireframe is enough — and its very roughness invites honest criticism (users happily redesign a sketch, but hesitate to criticise something that looks finished). Testing whether the *interaction* feels smooth? You need a clickable prototype. Building a high-fidelity prototype to answer a low-fidelity question wastes the time and money that rapid prototyping exists to save.

> [!warning] Don't confuse a mockup with an interactive prototype
> A **mockup looks** like the product; an **interactive prototype behaves** like it. A mockup can validate *appearance* but tells you nothing about whether users can *navigate* — for that you need clickable elements. Testing usability on a static mockup gives false confidence: it looked right, but nobody could actually use it.

## 🧪 Test & Iterate

> [!info] The testing rule ^def-testing-rule
> **Test the prototype with (at least) two real users, and use their feedback to refine and fix your solution.** Testing is not a formality at the end — it is the mechanism by which the prototype earns the right to become a product.

> [!info] Definition — Iteration ^def-iteration
> ==Iteration== is the repeated **test → learn → refine** cycle: each round of user feedback reshapes the prototype, which is then tested again. The Double Diamond's Develop phase is explicitly "ideate and prototype, **test and fix**" — the *fix* is the iteration.

> [!info] Definition — Product Validation ^def-product-validation
> ==Product validation== is confirming, through user testing, that the refined solution **actually meets the user's needs** — the goal of the Deliver phase (refine the selected solution). Validation comes *from users*, not from the team's opinion.

> [!warning] Two real users, watched — not surveyed
> "Test with two real users" means *observe them using it*, not ask them if they like the idea. Users are poor at predicting their own behaviour ("yes, I'd use that") but excellent at revealing it when they hesitate, misclick, or look for a feature that isn't there. Watch what they **do**, not just what they **say** — the same listening discipline from [[Lec_10-Practical Innovation 2]]'s interviews, now applied to behaviour instead of words.

> [!tip] Why iteration is where the design actually happens
> The first prototype is almost never the answer — it's a *question* posed to users. The real design emerges across iterations, as each test corrects an assumption the team couldn't see from the inside. A team that tests once and ships has skipped the part of the second diamond that does the work: **the loop, not the first build, is the method.**

## 🎨 In-Class Activity & Team Assignment

> [!example] Class activity — design a prototype in Canva
> - **Design a prototype** using **Canva** for your team's assignment product or service.
> - **Present it**, explaining its **features, benefits, and design process** — i.e. narrate not just *what* it is but *how* the design decisions were reached.

> [!info] Change to the team-assignment requirement ^def-assignment-maps-change
> The requirement was **eased from four maps to three**. You must now include only:
> 1. **Empathy Map**
> 2. **Persona Map**
> 3. **GTM (Go-to-Market) Canvas** — *introduced next week*
>
> The **Customer Journey Map is no longer required.**

> [!info] Recap — starting from a design question ^def-dq-recap
> A well-formed [[Design Question]] fixes three things (from [[Lec_10-Practical Innovation 2]]):
> 1. **Customer / User** — who the question is for.
> 2. **Need / Desire** — what the user needs.
> 3. **Problem / Challenge** — the challenge they face.
>
> Templates: *"How might we [action] for [user] to [outcome]?"* · *"How could we [action] to help [user] who [problem]?"* This session's prototyping work is always in service of answering *this* question.

> [!example] "Our Design Innovation Story" — the recorded final team presentation
> - **Describe every step in your design process** throughout the presentation — this illustrates the *logical flow* and development of the project.
> - **Incorporate short citations from your interviews** to justify your ideas — adds credibility and supports your design rationale.
> - **Insert the provided Moodle canvases** (GTM, Empathy Map, Persona) into your presentation, using **digital post-it notes** to present ideas on them, as practised in design-thinking methodology.
> - **Use the Double Diamond as the framework** for your story, to maintain clarity and engagement.
> - **Avoid too much text on slides** — keep them simple and visually appealing.

> [!tip] Why the presentation is graded as a *story*, not a product demo
> Calling it a "Design Innovation Story" is deliberate. The assessment isn't "is the product good?" — it's "can you show the *evidence chain* from user to solution?" User said X (interview quote) → map shows Y → design question asks Z → prototype does W → users validated it. The Double Diamond is the narrative spine because it makes that chain legible. A slick product with no traceable process fails the brief; a modest product with a clear, cited design journey succeeds.

## ✅ Summary

- This session runs the **second diamond** of the [[Double Diamond]] — **Develop** (ideate, prototype, test, fix) and **Deliver** (refine the selected solution) — starting from the [[Design Question]] framed in the first diamond. ^summary-core
