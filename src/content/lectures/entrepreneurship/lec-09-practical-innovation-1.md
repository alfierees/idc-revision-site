## 📚 Session Overview

The previous five classes built the **organisational conditions** for innovation — structure ([[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]]), strategy ([[Lec_03-Innovation Strategy]]), culture ([[Lec_06-Culture of Innovation]]), leadership ([[Lec_07-Innovation Leadership]]) — and then the **psychology of the creative act** ([[Lec_08-Problem Solving]]: motivation, safety, brainstorming). This class begins the **"Practical Innovation"** block: it hands you an actual **method** for turning those conditions into real solutions. That method is ==Design Thinking==.

Where Class 8 gave loose ideation *tools* (brainstorming, Six Hats), Design Thinking gives an **end-to-end process** — a repeatable path from "we don't fully understand the problem" to "we have a tested solution." This is Session One of three; it covers the **first half of the journey**: understanding the problem deeply before rushing to solve it.

> [!info] What this session covers
> 1. The **creative process** in innovative organisations (its four defining features).
> 2. **Design Thinking** — origin (IDEO / Stanford d.school), the five principles, and the desirability–feasibility–viability lens.
> 3. The **Double Diamond** framework — the divergent/convergent map of a design project.
> 4. The **Discover & Define** phase in depth, and its two core tools: the **Empathy Map** and the **Persona**.

> [!tip] The one idea to leave with
> **Fall in love with the problem, not the solution.** The entire first diamond exists to stop you jumping to answers before you actually understand the user. Every tool here (empathy map, persona) is a device for *staying in the problem* long enough to solve the right one.

## 🔄 The Creative Process in Innovative Organizations

Before the method, the lecture characterises *how* creative work actually behaves inside a good organisation. Four features recur.

> [!info] Four features of the creative process ^def-creative-process
> 1. ==Iterative, not linear== — ideas evolve through **feedback loops**; you cycle back, you don't march straight ahead.
> 2. ==Cross-disciplinary== — real innovation involves **collaboration across roles and departments**, not one lone genius.
> 3. ==User-centered== — focused on solving **real problems for real people**, not on the cleverness of the technology.
> 4. ==Tolerant of failure== — treats **experimentation as essential to learning**; a failed prototype is data, not disgrace.

> [!tip] Why these four are exactly what Design Thinking operationalises
> Notice these aren't random virtues — each is a direct callback to earlier classes, and each is *baked into* the Design Thinking process below:
> - **Iterative** ⇒ the Double Diamond loops (test → fix → retest); it is a cycle, not a line.
> - **Cross-disciplinary** ⇒ IDEO's whole model is mixed teams; connects to [[Matrix Structure]] and cross-functional design.
> - **User-centered** ⇒ the *Empathy* stage and the persona/empathy-map tools exist to force this.
> - **Failure-tolerant** ⇒ requires the [[Psychological Safety]] from [[Lec_08-Problem Solving]] — prototyping only works if a broken prototype is safe.

## 💡 Design Thinking

> [!info] Definition — Design Thinking ^def-design-thinking
> ==Design Thinking== is a **user-centered approach to innovation and problem-solving that begins from understanding user needs.** It is widely applied across business, education, and engineering.

> [!info] Origin ^def-design-thinking-origin
> Developed over several decades, associated above all with **[[IDEO]]** — a global design consultancy — and **Stanford**:
> - **David Kelley** — founder of IDEO and a professor at Stanford University (the d.school).
> - **Tim Brown** — CEO of IDEO, who popularised the term in business.

> [!tip] Why "design" thinking and not just "problem solving"
> The word *design* signals the key move: designers don't start from the solution or the technology — they start from the **person who will use the thing**. Traditional problem-solving asks "how do we build X?"; design thinking asks "what does this human actually need, and *then* what should we build?" That reordering — need first, solution second — is the whole discipline.

### The Five Principles (the classic 5-stage model)

> [!info] The five stages of Design Thinking ^def-five-principles
> 1. ==Empathy== — **understand the needs, experiences and motivations of users.** Get inside their world.
> 2. ==Define== — **clearly articulate the problem** based on the insights gathered during Empathy.
> 3. ==Ideate== — **generate a wide range of ideas and solutions** (this is where [[Lec_08-Problem Solving|brainstorming]] lives).
> 4. ==Prototype== — build **simple, experimental models** of potential solutions.
> 5. ==Test== — try prototypes with **real users** and gather feedback (then loop back).

> [!warning] The stages are not a one-way staircase
> A common mistake is to run Empathy → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test *once*, in order, and declare victory. In practice you **loop**: a failed test sends you back to redefine the problem or re-empathise. The five principles map onto the Double Diamond below, which makes this iteration explicit.

### The three lenses: Desirability, Feasibility, Viability

Design Thinking sits a solution at the **intersection of three lenses** — a solution is only good if it satisfies all three.

> [!info] The three lenses ^def-dfv
> - ==Desirability== — *What makes sense **to people and for people**?* Do users actually want it? **(Human lens.)**
> - ==Feasibility== — *What is **technically possible** within the foreseeable future?* Can we build it? **(Technology lens.)**
> - ==Viability== — *What is likely to become part of a **sustainable business model**?* Can we make money / sustain it? **(Business lens.)**

$$\text{Great Innovation} \;=\; \boxed{\text{Desirability} \;\cap\; \text{Feasibility} \;\cap\; \text{Viability}}$$

| Lens | Question | Perspective | If you ignore it… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do people *want* this? | Human / user | You build something nobody needs |
| **Feasibility** | Can we *build* this? | Technology / engineering | You promise something impossible |
| **Viability** | Can it *sustain* as a business? | Business / economics | You build a beloved product that bankrupts you |

> [!tip] Why design thinking *starts* at desirability
> All three lenses are required, but the method deliberately leads with **desirability** (Empathy first). The reasoning: feasibility and viability are constraints you can engineer around, but *desirability cannot be manufactured after the fact* — if people don't want it, no amount of clever engineering or clever pricing saves it. So you establish "do people need this?" **before** sinking resources into "can we build it / can we sell it?"

## 🔷🔷 The Double Diamond Framework

The visual backbone of the session — a map of *how a design project breathes* between exploring widely and narrowing down.

> [!info] Definition — the Double Diamond ^def-double-diamond
> The ==Double Diamond== is a design-process model (created in the UK, by the **Design Council**) that illustrates the phases of a design project. Its core idea is the alternation of **divergent thinking** (open up, explore many possibilities) and **convergent thinking** (narrow down, choose). Two diamonds = two rounds of open-then-narrow: one for the **problem**, one for the **solution**.

> [!info] The four phases ^def-double-diamond-phases
> **First diamond — the *problem* space ("designing the right thing"):**
> 1. ==Discover== — **understand and explore** the question. *Diverge:* research widely, observe users, gather insight without judging.
> 2. ==Define== — **narrow down and choose one problem** to solve. *Converge:* synthesise research into a single, sharp problem statement.
>
> **Second diamond — the *solution* space ("designing the thing right"):**
> 3. ==Develop== — **ideate and prototype, test and fix.** *Diverge:* generate many candidate solutions and build rough versions.
> 4. ==Deliver== — **refine the selected solution.** *Converge:* polish and ship the one that works.

```
        PROBLEM                        SOLUTION
   ◇ diverge → converge ◇         ◇ diverge → converge ◇
   /                    \        /                     \
  <   DISCOVER   DEFINE   >    <   DEVELOP    DELIVER    >
   \                    /        \                     /
    understand → focus            explore → refine
        "right thing"               "thing right"
```

> [!tip] Why *two* diamonds and not one — the deepest point of the session
> The genius of the model is separating the **problem diamond** from the **solution diamond**. Most teams collapse them: they hear a problem and immediately start diverging on *solutions*. The Double Diamond forces you to first diverge-and-converge on the **problem itself** — to *discover* broadly and then *define* one sharp question — **before** you're allowed to touch solutions. This is the structural cure for the number-one innovation failure: **solving the wrong problem beautifully.** This session (Class 9) covers only the **first diamond, Discover & Define**; Develop & Deliver come in the next sessions.

> [!warning] Diverge and converge are different mental modes — don't mix them
> Within each diamond, *diverging* (generate, no judgment — like [[Lec_08-Problem Solving|brainstorming]]'s "defer judgment") and *converging* (evaluate, decide) are opposite activities. Trying to do both at once — brainstorming while critiquing — kills both. The diamond shape is a reminder to fully open up **before** you narrow down.

### Mapping the two models together

| 5-Stage Design Thinking | Double Diamond phase | Diamond | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empathy** | Discover | First (problem) | Diverge |
| **Define** | Define | First (problem) | Converge |
| **Ideate** | Develop | Second (solution) | Diverge |
| **Prototype** | Develop | Second (solution) | Diverge → test |
| **Test** | Deliver | Second (solution) | Converge |

## 🔎 The First Diamond in Practice: Discover & Define

This is the heart of the session. The whole point of Discover & Define is to build **genuine understanding of the user** before defining the problem — and the two tools below are how you do it.

> [!info] Why the first diamond gets its own tools
> "Understand the user" is easy to say and hard to do — teams substitute their *own* assumptions for the user's reality. The **Empathy Map** and the **Persona** are structured devices that force real, evidence-based understanding and make it **tangible and shareable** across the team.

### 🫀 Empathy — the foundation

> [!info] Definition — Empathy ^def-empathy
> ==Empathy== is our ability to **see the world through other people's eyes** — to see what they see, feel what they feel, and experience things as they do. In design thinking it is the *first* and *load-bearing* stage.

> [!warning] Lack of empathy → product failure
> The lecture states it bluntly: **a lack of empathy can result in product failure.** If you don't truly understand the user, you optimise for *your* idea of their needs and build the wrong thing. Empathy is not soft-skills decoration — it is the risk-management step that prevents the most expensive mistake in innovation.

### 🗺️ Tool 1 — The Empathy Map

> [!info] Definition — Empathy Map ^def-empathy-map
> An ==Empathy Map== is a tool that helps you **take what you learn from observing human behaviour and turn it into actionable insights.** It organises observations of a user into quadrants — classically **what they *Say*, *Think*, *Do*, and *Feel*** (often extended with *See*, *Hear*, *Pains*, *Gains*) — so scattered research becomes a structured picture of the person.

> [!tip] Why "Say vs. Think" and "Do vs. Feel" are separated on purpose
> The map's power is in the **gaps between quadrants**. What a user *says* often differs from what they *think*; what they *do* often contradicts what they *feel*. Those contradictions are exactly where hidden needs hide — the unmet need a user can't articulate. A single "what do users want?" list flattens all that; the four-quadrant split surfaces the tension that points to a real insight.

### 👤 Tool 2 — The Persona

> [!info] Definition — Persona ^def-persona
> A ==Persona== is a **fictional representation of a target customer, built from market research and data.** The **persona canvas** gives a customer segment a **face and a name**, making it easier to *step into the customer's shoes*. Personas make talking about customers concrete and tangible, and make it easy to refer back to a consistent **pattern of characteristics**.

> [!tip] Why invent a fictional person from real data
> A persona is a deliberate abstraction: it compresses messy, statistical research about a whole **segment** into a single memorable human ("Sarah, 34, time-poor working parent…"). This does two jobs. First, it keeps the team **user-centered** — you design *for Sarah*, not for an abstract "market." Second, it creates a **shared reference**: when the team argues about a feature, "would Sarah use this?" is a far sharper test than "would customers use this?" The fiction is a tool for empathy at scale.

> [!warning] Personas must be built from *data*, not imagination
> The danger is inventing a persona that simply reflects the team's own assumptions — a mirror, not a user. A persona is only valid if it is grounded in **actual research** (interviews, observation, market data). An evidence-free persona re-imports exactly the bias that empathy was meant to remove.

### How the two tools chain into "Define"

> [!info] From tools to a problem statement
> The Discover tools feed the Define step: **observe users → fill the Empathy Map (actionable insights) → synthesise into a Persona (the who) → converge on a single, sharp problem statement (the what).** Only once you can state *"[Persona] needs [need] because [insight]"* has the first diamond done its job — and only then are you allowed into the solution diamond.

## ✅ Summary

- The **creative process** in innovative organisations is **iterative, cross-disciplinary, user-centered, and failure-tolerant** — and Design Thinking is the method that operationalises all four. ^summary-core
- **Design Thinking** (IDEO — David Kelley & Tim Brown; Stanford d.school) is a **user-centered** method with five stages: **Empathy → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test**, looping rather than linear.
- A good solution sits at the intersection of three lenses: **Desirability** (people), **Feasibility** (technology), **Viability** (business) — and the method leads with *desirability* because you can't manufacture want after the fact.
- The **Double Diamond** splits a project into a **problem diamond (Discover → Define)** and a **solution diamond (Develop → Deliver)**, each alternating **divergent** (explore widely) and **convergent** (narrow down) thinking. Separating the two diamonds is the structural cure for *solving the wrong problem well*.
- This session covers the **first diamond — Discover & Define**. Its foundation is **Empathy** (lack of it → product failure), and its two core tools are the **Empathy Map** (turns observation into actionable insight via Say/Think/Do/Feel) and the **Persona** (a data-grounded fictional user that keeps the team user-centered and gives a shared reference for decisions).
