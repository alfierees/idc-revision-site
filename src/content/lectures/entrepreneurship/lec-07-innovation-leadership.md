# Innovation Leadership

## 📚 Session Overview

This is the fourth of the four **organisational levers**. Classes 2, 3 and 6 covered **structure** ([[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]]), **strategy** ([[Lec_03-Innovation Strategy]]) and **culture** ([[Lec_06-Culture of Innovation]]). This class covers the last one — **leadership** — the human force that sets the direction and mobilises the other three levers. The core claim: *innovation does not happen by accident; someone has to point at a future worth chasing and pull people toward it.* We cover:

1. What **leadership** is — and its inseparable partner, **followership**.
2. Leaders as **setters of strategic direction** — vision and mission.
3. Why an **ambitious vision** is itself an engine of innovation.
4. **Transformational leadership** — the leadership style most strongly tied to innovation.
5. The **difference between leadership and management**.
6. Where leadership sits in the **organisational innovation ecosystem** (the Google case).

> [!info] Where this sits in the course
> Structure is the wiring, strategy is the direction on the map, culture is what happens when nobody is watching — but **leadership is the actor who chooses the direction and inspires people to follow it**. A firm can have a flat structure, a Blue Ocean strategy and an adhocracy culture and still stall, because none of those levers *decides* anything on its own. Leadership is the lever that *activates* the others: it authors the vision that culture then internalises and strategy then executes.

## 🧭 What Is Leadership?

> [!info] Definition — Leadership ^def-leadership
> ==Leadership== is *"an **influence relationship** among leaders and followers who intend **real changes and outcomes** that reflect their **shared purposes**."* (Rost, 1993, p. 102)

Unpack the definition — every clause is doing work:

- **Influence relationship, not authority.** Leadership is not a job title or a position on an org chart; it is a *relationship*. You lead only to the extent you actually influence others. This is why leadership can appear anywhere in a [[Flat Structure]], not just at the top.
- **Leaders *and* followers.** Leadership is a two-sided act. Without followers there is no leader — the relationship is mutual, not one-directional.
- **Intend real changes.** Leadership is inherently about *change* — moving from a present state to a different future. This is precisely why leadership and innovation are so tightly linked: both are about departing from the status quo.
- **Shared purposes.** The changes reflect goals held *in common*, not the leader's private agenda. Coercion toward one person's ends is not leadership.

> [!info] Definition — Followership ^def-followership
> ==Followership==: *everyone — leaders included — is a follower at one time or another.* Despite the cultural focus on leaders, **most of us are more often followers than leaders**.

> [!tip] Why followership matters
> The course deliberately introduces followership alongside leadership because the two are **roles, not fixed identities**. The same person is a leader in one context and a follower in another (the *Crimson Tide* (1995) clip is used in class to ask exactly this: in a given moment, who is leading and who is following?). For innovation this is liberating — you don't need a corner office to lead an idea; and a good leader is also a good follower, able to back someone else's better vision.

## 🎯 Leaders Set Strategic Direction

> [!info] The leader's core job ^def-strategic-direction
> Leaders are responsible for setting a **visionary direction** that **motivates everyone to contribute to the organisation's success.** Leaders set the *strategic decisions* — they choose the destination the whole organisation then aims for.

This connects leadership back to strategy ([[Lec_03-Innovation Strategy]]): strategy is the *content* of the direction, but leadership is the *act* of choosing it and getting people to move.

### Vision vs. Mission

> [!info] Definition — Organizational Vision ^def-vision
> An ==organizational vision== is a **clear and inspiring portrayal of what an organisation or team aspires to achieve in the future.** A **vision statement** communicates *future aspirations*: where does the organisation want to be in ten years?

> [!info] Definition — Mission ^def-mission
> A ==mission statement== is **grounded in the present**, conveying the **daily steps** an organisation promises to take in order to achieve the bigger vision. *(Source: leaders.com.)*

> [!tip] The intuition — horizon vs. footsteps
> **Vision is the horizon; mission is the footsteps.** Vision answers *"where are we going?"* (aspirational, long-term, emotional). Mission answers *"what do we do every day to get there?"* (concrete, present-tense, operational). A vision without a mission is a daydream; a mission without a vision is a treadmill. Great firms keep both explicit so employees can connect today's task to tomorrow's destination.

| | Vision statement | Mission statement |
|---|---|---|
| **Time frame** | The future (e.g. 10 years out) | The present / everyday |
| **Answers** | Where do we *want to be*? | What do we *do* to get there? |
| **Tone** | Aspirational, inspiring | Concrete, operational |
| **Function** | Motivates & orients | Guides daily action |

> [!example] Worked examples from class
> **LinkedIn** — *Vision:* "Create economic opportunity for every member of the global workforce." *Mission:* "To connect the world's professionals to make them more productive and successful."
> **Apple** — *Vision:* "To make the best products on earth and to leave the world better than we found it." *Mission:* "To bring the best personal computing products and support to students, educators, designers, scientists, engineers, businesspersons, and consumers in over 140 countries around the world."
> Notice the pattern: each **vision** is short, sweeping and future-facing; each **mission** is longer, specific and present-facing (who they serve, how, where).

## 💡 Why Vision Drives Innovation

> [!info] Ambitious vision → innovation ^def-vision-innovation
> When a vision is **ambitious**, it **challenges the status quo and encourages innovation.** It pushes team members to **think creatively and explore new possibilities** to overcome obstacles and reach new heights.

> [!tip] The mechanism
> An ambitious vision creates a **productive gap** between *where we are* and *where we say we're going*. That gap can't be closed by doing more of the same — the existing playbook, by definition, only produces the status quo. So an ambitious goal *forces* creative problem-solving: it makes innovation the only available route. This is the same "stretch goal" logic behind Google's [[20% Rule]] and moonshot targets — set the bar impossibly high and incremental thinking is no longer enough. Note the loop back to culture: a "YES" culture ([[Lec_06-Culture of Innovation]]) is what lets an ambitious vision actually be attempted rather than dismissed.

## 🔄 Transformational Leadership

The lecture's central leadership model. This is the style most consistently linked to innovation.

> [!info] Definition — Transformational Leadership ^def-transformational
> ==Transformational leaders== have the ability to **lead changes in an organisation's vision, strategy, and culture**, as well as to **promote innovation in products and technologies.** Per **Grădinaru et al. (2020)**, it is a style in which *"the followers are **encouraged and inspired to see past their own self-interest** in order to achieve a **greater vision**."*

> [!info] A "soft" leadership approach
> Transformational leadership is a ==soft leadership approach== that focuses on **empowering others, fostering innovation, and encouraging change.** Its five signature behaviours:
> - **Inspiring a shared vision** — articulate a future people *want* to belong to.
> - **Empowering team members** — push authority and ownership downward.
> - **Adapting to rapid change** — treat change as normal, not as threat.
> - **Encouraging innovation and initiative** — reward people for trying.
> - **Creating ethical, attitudinal, and process changes** among followers — change *how people think and behave*, not just what they do.

> [!tip] Why "see past self-interest" is the key phrase
> The defining move of transformational leadership is **elevating followers' motivation from self-interest to a shared, higher purpose.** A purely transactional relationship ("do X, get paid Y") only ever buys compliance. Transformational leadership buys *discretionary effort* — the extra creativity people give when they believe in the mission. That discretionary effort is exactly the raw material of innovation, which can never be fully specified in a job description or bought with a bonus alone.

> [!example] Jeff Bezos / Amazon (class clip)
> The lecture asks: *what sort of transformational innovation did Bezos bring to Amazon?* Bezos repeatedly led changes to Amazon's **vision** (from online bookstore → "everything store" → cloud infrastructure with AWS), its **strategy** (relentless long-term reinvestment over short-term profit), and its **culture** ("Day 1" mindset, customer obsession) — the textbook definition of transformational leadership operating across all three domains at once.

> [!warning] Why it's called "essential" for modern firms
> Transformational leadership is described as **essential for modern organisations that require agility and continuous transformation to thrive.** The pitfall to avoid: assuming stable, efficiency-focused (transactional) leadership is enough. In fast-moving, technology-driven markets, a leader who only optimises the present will preside over gradual obsolescence — the same trap a [[Hierarchy Culture]] falls into.

## ⚖️ Leadership vs. Management

A recurring exam-style distinction. They are complementary, not interchangeable — and confusing them is a classic error.

> [!info] Definitions side by side ^def-leadership-vs-management
> **Leadership** — creating a **compelling vision** for the future, setting **strategic context**, and developing **long-term strategies** to achieve that vision. Emphasises looking toward the **horizon and long-term future**, rather than just short-term results.
> **Management** — achieving organisational goals through the **effective and efficient handling of resources**: planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling. Focuses on **detailed plans and schedules** for specific results and the allocation of necessary resources.

| | Leadership | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Core question** | *What future should we pursue?* | *How do we deliver it efficiently?* |
| **Time horizon** | Long-term, the horizon | Short-term, specific results |
| **Primary activity** | Vision, strategic direction, change | Planning, organising, staffing, directing, controlling |
| **Deals in** | People, purpose, inspiration | Resources, plans, schedules |
| **Orientation** | *Doing the right things* | *Doing things right* |

> [!tip] Both are needed — they are not ranked
> The point is **not** that leadership is "better" than management. A vision no one executes is worthless; flawless execution of the wrong vision is worse. **Management without leadership** produces an efficient organisation marching confidently in the wrong direction; **leadership without management** produces an inspiring vision that never ships. Innovative organisations need leaders to *choose the horizon* and managers to *build the road to it* — and often need the same person to switch between both modes.

## 🧩 Leadership in the Innovation Ecosystem

> [!info] The four levers, one system ^def-ecosystem
> The course's organising diagram places **[[Organizational Innovation]]** at the centre of four interacting levers — **Structure, Culture, Strategy, and Leadership** — all sitting inside a wider **process** and **eco-system**, and connected outward through **[[Open Innovation]]**. Leadership is the lever that sets the vision the other three then serve.

> [!example] Google, read through all four levers
> The class analyses **Google** across the four perspectives — the template for the in-class exercise:
> - **Structure** — a **matrix** enabling agility and cross-functional collaboration.
> - **Culture** — inclusive, innovative, and **psychologically safe**.
> - **Leadership** — **transformational, empathetic, and data-driven**.
> - **Strategy** — innovation-led, user-focused, and ethically grounded.
> The lesson: a firm is innovative when all four levers *reinforce* one another. Google's transformational leadership is credible precisely because its structure, culture and strategy back it up. (See the fuller Google story in [[Lec_06-Culture of Innovation]].)

## ✅ Summary

- ==Leadership== is an **influence relationship** among leaders and followers who intend **real change** toward **shared purposes** (Rost 1993) — a *relationship*, not a title, so it can arise anywhere in the organisation. ^summary-core
- Leadership is inseparable from ==followership==: leader and follower are **roles, not fixed identities**, and most of us are followers more often than leaders.
- Leaders' core job is to **set strategic direction** via a **[[Organizational Vision|vision]]** (the future horizon) supported by a **mission** (the daily footsteps). An **ambitious vision drives innovation** by opening a gap the status quo cannot close.
- **[[Transformational Leadership]]** — the innovation-linked style — inspires followers to **see past self-interest toward a greater vision**; it leads change in **vision, strategy and culture** and is a *soft* approach built on empowerment, adaptability and encouraging initiative (Grădinaru et al. 2020). Bezos/Amazon is the exemplar.
- **Leadership vs. management**: leadership = *vision, horizon, doing the right things*; management = *resources, schedules, doing things right*. Both are required; neither is superior.
- Leadership is the fourth of the four **organisational innovation levers** — Structure, Culture, Strategy, Leadership — which must **reinforce one another**, as the four-perspective Google analysis shows.
