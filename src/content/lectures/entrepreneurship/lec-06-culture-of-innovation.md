# Culture of Innovation

> Part of: [[Entrepreneurship]] | Key concepts: [[Organizational Culture]], [[OCAI Framework]], [[Barriers to Innovation]], [[Radical Innovation]], [[Google Culture Case Study]]

---

## 📚 Session Overview

Classes 2 and 3 covered the **structure** ([[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]]) and **strategy** ([[Lec_03-Innovation Strategy]]) levers. This class moves to the **culture** lever — the shared values and behaviours that decide whether new ideas actually survive inside the organisation. The question shifts from *"how are we wired?"* and *"which battle do we pick?"* to *"do our people instinctively welcome or reject new ideas?"* We cover:

1. What **organisational culture** is.
2. **Culture-of-innovation models** — the four Cameron & Quinn ([[OCAI Framework]]) culture types and how each affects innovation.
3. **Internal and external barriers** to innovation, and why [[Radical Innovation]] faces the steepest ones.
4. How to **tackle resistance** to innovation.
5. **Google's culture** as a case study of a deliberately innovation-friendly culture.

> [!info] Where this sits in the course
> Structure is the wiring, strategy is the direction — but **culture is what happens when nobody is watching**. A firm can have the right structure and a brilliant strategy and still kill innovation because its people fear change. Culture is the lever that determines whether the other levers are actually used. This is why culture, not budget, is repeatedly named the *biggest* obstacle to innovation in large firms.

## 🧭 What Is Organisational Culture?

> [!info] Definition ^def-org-culture
> ==Organisational culture== is the **shared values, beliefs, and behaviors that shape how members of an organisation think, act, and interact** — both inside the organisation and with the outside world. *(The Business Dictionary.)*

> [!tip] The intuition
> Culture is the *invisible operating system* of a firm. Structure and strategy are written down; culture is not — it lives in "how we do things here." Because it is unwritten and self-reinforcing, it is slow to change and can silently override formal decisions: leadership can *announce* an innovation push, but if the culture punishes failed experiments, people will quietly avoid them.

## 🚧 Culture as an Obstacle to Innovation

A survey of **270 corporate leaders** (HBR, Kirshner) identified **cultural issues as a major obstacle to innovation** in large companies. Four themes explain why — and hint at the fixes:

> [!warning] Why big firms resist innovation (HBR)
> - ==Cultural resistance== — many large companies prize **operational excellence and predictable growth**, which breeds resistance to experiments that might disrupt stable operations. *(The very habits that make a firm efficient make it hostile to disruption.)*
> - ==Historical baggage== — long-standing employees **remember past innovation failures**, creating a culture sceptical of new ideas.
> - ==Innovation subcultures== — deliberately establishing **protected subcultures** gives new ideas space to develop free of the broader organisational norms. *(A fix, not just a barrier.)*
> - ==Incentives and diversity== — introducing **new incentives that reward innovative behaviour** and bringing in **diverse talent** shifts the culture toward creativity. *(Another fix.)*

> [!tip] The pattern
> The first two items (resistance, baggage) are the *disease*; the last two (subcultures, incentives + diversity) are the *treatment*. Culture change works by **carving out protected space** and **re-pointing the reward system** — you rarely convert the whole organisation at once.

## 🎭 The Four Culture Types — Cameron & Quinn's OCAI

> [!info] What OCAI is ^def-ocai
> The ==Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI)==, by **Cameron & Quinn**, classifies a firm's culture into **four types**. Each has a distinctive relationship with innovation. No type is "wrong" — but they support *different kinds* of innovation.

| Culture type | Feel of the workplace | Held together by | Leaders are | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ==Clan== | Pleasant, family-like; people share personal information | **Loyalty & tradition** | Mentors / parent-figures | Lego |
| ==Adhocracy== | Dynamic, entrepreneurial, creative; people take risks | **Innovation & experimentation** | Innovators & risk-takers | Nvidia |
| ==Market== | Result-oriented; getting the job done | **Winning & goals** | Hard drivers / competitors | General Electric |
| ==Hierarchy== | Formalised, structured; procedures govern behaviour | **Rules & procedures** | Coordinators / organisers | McKinsey |

### How each culture shapes innovation

> [!info] Impact of each type on innovation
> - **Clan** → facilitates innovation through a **supportive, collaborative** environment; open communication and engagement surface ideas from a nurtured workforce.
> - **Adhocracy** → **most conducive to groundbreaking innovation**; actively promotes risk-taking and creativity → best for *radical* innovation and continuous improvement.
> - **Market** → drives innovation aimed at **competitiveness and market demands**, mostly via **incremental improvements** tailored to customer needs.
> - **Hierarchy** → focuses on **stability and efficiency**, often at the expense of flexibility; **limits radical innovation** but supports **incremental** process improvements that cut cost.

> [!tip] Radical vs. incremental, mapped onto culture
> Read the four types along a spectrum from *exploration* to *exploitation*. **Adhocracy** and **Clan** lean toward exploration (new ideas, radical bets); **Market** and **Hierarchy** lean toward exploitation (refine, optimise, incremental gains). This is why a hierarchy-heavy firm can be excellent at cost-cutting innovation yet consistently miss disruptive shifts — its culture is tuned to *stability*, not *novelty*. See [[Lec_02-Organisation Structure]]: a flat structure and an adhocracy culture tend to reinforce each other.

| | Radical innovation | Incremental innovation |
|---|---|---|
| Best-fit cultures | Adhocracy (strongest), Clan | Market, Hierarchy |
| What the culture rewards | Risk-taking, novelty, experiments | Efficiency, targets, reliability |
| Typical output | New paradigms, breakthrough products | Process/cost improvements, feature refinements |

## 💥 Barriers to Innovation

> [!info] Definition ^def-barriers
> ==Barriers to innovation== are **factors that prevent or hinder an organisation's ability to engage in innovative activities.**

> [!info] Radical innovation faces the steepest barriers ^def-radical
> ==Radical innovation== is the development of **fundamentally new products, services, or processes** with a significant impact on the market and on firms' economic activity. Its barriers are **inherently greater** because of the **higher risk, greater uncertainty, and greater complexity** of altering the status quo or creating a new technological paradigm.

Cameron-style culture explains *why* barriers form; the **Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos (2014)** systematic review classifies *what* those barriers are, splitting them into **internal** (inside the firm's control) and **external** (in the environment).

### Internal barriers

> [!warning] Barriers inside the firm (Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos, 2014)
> - ==Restrictive mindset== — fear of change and a preference for the **status quo**, which stifles innovation.
> - ==Lack of competences==, across the innovation pipeline:
>   - **Discovery** — inability to *identify and articulate* opportunities.
>   - **Incubation** — inability to *develop* opportunities into viable business proposals.
>   - **Acceleration & commercialisation** — inability to *scale* an innovation to stand on its own.
> - ==Insufficient resources== — lack of internal **finance, skills, or information**.
> - ==Unsupportive organisational structure== — traditional **hierarchical** structures impede the coordination and communication innovation needs.

### External barriers

> [!warning] Barriers in the environment (Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos, 2014)
> **Resistance / lack of support from specific actors:**
> - ==Customer resistance== — changing needs and lack of experience make customers reluctant to adopt new tech.
> - ==Unsupportive government== — restrictive regulations, laws, and standards.
> - ==Shortage of external finance== — financial institutions are conservative toward innovative firms.
> - ==Rivalry== — competitors' advances push competing technologies and block yours.
>
> **Restrictive macro environment:**
> - ==Undeveloped network/ecosystem== — resistance within the stakeholder network.
> - ==Technological turbulence== — rapid change makes committing to one technology risky.
> - ==Inappropriate infrastructure== — inadequate facilities.
> - ==Restrictive local culture== — cultural and religious beliefs resist adoption.

> [!tip] Internal vs. external — the useful distinction
> **Internal barriers** are the ones the firm can fix directly through culture, structure, hiring, and budget (this is where the OCAI levers bite). **External barriers** must be *managed* rather than removed — through partnerships, lobbying, ecosystem-building, and timing. The [[Discovery Competences|discovery → incubation → acceleration]] chain is worth memorising: it maps neatly onto the *idea → prototype → scale* journey, and a firm can fail at any single link.

> [!example] In-class exercise
> Spend 2 minutes discussing different barriers to innovation. Then **choose one key barrier and propose a practical solution** to overcome it. *(Ties the taxonomy above back to the HBR fixes: protected subcultures, new incentives, diverse talent.)*

## 🔵 Google's Culture Case Study

Google is the class's exemplar of a **deliberately engineered innovation culture** — an adhocracy at scale.

> [!info] Origins
> Founders ==Sergey Brin and Larry Page== met as PhD researchers at **Stanford (1995)**. In **1997** they named the project *Google* — a play on **googol** ($10^{100}$), signalling the scale of information they intended to organise. **Eric Schmidt** was appointed CEO in **2001**. Since the **October 2015** restructuring, Google sits under the holding company ==Alphabet==, with **Sundar Pichai** as CEO.

> [!info] Two founding goals (Steiber, 2014)
> From the start, the leaders had two explicit goals: **(1) organise the world's information** and **(2) make the company the best in the world.** Crucially, they **did not want to build a conventional company** — they set out to invent their own model based on *"thinking differently."* The culture was a *design choice*, not an accident.

**Creative environment.** The workplace deliberately feels like a **university campus** (a heritage from Stanford): young, cool, fun, free international food, toys, bright colours, airiness — a combination of **seriousness and play**. The physical environment is itself a culture tool.

> [!info] "Googliness" — culture as informal control ^def-googliness
> ==Googliness== is the process of **adjusting to the company culture**; an employee who acts according to Google's norms and values is *"Googley."* Managers are expected to be **ambassadors of Googliness**. Its key function: **it reduces the need for formal rules and instructions** — shared values do the coordinating that a rulebook otherwise would.

> [!tip] Why "Googliness" matters strategically
> This is the culture lever doing the work of the structure lever. Instead of controlling behaviour through **hierarchy and procedure** (a *hierarchy* culture), Google controls it through **internalised shared values** (an *adhocracy/clan* blend). Strong culture substitutes for formal rules — cheaper, faster, and far more supportive of the autonomy that innovation needs. The cost: it only works if hiring rigorously selects for cultural fit.

> [!info] The 20% Rule ^def-twenty-percent
> Employees may spend ==20% of work hours== developing **their own ideas to a pitch level**. By **2005, half of Google's new ideas came from the 20% time**. Beyond its output, the rule is an **important symbol** of Google's innovative approach — it signals, structurally, that experimentation is expected, not tolerated.

> [!tip] Google's "YES" culture
> Google is characterised as a **"YES" culture** — the default response to a new idea is *yes, try it* rather than *no, prove it first*. This directly attacks the two HBR barriers (cultural resistance and historical baggage): a firm that defaults to "yes" cannot accumulate a culture of scepticism toward new ideas. *(Ref: Eric Schmidt on Google's culture, Bloomberg 2016.)*

## ✅ Summary

- ==Organisational culture== is the **shared values, beliefs and behaviours** that shape how members think and act — the firm's invisible operating system, and repeatedly named the **biggest obstacle to innovation** in large companies. ^summary-core
- Large firms resist innovation via **cultural resistance** and **historical baggage**; the fixes are **protected innovation subcultures** and **new incentives + diverse talent** (HBR, Kirshner).
- **Cameron & Quinn's [[OCAI Framework]]** gives four culture types — **Clan** (supportive/collaborative), **Adhocracy** (risk-taking, *best for radical* innovation), **Market** (competitive, *incremental*), **Hierarchy** (stable/efficient, *limits radical*, supports incremental). Adhocracy + Clan favour exploration; Market + Hierarchy favour exploitation.
- **[[Radical Innovation]]** faces the steepest barriers (higher risk, uncertainty, complexity). Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos (2014) split them into **internal** (restrictive mindset, missing discovery/incubation/commercialisation competences, insufficient resources, unsupportive structure) and **external** (customer/government/finance/rivalry resistance; turbulent, ill-equipped macro environment).
- **Google** is the exemplar innovation culture: a deliberate, campus-like adhocracy where **"Googliness"** substitutes shared values for formal rules, the **20% rule** institutionalises experimentation (half of 2005's new ideas), and a **"YES" default** blocks the accumulation of scepticism.
- Culture sits alongside structure, strategy and leadership as the levers of [[Organizational Innovation]]: it decides whether new ideas survive contact with the organisation.
