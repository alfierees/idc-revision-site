## Why macroeconomics exists

Modern macroeconomics grew out of the **Great Depression**. Before 1929, the dominant view followed **Adam Smith** (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776): government should stay small, markets reach equilibrium on their own, *laissez faire*.

## What happened in 1929

From 1915 to 1929, more and more people bought stocks, including very risky ones, often **with loans**. They expected to sell at a higher price, repay the loan and keep the profit. The crash came in late October 1929 (Black Thursday on 24 October, then 28 and 29 October).

The slides trace a self-reinforcing chain:

1. The market crashes and investors cannot repay their loans.
2. Companies lose value and income, so they lay off workers.
3. Laid-off workers spend less.
4. Companies lose more income and lay off more people.

| Consequence | Figure in the slides |
|---|---|
| US unemployment | from about 5% to about 25% |
| Homelessness | 2 million Americans in 1933 |
| Banks | by 1933, half as many active banks as in 1930 (Bernanke, 1983) |
| Prices | deflation through the early 1930s |

> [!note] Labour market view
> If labour demand falls and the wage does not fall to clear the market, the quantity of labour supplied exceeds the quantity demanded. That gap is **unemployment**.

## Policy: Hoover, then Roosevelt

**Hoover (1929 to 1933)** kept to a balanced budget, customs duties and the gold standard.

**Roosevelt (1933 to 1945)** introduced *relief, recovery and reform*:

- **Relief:** the Social Security Act.
- **Recovery:** the New Deal, with USD 3.3 billion of government-funded buildings, roads, dams, hospitals, airports and schools.
- **Reform:** the Emergency Banking Act, FDIC deposit insurance, the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Glass-Steagall Act (1933).

## Keynes

**John Maynard Keynes** resigned from the Versailles delegation because he thought Germany's reparations were too harsh (*The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, 1919). In *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) he argued:

- Recessions combine unemployment with low production.
- Government can lead the way out by **creating new income for consumers**.
- Consumers with income raise demand, firms hire to meet it, and unemployment falls.
- Demand can also be raised by printing money.

The slides also link the depression in Germany to political collapse: the Nazi vote rose from 2.6% in 1928 to 38.4% in July 1932, and Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933.

## Later crises

| Crisis | Monetary policy | Fiscal policy |
|---|---|---|
| **2008** | Rates cut close to zero, quantitative easing, emergency lending facilities | TARP (USD 700 billion), ARRA (USD 787 billion), housing aid, then Dodd-Frank (2010) |
| **COVID-19, US** | Rates to 0 to 0.25%, bond buying, credit facilities | Several relief acts, including USD 868 billion (Dec 2020) and USD 1,844 billion (Mar 2021) |
| **COVID-19, Israel** | Policy rate cut to 0.1%, government bond purchases up to NIS 85 billion, corporate bond purchases, easier bank rules | About NIS 202.3 billion of approved measures |

> [!tip] Exam takeaway
> **Monetary policy** works through interest rates and credit. **Fiscal policy** works through government spending, transfers and taxes.
