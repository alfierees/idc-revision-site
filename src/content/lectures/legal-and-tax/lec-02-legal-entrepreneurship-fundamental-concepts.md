# Legal Entrepreneurship — Fundamental Concepts

> Part of: [[Legal and Tax Aspects]] | Key concepts: [[Corporate Structure]], [[Contracts]], [[Founders' Agreement]], [[Intellectual Property Rights]], [[Fundraising]], [[Valuation]], [[Regulatory Compliance]]

> **Session 2** — Legal and Tax Aspects of Entrepreneurship, Dr. Yuval Reinfeld (Reichman University)

---

## 🎯 Why This Session Exists

Session 1 framed the *startup lifecycle* and the structure of the Israeli legal system. This session zooms into the **vocabulary** a founder needs to operate inside that lifecycle. It is essentially a structured glossary — but the point is **not** to memorise definitions. The goal is to recognise *which* legal instrument solves *which* business problem, and *when* in the venture's life each one becomes urgent.

> [!info] The opening cautionary tale — "Scope 360"
> Or and Liat built a promising 3D photo-sharing startup and attracted investor interest. Eager to ship, they **postponed all legal work** as something that "could wait." Six months in, disputes over management and division of responsibilities erupted. Because nothing had been written down, the conflict caused real financial damage and **scared off the investors**. The lesson is the same as Session 1's "legal debt compounds": the cheapest moment to settle the rules is *before* there is money or ego at stake.

> [!tip] The intuition driving the whole session
> Almost every concept below is a **tool for converting an informal expectation into an enforceable arrangement**. A handshake ("we're equal partners") is worthless under stress; a ==founders' agreement== with vesting and dispute mechanisms is not. Law turns trust into structure.

---

## 🧭 The Mission: Three Legal Pillars of a Venture

The session organises a founder's legal universe into **three recurring pillars**. Every concept later in the note slots into one of them.

| Pillar | What it governs | Representative instruments |
|--------|-----------------|----------------------------|
| **Business Structure** | *What kind of legal entity* the venture is, and who owns/controls it | Corporation, corporate structure, subsidiaries, ESOP, vesting |
| **Contractual Aspect** | *Relationships and obligations* between people and firms | Founders' agreement, NDA, investment agreement, service agreement, licences |
| **Regulation** | *Mandatory external rules* the venture must obey | Privacy law (GDPR), employment law, antitrust, regulatory compliance |

> [!info] Why law is an "anchor", not a brake
> The law lets entrepreneurs build a **clear and transparent framework** for operations. Through contracts, bylaws, and partnership agreements, founders can fix the division of responsibilities, protect assets and IP, and define relationships with investors and clients. A proper legal framework does double duty: it **protects against future risk** *and* **strengthens credibility** with investors and partners. The session's tagline: *"a successful business is one that has closed its legal risk gaps."*

## ⚠️ The Five Core Legal Risks

These are the failure modes a founder is implicitly managing against. Each maps to a pillar above.

| # | Risk | What goes wrong | Pillar |
|---|------|-----------------|--------|
| 1 | **No founders' agreement** | Uncertainty over responsibilities, voting rights, and obligations → disputes, instability, investors walk away | Contractual |
| 2 | **Improper corporate structure** | Complications in duties/rights, taxation, and lawsuit protection; deters investors; friction in fundraising, M&A | Structure |
| 3 | **No NDAs** | Ideas, technology, or sensitive data leak to outsiders → loss of competitive advantage | Contractual |
| 4 | **Neglecting privacy** | Breach of privacy regulation (e.g. ==GDPR==) → heavy fines, legal consequences, reputational damage | Regulation |
| 5 | **Improper open-source use** | Violating licence terms → copyright claims, forced disclosure of proprietary code as open source, security exposure, loss of product control | IP / Contractual |

> [!warning] Risk #5 is the one founders underestimate
> Open source feels "free", but every licence carries **terms**. So-called *copyleft* licences (e.g. GPL) can require you to release your own code as open source if you distribute software that incorporates the licensed code. A careless dependency can therefore **forfeit control of your core IP** — exactly the asset investors are buying.

## 🏢 Pillar 1 — Business Structure

### Corporation

> [!info] Definition
> A ==corporation== is a **separate legal entity** created to distinguish the **personal assets of shareholders** from those of the company. It provides shareholders with protection and enables raising capital by **issuing shares**.

The decisive feature is ==limited liability==: register a limited company (Ltd.) and the founders' personal assets are shielded from the company's debts and lawsuits. ^corporation

### Corporate Structure

> [!info] Definition
> The ==corporate structure== is the definition of the company's **internal structure** — the type of entity (Ltd., private company, partnership) and the roles of its managers.

Why it matters: the structure simultaneously determines **legal liability**, **taxation**, and **fundraising opportunities**. A clear structure lets the company operate efficiently, minimise legal risk, keep a consistent division of responsibilities, and maximise fundraising potential. It also governs the ability to give **employee incentives** (such as ==stock options==) and the company's accessibility to markets and investors.

#### The three entity types

| Structure | Liability | Ownership & control | Typical use | Capital raising |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Private Ltd. / LLC** | **Limited** — company assets separate from shareholders' personal assets | Founders keep majority; can grant options to employees/investors | **Startups** (the default choice) | Issue shares; flexible incentive design |
| **Partnership** | **Personal** liability for each partner (unless a limited partnership) | Two+ parties share ownership per a partnership agreement | Law firms, accounting firms — where personal accountability matters | Limited; depends on partners |
| **Public Corporation** | Limited | Shares held by the public (typically after an ==IPO==); management accountable to dispersed shareholders | Large, mature companies | **Large-scale** via public share sales — but strict regulatory & transparency duties |

> [!tip] Why startups almost always pick the Ltd. / LLC
> It is the only common structure that (a) shields founders personally, (b) lets you **slice ownership into shares** for investors and employees, and (c) is flexible enough to layer on option plans. A partnership exposes you personally; a public corporation's reporting burden is overkill for an early venture.

### Subsidiaries

> [!info] Definition
> A ==subsidiary== is a company **owned or controlled by another company** (the ==parent company==). The parent typically holds **more than 50%** of the subsidiary's shares and oversees its operations.

Subsidiaries may operate independently day-to-day but remain bound by the parent's **policies and strategic direction** — useful for ring-fencing risk, entering new jurisdictions, or separating product lines. ^subsidiaries

### Start-up

> [!info] Definition
> A ==startup== is a newly established company built on a **unique idea**, whose primary goal is **rapid growth**. They usually operate in technological/innovative fields and seek **external funding** to turn the idea into a successful business.

The "rapid growth + external funding" combination is what drives almost every legal need in this note: growth means hiring (employment law, ESOP), funding means investors (investment agreements, valuation, anti-dilution), and innovation means IP to protect. ^startup-def

## 📜 Pillar 2 — Contracts & Agreements

### What a contract is

> [!info] Definition
> A ==contract== is a legal document binding the parties to specific obligations. It is finalised **only once the elements of [[offer and acceptance]] are fulfilled**.

==Contract Law== regulates the validity of contracts and the requirements for a binding agreement — **offer, acceptance, and legal capacity** (Sections 2–6 of the Israeli Contracts Law). ^contract-law

> [!warning] Cross-border contracts add requirements
> If a contract involves **foreign parties**, additional rules from other jurisdictions may apply — e.g. an obligation to include a ==jurisdiction clause== specifying *which* country's courts and law govern a dispute. *Example:* an Israeli startup contracting with a U.S. VC will usually have terms governed by **both** Israeli and U.S. law, with the governing jurisdiction stated explicitly.

### Map of the key agreements

This is the heart of the session. Group them by the relationship they regulate:

| Agreement | Who it binds | Core purpose |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Founders' Agreement** | Co-founders | Align vision, roles, share split, dispute resolution, founder-departure protection |
| **NDA** | Anyone exposed to confidential info | Prevent leakage of sensitive information |
| **Investment Agreement** | Founders ↔ investors | Terms of an investment: valuation, shares, rights, influence |
| **Service Agreement** | Supplier ↔ business client | Define services, timelines, financial terms, liability |
| **Non-Compete** | Employer ↔ employee | Stop ex-employees competing / leaking trade secrets |
| **Privacy Policy** | Company ↔ users | Disclose how personal data is collected/stored/used (legally required) |
| **Terms of Service (ToS / T&C)** | Operator ↔ users | Define rights/obligations for using a service or site |
| **Licence Agreement / EULA** | Licensor ↔ licensee/user | Grant *permission to use* an asset while retaining ownership |
| **SLA** | Provider ↔ client | Quantitative service-quality commitments |

### Founders' Agreement

> [!info] Definition
> The ==founders' agreement== aligns founders on **vision, values, roles, and authorities**, defines **profit and share distribution**, sets **protections if a founder leaves**, and establishes **decision-making and dispute-resolution mechanisms**.

In doing so it promotes transparency and collaboration and **protects the company's IP** — the elements that enable healthy, sustainable growth. This is the single instrument whose absence sank "Scope 360". ^founders-agreement

### Voting Rights

> [!info] Definition
> ==Voting rights== let shareholders (or other stakeholders) **vote on critical corporate decisions** — electing the board, approving mergers & acquisitions, amending bylaws, issuing new shares, approving dividends and executive compensation.

Rights **vary by share class**, so some shareholders can wield disproportionate influence (e.g. founders holding super-voting shares). Voting rights are how a shareholder **protects their interests** inside the company. ^voting-rights

### NDA — Non-Disclosure Agreement

> [!info] Definition
> An ==NDA== obligates the parties to keep shared information **confidential**, to prevent leakage of sensitive data. *Example:* an NDA between a company and a new employee barring disclosure of internal developments.

### Investment Agreement

> [!info] Definition
> An ==investment agreement== sets the **terms and conditions of an investment**: how and under what conditions the investor joins, how many shares they receive, what rights they get, and how they can influence management. It protects **both** founders and investors.

> [!example] The seven clauses every founder must recognise
> | Clause | What it does | Founder concern |
> |--------|--------------|-----------------|
> | **Valuation** | Sets the company's pre-investment value → drives how many shares the investor gets | A low valuation = more dilution |
> | **Share allocation** | Defines the investor's ownership % | Direct dilution of founders |
> | **Voting rights** | Investor's influence over big decisions | Loss of control |
> | **Veto rights** | Lets the investor **block** decisions (e.g. new share issuances) | Can freeze your fundraising |
> | **Preemptive rights** | Investor may join future rounds to **keep** their % | Pro-rata protection for the investor |
> | **Exit rights** | Investor's options on a sale or IPO | May force a sale timeline |
> | **Information rights** | Access to financial/operational data | Ongoing reporting burden |

### Due Diligence

> [!info] Definition
> ==Due diligence== is the process where potential investors/partners **examine all aspects of a company before** investing or closing a deal — financial status, client base, legal agreements, and IP assets.

Its function is to **verify representations are accurate** and surface hidden problems before money changes hands. This is precisely where the "legal debt" from Session 1 comes due: every skipped agreement, every IP left in a founder's name, every missing compliance step **surfaces in diligence** and can kill or reprice the deal. ^due-diligence

### Service Agreement & SLA

> [!info] Definitions
> A ==service agreement== defines the terms for a supplier providing services to a business client — responsibilities, timelines, financial conditions — and protects the company if the other side fails to comply.
>
> A ==Service Level Agreement (SLA)== is the **quantitative** layer inside (or alongside) it: it commits the provider to **measurable** service levels — response times, system availability, maximum downtime.

> [!example] A concrete SLA
> An internet provider commits to **99.9% uptime** and promises to **restore service within four hours** of an outage. The numbers matter because they let the client *measure* compliance and trigger **compensation or corrective action** if breached.

### Non-Compete Agreement

> [!info] Definition
> A ==non-compete agreement== prevents an employee from working for competitors or starting a competing business for a set period after leaving. Its purpose is to protect **trade secrets** and sensitive information.

> [!warning] Non-competes are often *unenforceable* in Israel
> Under the Israeli Supreme Court ruling in **AES v. Saar**, an employee's commitment not to compete with a former employer is **generally unenforceable** — *except* in narrow cases where it is justified, e.g. to protect **genuine trade secrets** or in return for **special compensation**. A founder cannot rely on a non-compete alone to lock in talent; the real protection comes from NDAs, IP assignment, and good incentives.

### The "website/product" trio: Privacy Policy, ToS, Licences

| Document | Legally required? | What it protects |
|----------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Privacy Policy** | **Yes** (e.g. under GDPR) | Discloses how personal data is collected, stored, used; protects users' privacy and your compliance |
| **Terms of Service (ToS / T&C)** | **No**, but strongly advisable | Defines users' rights and usage conditions; protects the company against misuse |
| **Licence Agreement** | Depends | Grants permission to use an asset (software, brand, IP) for payment/conditions while the **licensor keeps ownership** |
| **EULA** (End User Licence Agreement) | Typically | Defines terms of use for purchased software/digital products; limits the company's liability for misuse |

> [!tip] Required vs advisable — know the difference
> A **privacy policy is mandatory** the moment you touch personal data; skipping it is the GDPR risk (#4). A **ToS is optional in law but reckless to omit** — it is your shield against abusive users. This distinction is exam-friendly: *which documents does the law force on you, and which do you adopt out of self-interest?*

### IP Licensing

> [!info] IP Licence
> An ==IP licence== grants the right to use a **patent or trademark**, enabling broad distribution **while the owner retains ownership**.

| Licence type | What it permits | Example |
|--------------|-----------------|---------|
| **Software licence** | Use software under set conditions; owner controls updates/distribution | Adobe Photoshop for personal use |
| **Reseller licence** | Another company resells the product to its own customers | Apple → Best Buy reselling iPhone/iPad/Mac |
| **Open-source licence** | Free use and distribution; often monetised via paid premium add-ons | WordPress free, with paid plugins/themes |

## 💡 Intellectual Property & Ownership

### Intellectual Property Rights

> [!info] Definition
> ==Intellectual property rights== protect inventions, creations, and trademarks from unauthorised use. They include **patents, copyrights, design rights, trade secrets, and trademarks**.

For a startup, IP is frequently the *entire* asset base — which is why Session 1 stressed that **IP must sit inside the company**, not in a founder's personal name. ^ip-rights

### Ownership & Digital Assets

> [!info] Definitions
> ==Ownership== is the legal right to **possess and use** an asset, including the ability to **transfer** that right. It applies to physical assets (land, equipment) and digital assets (websites, software), and grants the owner the authority to decide use, profit from, or sell the asset.
>
> ==Ownership of digital assets== covers all ownership rights over digital property — websites, apps, social-media accounts, images, videos. These can be **traded, transferred, or protected** via IP rights (copyright, patents) to safeguard their economic value.

### Open Source

> [!info] Definition
> ==Open source== is software code **freely available to the public** that anyone can use, modify, and redistribute. It promotes knowledge-sharing and innovation — but recall **Risk #5**: licence terms still bind you, and *copyleft* obligations can force disclosure of your own code.

### Vesting & ESOP — aligning employees with the company

> [!info] Vesting
> ==Vesting== rights are granted **gradually over time**, typically for equity-based employee compensation. A startup may *promise* an employee shares, but ownership only **transfers after a set period** — encouraging retention and aligning the employee's incentives with the company's success.

> [!info] ESOP — Employee Stock Ownership Plan
> An ==ESOP== lets employees receive company shares under specific conditions, giving them a **sense of ownership** and a stake in the company's growth (just like other investors). It is a financial incentive to **retain employees and attract top talent**, and it operates on a **vesting schedule** — employees earn shares gradually, reinforcing long-term commitment.

> [!tip] Why vesting protects the *company*, not just the employee
> Without vesting, a co-founder or early hire who leaves after two months keeps their full equity forever — "dead equity" that demotivates everyone who stays and clutters the cap table. A **typical vesting schedule** (e.g. 4 years with a 1-year cliff) means a departing person earns only the fraction of equity proportional to their tenure. Vesting is the structural answer to the "Scope 360" departure problem.

## 💰 Pillar (cross-cutting) — Fundraising & Valuation

### Fundraising and the investor ladder

> [!info] Definition
> ==Fundraising== is the process of raising capital to support operations — from personal equity, private investors, venture capital funds, and crowdfunding. External financing **reduces personal financial risk**, **increases investment scale**, and brings the **expertise and connections** of experienced investors.

| Investor type | Stage | Source of money | Involvement |
|---------------|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Angel Investors** | Very early — idea still in development | Personal wealth | Low day-to-day; offer mentorship, connections, guidance |
| **Venture Capital (VC)** | Later — product exists, demand proven | Funds from institutions (pension funds, banks) | **High** — involved in management and strategic decisions |
| **Public markets (IPO)** | Mature | The investing public | Reporting/transparency obligations to all shareholders |

> [!example] Angel vs VC in one line
> An **angel** writes an early cheque on belief in the idea (e.g. seed funding for a new AI photo app for a 10% stake). A **VC fund** invests larger sums *after* the product and market are proven, takes shares, **and a seat at the table**. The trade-off: more capital, but more control given up.

### IPO and Exit Strategy

> [!info] Definitions
> An ==IPO (Initial Public Offering)== is the process of offering shares to the public **for the first time** and beginning to trade on a stock exchange. It raises substantial capital but imposes **strict regulatory and reporting requirements** and far greater transparency.
>
> An ==exit strategy== is the founders'/owners' plan for **leaving the company or realising a profit** — defining how and when the company will be sold, exit the market, or go public via IPO.

### Anti-Dilution Provisions

> [!info] Definition
> ==Anti-dilution provisions== protect an existing shareholder when the company later **issues new shares at a lower price** than they paid. The mechanism **reduces the conversion price** of their shares, preserving their relative ownership and investment value.

> [!tip] The intuition
> If you invest at \$10/share and the next ("down") round prices shares at \$5, your stake is worth less and your ownership % shrinks. Anti-dilution effectively **re-prices your earlier shares closer to the new, lower price**, restoring some of the ownership you'd otherwise lose. It is one of the investor-protection clauses inside the investment agreement above.

### Valuation

> [!info] Definition
> ==Valuation== is the **monetary value of a company** as assessed by the market or a professional evaluation — how much the company is "worth" for investment or sale.

Valuation directly drives fundraising: investors must know the value to determine what **ownership % they get for their money**. Crucially:

$$
\text{Ownership \%} = \frac{\text{Investment amount}}{\text{Post-money valuation}}
$$

$$
\text{Post-money} = \text{Pre-money} + \text{Investment}
$$

> [!example] Higher valuation → less dilution
> A startup valued at **\$10M** that raises **\$1M** gives up
> $$\boxed{\frac{\$1\text{M}}{\$10\text{M}+\$1\text{M}} \approx 9\% \ \text{(}\approx 10\%\text{ as framed in the slide)}}$$
> As the company proves itself, valuation rises, so the **same \$1M buys a smaller slice** — the founder keeps more of the company. This is the financial reason Session 1 said *"later money is cheaper to the founder."*

#### Methods for calculating valuation

| Family | Method | How it works | Worked example |
|--------|--------|--------------|----------------|
| **Multiples & comparison** | Profit/revenue multiple | Apply an industry multiple to annual profit or revenue | Profit \$1M × multiple 5 = **\$5M** |
| | Comparable transactions | Value off recently sold/valued similar companies | A peer sold for \$10M ⇒ value ≈ **\$10M** |
| **Revenue / cost / user** | Revenue-based | Annual revenue × multiple (suits fast-growing startups) | Revenue \$2M × 4 = **\$8M** |
| | Entry-barrier (replacement cost) | Cost to **replicate** what the company built (dev + tech) | Dev cost \$2M ⇒ value ≈ **\$2M** |
| | User-based | Number of users × value per user | 100,000 users × \$20 = **\$2M** |

> [!warning] No single "true" valuation
> These methods can give wildly different numbers for the same company — a profitable firm and a pre-revenue user-growth firm are valued on entirely different logics. Valuation is a **negotiation anchored by a method**, not an objective fact. Pick the method that fits the company's stage and story.

## 🔧 Cloud Computing for Founders

Modern ventures rarely buy servers; they **rent computing via the cloud**. Knowing the service models matters for cost, control, and the **contracts** (and SLAs) you sign with providers.

> [!info] Cloud computing
> ==Cloud computing== is accessing computing power and digital resources **over the internet** instead of buying and maintaining physical servers, storage, and infrastructure — giving flexible, on-demand access.

### IaaS vs PaaS vs SaaS

| Model | You manage | Provider manages | Analogy | Use when… | Example |
|-------|-----------|------------------|---------|-----------|---------|
| **IaaS** (Infrastructure) | OS, apps, data | Servers, storage, networking, maintenance, security | Renting **empty rooms** to set up your own business | You need direct control of servers/infrastructure | AWS, Google Cloud |
| **PaaS** (Platform) | Your application & data | OS, databases, dev tools, infrastructure | Renting a **fully equipped kitchen** — tools ready, no maintenance | You want to build/run an app without managing servers | Google App Engine |
| **SaaS** (Software) | Nothing (just use it) | Everything — software delivered over the web | **Renting access** to software via your browser | You just need to *use* finished software | Asana, Google Workspace |

> [!tip] The ladder of control vs convenience
> Moving **IaaS → PaaS → SaaS**, you hand over more management and gain more convenience. IaaS = maximum control, maximum responsibility; SaaS = zero installation, zero control over the stack. A founder picks the rung that matches how much of the stack the product actually needs to own.

## 🏛️ Pillar 3 — Regulation

Regulation is the **hard law** (Session 1) the venture cannot opt out of. Three areas recur for startups:

> [!info] Regulatory Compliance
> ==Regulatory compliance== means adhering to the laws and regulations required by the state — across consumer protection, privacy, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, environmental rules, and more. Failure risks **fines and penalties**, so companies build procedures and oversight to ensure they meet legal requirements.

> [!info] Employment Law
> ==Employment (labor) law== protects workers' rights and regulates the employer–employee relationship — minimum wage, working hours, vacation days, and protection against workplace discrimination. Its purpose is **fair and equal working conditions** for all employees.

> [!info] Antitrust (Competition) Law
> ==Antitrust law== regulates corporate activity to **prevent monopolistic concentration** and promote fair competition. *Example:* in Israel, the law restricts dominant companies from merging with competitors in the same market to preserve competition.

## 📝 Summary

- Law is the **anchor** of a venture: it converts informal expectations into enforceable structure. The "Scope 360" story shows that **postponing legal work compounds into disputes, financial damage, and lost investors**. ^three-pillars
- A founder's legal universe has **three pillars**: **business structure**, **contracts**, and **regulation** — and five core risks (no founders' agreement, bad corporate structure, no NDAs, neglecting privacy, improper open-source use). ^five-risks
- **Structure:** a ==corporation== gives ==limited liability== and lets you issue shares; startups default to the **Ltd./LLC** over partnerships (personal liability) and public corporations (heavy reporting). Subsidiaries (parent owns >50%) ring-fence operations. ^corporate-structure
- **Contracts** regulate relationships: the ==founders' agreement== is the most important early document; ==NDAs== protect secrets; ==investment agreements== carry seven key clauses (valuation, share allocation, voting, veto, preemptive, exit, information rights); ==non-competes== are largely **unenforceable in Israel** (*AES v. Saar*). Privacy policies are **mandatory**, ToS merely advisable. ^investment-agreement
- **IP & ownership** are often the startup's whole value: IP rights (patents, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets) must sit **inside the company**. ==Vesting== and ==ESOP== align employees by granting equity gradually. ^vesting-esop
- **Fundraising** climbs angels → VC → IPO, trading capital for control; ==valuation== drives dilution ($\text{ownership} = \text{investment} / \text{post-money}$), is computed by **multiples, comparison, revenue, replacement-cost, or per-user** methods, and is protected by ==anti-dilution== clauses. ^valuation
- **Cloud:** IaaS → PaaS → SaaS trades control for convenience. **Regulation** (compliance, employment law, antitrust) is hard law the venture must obey. ^regulation
- The session's conclusion — what law *does* for a founder: **protect rights, prevent risk, regulate relationships, enable capital raising and expansion, build trust, and ensure regulatory compliance.**
