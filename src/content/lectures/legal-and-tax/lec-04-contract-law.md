## 🎯 Why This Session Exists

[[Lec_03-Company Law & Corporate Structures|Session 3]] built the *vessel* (the company); contracts are the **bloodstream** that runs through every venture — founders' agreements, employment, NDAs, leases, investment rounds, EULAs all live or die on contract law. A founder needs to know three things: **when a binding contract actually exists**, **how a court will read it when the parties later disagree**, and **how far "we agreed to it" can take you before the law refuses to help**. This session answers all three.

> [!tip] The thread running through the whole session
> Contract law is a constant tug-of-war between **[[Freedom of Contract|freedom of contract]]** (let people make their own deals — Section 24) and **[[Public Policy|public policy]]** (the state's refusal to enforce deals that harm society or the weak — Section 30). Every doctrine here — capacity, intention, interpretation, the writing requirement, completion mechanisms — is the law deciding *where on that spectrum to stand*. The recurring lesson: **substance beats form**, and **freedom of contract today is "weak and eroded."**

## 🤝 Part 1 — What Is a Contract?

> [!info] Definition
> A ==contract== is a **legally binding agreement** between two or more parties — a *promise or set of promises* that the law will **enforce**. In plain terms: an understanding in which people agree to do (or not do) something, and **if they fail to perform, the law may intervene**. ^contract-definition

The defining word is **enforceable**. Many agreements exist without legal force (see Part 2); what turns an understanding into a *contract* is that the state stands behind it.

### Sources of contract law

Contracts have deep historical roots developed over centuries:

- **Roman law** — the principle ==*pacta sunt servanda*== ("agreements must be kept") is the bedrock.
- **Common law** — developed in England through **judicial precedent** (judge-made law).
- **Jewish law** — a further historical source feeding Israel's [[Mixed Jurisdiction|mixed jurisdiction]].

### Conditions for a valid contract

> [!info] The five (in Israel, four) conditions
> A contract requires:
> 1. ==Offer and acceptance== — one party proposes terms, the other agrees (*Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*).
> 2. ==Intention to create legal relations== — both parties must mean the deal to be legally binding (*CA 440/75 Zandbank v. Danziger*).
> 3. ==Legal capacity== — the parties must be legally able to contract (not minors, not mentally incapacitated).
> 4. ==Legality of purpose== — the contract must not violate the law or public policy (*Kestenbaum*).
> 5. ==Certainty / definiteness== — the contract must be specific enough to be enforced (*CA 77/158 Hava Rabinai*).

> [!warning] Consideration is NOT required in Israeli law
> In **common-law** systems, a contract needs ==consideration== — each side must exchange *something of value* (money, goods, services). **Israeli contract law dropped this requirement.** A gratuitous promise can still bind (this is why the **Gift Law** and cases like *Barashi* turn on *serious intention* rather than on consideration). Don't import the common-law consideration rule into an Israeli exam answer. ^contract-conditions

## 📑 Part 2 — Types of Contracts & the Consent/Contract Distinction

### Obligatory vs. real transactions

> [!info] Two transaction archetypes
> - An ==obligatory transaction== **faces the future** and carries **risk**: performance (and compensation) happens *later*, not at the moment of agreement. *Example:* a contractor selling an apartment **"on paper"** before it is built.
> - A ==real transaction== is the old marketplace model: each party shows up with goods and **exchanges immediately**. There is **no risk** — everyone is compensated **on the spot**.
>
> Every contract carries **entry and exit costs**. ^transaction-types

The entrepreneurial world runs almost entirely on **obligatory transactions** (investment, employment, SaaS subscriptions, founders' vesting) — precisely the risky, future-facing deals that need contract law's protection.

### Common contract types in a venture

Employment contract · lease agreement · sales contract · service agreement · founders' agreement · ==NDA (confidentiality)== · loan agreement · ==EULA== (end-user licence) · ==Terms of Use (ToU/ToS/T&C)== · real-estate contract · insurance policy · partnership agreement · investment agreements.

### Consent ≠ Contract

> [!info] Consent is broader than contract
> At the base of every agreement lies ==consent== — two parties agreeing on something. But **consent does not automatically become a contract or even a legally recognized agreement**. The concept of consent is *wider* than both "agreement" and "contract." ^consent-vs-contract

> [!example] The classic non-contract: a political deal
> Two Members of Knesset agree to vote for a bill; one later changes his mind and votes against. There are **consequences (reputation)** but **no legal consequences** — there was consent, but **no intention to create legal relations**, so no contract.

> [!warning] Agreements the law will NOT enforce
> Even where consent exists, a contract fails (is void or voidable) when it is:
> 1. **Contrary to law** — e.g. a drug-sale contract or an agreement to launder money.
> 2. **Missing formal requirements** — e.g. a real-estate contract not in writing as the law demands.
> 3. **Based on coercion, deception, or abuse** — e.g. exploiting a supplier's financial distress to force an unfair deal.
> 4. **Containing unfair clauses** — e.g. a lease letting the landlord evict the tenant with no notice or reason.

### Why the state must intervene at all

> [!info] Enforcement = legal validity
> The element of ==enforceability== is what separates a contract from a mere understanding. Individuals get **great freedom** to act — yet the **state must sometimes intervene**, because without enforcement, promises about the *future* (obligatory transactions) would be worthless and no one would risk relying on them. ^enforceability

> [!example] *CA 3158/20 Achi Tekuma Ltd. v. Daniel Meshulam* — you can't enforce a hidden illegal deal
> A 2004 sale agreement transferred lease rights for **\$305,000**. The seller claimed the *real* deal was different — he was secretly promised **three apartments**, undocumented **for tax reasons**, and the cash was never actually paid.
> **Justice Grosskopf (Supreme Court):** you **cannot legitimize an illegal transaction by concealing it**. The parties' intention at formation was to act in a **clearly illegal manner**, so **even the hidden contract is void** as an illegal contract — no enforcement. *Lesson:* illegality of purpose (condition 4) **kills the contract**, and dressing it up cannot save it.

## ⚖️ Part 3 — Special Contract Laws & the "Cry of Fairness"

Beyond the general statute, specific contract types get **their own protective laws** under the principle ==*lex specialis*== (the specific law overrides the general):

> [!info] The special-contracts regime
> **Consumer Protection Law · Standard Contracts Law · Insurance Contracts Law · Gifts Law · Sale Law · Banking Law.** Each exists because one side is structurally **weaker** and needs extra protection. ^special-contracts

Examples of the heightened requirements:

- **Spouses** — a prenuptial agreement requires a **notary**.
- **Standard contracts** — stricter protection against the **stronger drafting party** (think SaaS click-wrap terms).
- **Sale of real estate** — must be **in writing** (with the narrow exception of *Kalmer v. Gai*, Part 7).

This is the **"cry of fairness"**: the law refuses to treat a consumer signing a 40-page bank form as the equal of the bank that wrote it.

## 🗽 Part 4 — Fundamental Principles: Freedom vs. Its Limits

### Freedom of contract (Section 24)

> [!info] Section 24 — Freedom of contract
> "The content of a contract may be **whatever the parties agree upon**." This bundles **freedom of design** (freedom to *form* a contract at all) and **freedom of content** (freedom to set its terms). ^freedom-of-contract

### Can freedom be absolute? — No.

> [!tip] Aharon Barak's principle: no right is absolute
> Barak taught that **freedom of contract cannot be absolute** — every right must be **balanced** against competing rights and freedoms. The real question is *which way the court leans*. The course's blunt conclusion: **today the court prefers other freedoms at the expense of freedom of contract** — freedom of contract is **"quite weak and quite eroded."** ^no-absolute-freedom

> [!example] The Barak–Zamir debate (*Rekant* case)
> Can a contractual clause override the principle of **equality** (here, age-based)?
> - **Justice Barak:** constitutional values like **equality prevail** over freedom of contract — fundamental rights can limit contractual autonomy.
> - **Justice Zamir:** warns this **"endangers the status of contract law,"** because excessive judicial intervention undermines the **predictability and stability** of contracts.
>
> In *Association of Insurance Appraisers v. The Insurance Commissioner*, Zamir made the paradox explicit: *sometimes you must limit freedom of contract in the name of freedom of contract itself.* The resolution is **proper judicial discretion** to find the **balance point** — fairness without gutting contractual autonomy.

### Public policy & illegality (Section 30)

> [!info] Section 30 — the void contract
> A contract is **void** when it is **illegal, immoral, or contrary to public policy** (*Egged v. Mashiach*). Sections 24 and 30 are the **two poles**: ==Section 24== (freedom) versus ==Section 30== (public policy). The clash between them maps the **conservative vs. liberal** divide on the bench. ^public-policy

> [!example] *Egged v. Mashiach* — indirect restraints count too
> An **indirect** contractual restriction on **freedom of occupation**. The court was unimpressed that the restraint was indirect: *even an indirect restriction implied by a Basic Law is equivalent to a direct restriction* on freedom of occupation. You cannot evade public policy by being subtle about it.

### A contract cannot contradict cogent (mandatory) provisions

> [!warning] Some rules can't be contracted away
> ==Cogent provisions== are mandatory legal rules that a contract **may not override**, no matter what the parties agree (*Zim v. Maziar*; *Lagil Trampoline*; *Levin v. Levin*).

> [!example] Three cogent-provision cases
> - **_Zim v. Maziar_ (Justice Zilber):** freedom of contract matters, but it stands **below the sanctity of life**. Public policy voids exemption clauses of that kind — the court doesn't *abolish* freedom of contract, but **life-and-death matters nullify** such waivers.
> - **_Lagil Trampoline v. Nahmias_:** a liability-waiver sign had **no validity** — (a) it wasn't part of the contract (the sign's text appeared *inside the facility*, not at **ticket purchase**, the moment of formation), and (b) **sanctity of life** cannot be waived on a sign.
> - **_Levin v. Levin_ (Justice Zamir):** a clause **barring the parties from going to court** contradicts public policy — the court "will not lend its hand" to a contract that blocks **access to justice**, as that breeds anarchy.

### The market-overt rule (Section 34, Sale Law)

> [!info] Section 34 — protecting the good-faith buyer
> The ==market-overt rule== can give a **good-faith buyer** good title even when they bought from someone who wasn't the true owner — but property rights are guarded strictly, so the conditions are demanding. ^market-overt

> [!info] The four conditions for a market-overt transaction
> 1. **Good faith (subjective)** — acting honestly, unaware of any defect in title.
> 2. **For value** — the buyer gave consideration.
> 3. **Good faith at the time the asset is transferred.**
> 4. The asset was bought from someone who **appeared to be the seller**, and the sale was in the **ordinary course of that seller's business**.

> [!example] *Knaan* — the flea-market Reuven Rubin
> A woman bought a painting for **250 shekels** at a flea market; it turned out to be a **stolen Reuven Rubin**. Owner vs. museum — who wins? **The museum.** She had to **return the painting**, because the sale was **not in the ordinary course of the seller's business** (he didn't normally sell such paintings), so condition 4 failed. Property rights are "stronger than contractual rights," so the bar is high.

## 🏗️ Part 5 — The Statutory Architecture & the Life of a Contract

> [!info] The two governing statutes
> Israeli contract law rests on two acts:
> - **Contracts (General Part) Law, 1973** — 60 sections (formation, interpretation, validity).
> - **Contracts (Remedies for Breach) Law, 1970** — 20 sections (what happens when it goes wrong).

> [!info] The four chronological stages of a contract
> 1. **Pre-contractual** — offer, negotiations, expressions of interest.
> 2. **Formation** — the "signing" stage. *Note:* a contract can exist **without a physical signature**, and a contract may exist but **not yet take effect**.
> 3. **Performance** — doing what the contract requires.
> 4. **Termination** (everything completed) **or breach** (something went wrong). ^contract-lifecycle

## ✅ Part 6 — Before Formation: Capacity & Intention

### First test — legal capacity

> [!info] The capacity gate
> The **first test** before a contract can form is ==legal capacity==: is the person legally competent to contract? **Minors and legally incapacitated persons cannot express rational will** (Sections 3, 4 and 8, Legal Capacity and Guardianship Law). ^capacity

### Second test — intention to create legal relations

This is the **precondition** and one of the most exam-heavy doctrines. Two complementary lenses — **intention** and **specificity** — work together; only in exceptional cases do they contradict (a vague approach vs. a detailed offer). Case law built **two tests**:

> [!info] The Theoretical Test (*Levin*)
> Examine the **parties' intent and consent** to establish legal relations. **Justice Zamir (Levin):** *"If the parties have made up their minds to create legal obligations that grant rights or impose duties within the realm of law, then it is a contract."* The driver is the **autonomy of individual will**. This test is **not in any statute** — it's a product of **case law**, inferred from the circumstances. ^theoretical-test

> [!info] The Practical Test
> The parties' intent is **inferred from objective indicators**:
> - **The language** of the agreement (written or oral) — *the text*.
> - **The circumstances and background** — *the context*.
>
> Neither stands alone: *"a word cannot stand alone without context, and the circumstances cannot be interpreted without supporting language."* ^practical-test

> [!example] *Levin v. Levin* — substance beats an express "no legal effect" clause
> A divorcing couple drafted a **separate alimony agreement** and wrote that it would have **no legal validity**. The husband promised alimony **above the legal minimum**, then stopped paying.
> **Justice Zamir:** the agreement **is legally binding**. Under the intention test, **substance (true purpose + circumstances) outweighs form (the wording)**. Even though they wrote "no legal effect," the context showed a **genuine intent to create obligations**. *Lesson:* you cannot disclaim legal effect by mere words if everything else points to a real deal.

## 🔍 Part 7 — Contract Interpretation: The *Apropim* Revolution

This is the single most important interpretive doctrine in Israeli contract law.

### Section 25(a) — the original two-step rule

> [!info] Section 25(a) (original wording)
> "A contract shall be interpreted **according to the intent of the parties, as implied from the contract itself**, and insofar as it is not implied from it — **from the circumstances**." This created a **two-step test**: examine the **language first**; only if intent is unclear, look to **external circumstances**. ^section-25-original

### *Apropim* — Barak rewrites the order

> [!info] *Apropim* (Justice Barak): the pendulum model
> Barak argued the legislator was **mistaken**. Interpretation should begin with the **circumstances**, move to the **language**, then return to the **circumstances** — a ==pendulum model==. The circumstances are the **more significant tool** for uncovering true intent. His signature line: saying *"the language is clear"* marks the **end** of the interpretive process, **not its beginning**. ^apropim-doctrine

> [!example] The facts behind *Apropim*
> To house the 1990s wave of immigration from the former USSR, the **Ministry of Housing** promised contractors that the state would **buy unsold apartments** — up to **50% in central Israel**, up to **100% in the periphery**. The contract spelled out the **deduction for delay** clearly for the **center**, but for the **periphery** it specified the deduction **only for a delay in submitting the purchase request**, not for a construction delay. The gap forced the court to decide *how* to read the contract — by language alone, or by purpose and circumstances.

> [!info] Barak's conclusion
> "The transition from the **internal source** (the contract's language) to the **external source** (the surrounding circumstances) is **not subject to any preliminary conditions**. No prior examination is required to determine whether the language is clear — that question becomes clear only at the **end** of the interpretive process."

> [!example] *Vegetable Growers Association* — a partial pushback
> **Justice Rivlin (majority):** the **goal** is always the parties' intent, and the **starting point** is the **language** — "the vessel containing the parties' intent" and *"sometimes the central evidence."* A contract **cannot** be given a meaning its language **cannot bear**. The court moves **back and forth** between language and circumstances, subject to a **rebuttable presumption** that the contract's purpose is what its **ordinary wording** suggests. This softens *Apropim* toward the text without abandoning the pendulum.

### The 2011 amendment of Section 25

> [!info] Why Section 25 was amended (2011)
> Critics said *Apropim* created **legal uncertainty**, invited **litigation**, and undermined the principle that the **written agreement is the decisive expression** of intent — leaving courts free to pick between competing readings. The 2011 amendment aimed to restore **certainty** while preserving the parties' true bargain. ^section-25-amendment

> [!info] Section 25 — current wording (post-2011)
> **(a)** A contract is interpreted **according to the intent of the parties as implied from the contract *and* from the circumstances** — *however*, **if the intent is explicitly implied from the language, the contract is interpreted according to its language**.
> **(b)** Where multiple interpretations are possible, prefer the one that **upholds validity** over one that voids it.
> **(b1)** Where one party had an **advantage in drafting** (e.g. a standard form), prefer the interpretation **against that party** (*contra proferentem*).
> **(c)** Customary terms are read by their **customary meaning** in such contracts.

> [!tip] Did the amendment actually change anything?
> Largely a **codification of the back-and-forth**: language and circumstances are now read **together**, but a **clear** linguistic intent governs (a nod back toward the text). The deeper interpretive freedom *Apropim* unleashed never fully went away. Note also the **completion tools** that fill gaps: **Section 39** (good faith), **Section 26** (custom between the parties, then custom in similar contracts), and complementary statutes like the **Sale Law**. ^did-amendment-change

> [!info] Section 26 — filling gaps by custom
> "Details **not determined** in the contract shall be according to the **custom between the parties**, and absent that — the **custom common in contracts of the same type**; these details are **deemed agreed upon**." This is the statutory engine of *contract completion* (Part 9).

## ✍️ Part 8 — Formation: Offer, Acceptance & Serious Intention

> [!info] Section 1 — how a contract is born
> "A contract is formed by way of ==offer and acceptance==." Two elements are then tested: **serious intention (gmirot da'at)** — the *mental* element — and **definiteness (mesuyamut)** — the *factual* element.

### Offer and acceptance mechanics

> [!info] Sections 2 & 5 — defining offer and acceptance
> - **Section 2 (Offer):** a communication is an **offer** if it shows the offeror's ==serious intention (gmirot da'at)== to contract **and** is **sufficiently definite** to allow formation by acceptance. An offer may be made **to the public** (*Shamsian*).
> - **Section 5 (Acceptance):** acceptance is by a **notice from the offeree delivered to the offeror**, showing serious intention to contract **in accordance with the offer**.

> [!warning] Formation happens at *delivery* of acceptance — not at the meeting of minds
> Even if minds met on **Sunday**, if the **notice** of acceptance is delivered only on **Tuesday**, the contract forms **on Tuesday**. And **Section 60(b)** makes **constructive notice** enough: a notice is "delivered" when it **reaches the recipient or their address** — so a letter sitting **unread in your mailbox** already concluded the contract. ^offer-acceptance

> [!tip] The offeree holds the stronger hand
> Once the offeror makes an offer, they are **bound by it** and **depend on the offeree's decision** — they have **tied themselves** to the terms. The offeree can take it or leave it. That asymmetry matters in negotiations (e.g. a real-estate sale).

### Serious intention (*gmirot da'at*) — the objective test

> [!info] The test for serious intention
> ==Serious intention (gmirot da'at)== is the **mental element** (loosely, the *mens rea* of contract). The test is **objective** (*Barashi*). *Rabinai* says examine intention **"exactly at the moment of signing."** Barak (in *Apropim*) conceded that a perfect overlap of wills "never truly exists" — there is always a **meeting of the minds** somewhere in between — **but** if the evidence shows an **identical subjective intention** at formation, *that* governs (rare), even if the parties objectively appeared to intend otherwise. ^gmirot-daat

> [!example] *Peretz Bonei HaNegev v. Bochbot* (CA 1932/90)
> Bochbot contracted to sell his apartment (lived in by his son) in **three installments**; he cashed only part of the first payment and returned the rest. He claimed he **couldn't read or write** and didn't understand the deal — that he thought signing would earn him **\$1,000 compensation**, not that it was a **sales contract**. *Legal questions:* is the contract valid, and did the father have **gmirot da'at**? (He withdrew the illiteracy argument on appeal.) The case probes whether the **objective** act of signing reflected a **true intention** to sell.

> [!example] *Barashi* (CA 3601/96) — when the objective test isn't enough
> Relatives had a **mentally and physically unstable, ill** elderly man sign a **gift contract** transferring his assets to them — drafted entirely by them, signed **in a car**, ~18 months **after** he'd made a sound-minded **will** saying something different. He was never declared incompetent.
> - **District Court:** at signing he **lacked understanding** of the documents' meaning — legally, **no gmirot da'at** — so the transactions were **invalid**; also **unconscionability/exploitation**.
> - **Supreme Court (Justice Beinisch):** appellate courts rarely disturb factual findings; the gift **promise counts as a contract** (the Gift Law is silent on gmirot da'at, so the **Contracts Law** applies). Crucially, **the objective test is insufficient where the signer has impaired capacity** — the external act of signing **does not reflect true will**. **Appeal dismissed.** *Lesson:* the objective test bends for vulnerable parties.

## 📐 Part 9 — Definiteness, Completion & the Writing Requirement

### Definiteness (*mesuyamut*)

> [!info] The factual element
> ==Definiteness (mesuyamut)== asks whether the agreement is **specific enough** to enforce — the *actus reus* of contract. Crucially, a contract **need not be perfectly complete** if serious intention is proven; courts have **completion mechanisms** to fill gaps. ^mesuyamut

> [!example] *Hof HaTchelet* (Blue Coast, CA 133/89) — completing a missing plot
> The contract sold "**one of**" the seller's plots without specifying **which**. On execution the seller claimed it was a mere **MOU** lacking definiteness. **The court completed it** and transferred ownership.
> On **missing price**, the court drew a sharp line: if the absence of a price reflects an **underlying disagreement**, it signals **no consent** (a deliberate "negative arrangement") — the court **cannot complete it**. But if the parties simply **didn't know the market value** at the time, the court **will complete** by setting a **reasonable price**.

> [!info] Essential terms of a real-estate deal — and the *Dor Energy* erosion
> Traditional case law required: **nature of the property, date of delivery, nature of the transaction, payment dates** (plus taxes and expenses). ==*Dor Energy*== **"eroded"** these into just **three minimal essentials**: **(1) date of delivery, (2) price, (3) nature of the transaction.** A missing appendix was held **minor**; Justice Or completed it via completion mechanisms and custom — a **binding contract had formed**. ^dor-energy

> [!info] The five completion mechanisms
> Where there is serious intention + definiteness but **one detail is missing**, a court can complete it via:
> 1. **Agreed mechanism** — the parties pre-specified how to fill it (e.g. "per an appraiser").
> 2. **Dispositive legal provisions** — statutory defaults (e.g. **Section 46**: a **reasonable price**).
> 3. **Principle of optimal performance** — where a party tries to **withdraw** over a missing detail (as in *Dor Energy*), complete it **in favor of performance** so the contract survives.
> 4. **Custom (usage)** — complete per **similar agreements**.
> 5. **Reasonableness & good faith** — complete per accepted standards and **Section 39** good faith. ^completion-mechanisms

> [!tip] The definiteness takeaway
> *"There is no need for the contract to be perfectly complete if serious intention has been proven."* If gmirot da'at is clear, courts **strain to save** the deal (*Dor Energy*) rather than let a party escape on a technicality — what the slides call avoiding "pettiness" that "ruins a deal."

### The writing requirement

> [!info] Section 8, Land Law — writing is constitutive for real estate
> "A transaction in **real estate** requires a **written document**; in its absence, the transaction is **invalid**." For land, writing isn't just evidence — it's a **condition of validity**. ^writing-requirement

> [!info] Section 12 — good faith in negotiation
> "In negotiating a contract, a person shall act in an **accepted manner and in good faith**; a party who did not shall be **liable for the damage** caused." Bad-faith negotiation has consequences **even before a contract forms**.

> [!example] *Kalmer v. Gai* — when good faith softens the writing rule
> A **combination transaction** with **incomplete documents**. **Justice Barak (majority):** in **special and exceptional** circumstances, the writing requirement can be **relaxed through good faith**; where the **writing principle conflicts with good faith, good faith prevails** — *provided two conditions*:
> 1. **Change of position** — the promisee actually **relied** on the contract and **began performing/acting** on it.
> 2. **Degree of fault** of the withdrawing party — whether the party now backing out is the one who **induced the reliance**.
>
> Barak framed this as a **"flexible balance"**: the writing requirement must be applied **with great care**, and only in **extreme** cases strengthened. It expresses the system's **"monarchic" balance** of **caution with flexibility**. *Exception to the rule, not the rule.*

## 📝 Part 10 — Memoranda of Understanding (MOU)

> [!info] Is an MOU binding?
> A ==memorandum of understanding (MOU)== is an **interim agreement**, and **the parties' intent determines its legal effect**. It may show **intent to contract**, **no intent**, or a **non-binding intent / suspensive condition**. An MOU can be **a stage in negotiations** that a party may still **withdraw from** — or it can already be a **binding contract**. The decider is, again, **intention + definiteness** (*Rabinai*). ^mou

> [!example] *Rabinai* — the MOU that *was* a contract
> A short MOU (signed 20.1.72) named the **seller, the buyer (Man Sheked Ltd.), the lot, the total price (275,000 IL), an advance (15,000 IL), and a payment schedule** tied to signing a "final contract." Despite calling itself a *memorandum*, it contained **all the essential terms** — so it satisfied **gmirot da'at + mesuyamut** and was treated as **binding**. The label "MOU" does not control; the **substance** does.

> [!info] Details needed to show specificity (certainty)
> Names of the parties · nature of the asset · nature of the transaction · consideration · payment dates · tax expenses. Per *Dor Energy*, **Sections 5–6 were relaxed** — the conservative ("all terms") vs. liberal ("minimal essentials") approaches define the spectrum. ^mou-specificity

## 📋 Summary

- A ==contract== is an **enforceable** agreement. It needs **offer & acceptance, intention to create legal relations, capacity, legality of purpose, and certainty** — but **NOT consideration** (Israel dropped the common-law rule). ^contract-conditions
- **Consent is broader than contract:** agreement without intention to be legally bound (political deals), or contracts that are illegal, informal, coerced, or unfair, are **not enforced**. *Achi Tekuma* — a concealed **illegal** contract is **void** regardless. ^consent-vs-contract
- **Special contract laws** (*lex specialis*: Consumer Protection, Standard Contracts, Insurance, Gifts, Sale, Banking) protect the **weaker party** — the "cry of fairness." ^special-contracts
- The master tension is ==Section 24== (**freedom of contract**) vs. ==Section 30== (**public policy / illegality**). **No right is absolute** (Barak); today freedom of contract is **"weak and eroded."** **Cogent provisions** can't be waived (*Zim v. Maziar*, *Lagil Trampoline*, *Levin* — sanctity of life, access to courts). The **market-overt rule (s.34)** protects good-faith buyers under **four strict conditions** (*Knaan*). ^public-policy
- Two statutes govern (**General Part 1973**, **Remedies 1970**); a contract runs **pre-contract → formation → performance → termination/breach**. ^contract-lifecycle
- **Before formation:** test **capacity** (no minors/incapacitated), then **intention to create legal relations** — a **theoretical test** (*Levin*: did they mean to bind?) and a **practical test** (language + circumstances). *Levin v. Levin*: **substance beats an express "no legal effect" clause.** ^theoretical-test
- **Interpretation:** *Apropim* (Barak) replaced the language-first rule with a **pendulum** (circumstances ↔ language ↔ circumstances); *Vegetable Growers* re-anchored the **language** as starting point; the **2011 amendment to s.25** codified reading both together while letting **clear language govern**, adding **validity-preserving** and **contra-proferentem** rules. Gaps fill via **s.26 (custom)** and **s.39 (good faith)**. ^apropim-doctrine
- **Formation = offer + acceptance (s.1).** An **offer (s.2)** needs **gmirot da'at + definiteness** and may be public; **acceptance (s.5)** forms the contract **on delivery of notice** (constructive notice suffices, s.60(b)). ^offer-acceptance
- **Serious intention (gmirot da'at)** is tested **objectively** (*Barashi*, *Rabinai*) — but the objective test **bends for impaired-capacity** signers (*Barashi*; *Peretz Bonei HaNegev*). ^gmirot-daat
- **Definiteness (mesuyamut)** need not be perfect: courts use **five completion mechanisms** (agreed mechanism, dispositive law incl. s.46 reasonable price, optimal performance, custom, reasonableness). *Dor Energy* cut real-estate essentials to **delivery date, price, transaction type**; *Hof HaTchelet* completes missing terms unless the gap signals **no consent**. ^completion-mechanisms
- **Writing:** **Section 8 Land Law** makes writing **constitutive** for real estate, but *Kalmer v. Gai* lets **good faith override** it in exceptional cases (reliance + fault). An **MOU** binds or not **per the parties' intent** — *Rabinai* shows a "memorandum" with all essential terms is a **real contract**. ^mou
