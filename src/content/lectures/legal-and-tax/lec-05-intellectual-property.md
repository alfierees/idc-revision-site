# Intellectual Property

> Part of: [[Legal and Tax Aspects]] | Key concepts: [[Property Right]], [[Intellectual Property Rights]], [[Copyright]], [[Patent]], [[Trade Secret]], [[Trademark]], [[Design (IP)]], [[Novelty]], [[Inventive Step]], [[Distinctiveness]], [[AI and Copyright]]

> [!abstract] Why this lecture matters
> For a founder, IP is usually the **only real asset** the company owns before it has revenue — the code, the brand, the invention, the secret recipe. Investors price a startup on the strength and *defensibility* of its IP. This lecture answers two practical questions: **which of the five IP regimes protects my asset**, and **what do I have to do (register? keep secret? just create?) to actually hold that protection**. Get the regime wrong and you either over-pay for protection you didn't need or — worse — disclose something that destroys the protection you could have had.

---

## 1. Foundation: What Is a Property Right?

Before IP makes sense you have to see that it is a *species of property right*, and inherit all the strength that property rights carry.

> [!info] Definition — Property right ^property-right
> A ==property right== is a legal right that grants a person **direct control over an asset**, tangible or intangible. It lets the holder **use** the asset, **derive benefit** from it, and **protect it against harm** caused by others.

### Why property rights are the "strongest" rights

Three characteristics make property rights more powerful than ordinary (e.g. contractual) rights:

| Characteristic | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| ==Strong & heightened protection== | One of the strongest rights in the legal system — protected more aggressively than contractual rights | A court will defend it forcefully against intrusion |
| ==Attached to a specific asset== | The right binds the *holder-to-asset* link, not just relations with other people | A landowner can enforce against **any** infringer, not only a person they contracted with |
| ==Enduring over time== | Generally independent of time or the identity of the holder | Ownership of a house does not expire when the holder changes; it **passes by inheritance** |

### Types of property rights

- **Absolute rights** — e.g. ==ownership== of real estate (the fullest bundle of control).
- **Limited rights** — e.g. a ==lease==, ==lien==, or ==tenancy== right (a carved-out slice of control).

> [!tip] The key distinction: property right vs contractual right ^property-vs-contract
> This is the single most important framing in the lecture. A **contractual right** binds **only the parties** to the agreement (*in personam*). A **property right** is valid **against the whole world** (*erga omnes* / *in rem*).
>
> *Practical upshot for IP:* once you hold a registered patent or trademark, you can stop **anyone** from infringing — you don't need to have a contract with them first. That "against everyone" quality is exactly why founders fight to convert know-how into registered IP rights.

| | Contractual right | Property right |
|---|---|---|
| **Binds** | Only the parties to the agreement | Everyone — the whole world |
| **Latin** | *in personam* | *in rem* / *erga omnes* |
| **Enforce against** | Your counterparty | Any infringer |
| **Example** | A supply agreement | Ownership of land; a patent |

## 2. Intellectual Property as a Modern Field

Property law is **ancient** — it has always regulated rights in **tangible** assets:

- ==Movables== (portable property: furniture, vehicles).
- ==Real estate== (immovable property: land, houses).

But property law sometimes reaches **intangible** assets too, and that is where IP lives.

> [!info] Intellectual property — a young discipline ^ip-modern
> IP is only about **200 years old**, began being **taught in universities only in the 1980s**, and developed mainly alongside **information and computer technologies**. It is a product of the modern era because the **digital economy and innovation** made intangible assets economically central.

> [!warning] The defining challenge — where is the boundary of the asset? ^ip-boundary
> With a house, the boundary is obvious (the walls). With IP it is **hard to define what counts as the protected asset** — an **abstract idea** versus a **concrete creation**. IP law spends most of its energy drawing that line: ideas are *not* protected, but a particular *expression*, *invention*, or *sign* is. Founders get this wrong constantly ("someone stole my idea") — ideas alone are free; only the protectable form is owned.

## 3. The Five Fields of Intellectual Property

> [!example] The map of IP ^five-fields
> Everything in this lecture sits in one of **five regimes**:
> 1. ==Copyright== — creative works
> 2. ==Patents== — technological inventions
> 3. ==Trade secrets== — confidential commercial information
> 4. ==Trademarks== — brand-identifying signs
> 5. ==Designs / industrial designs== — visual appearance of products

The whole point of the lecture is to pick the **right** regime for a given asset. The master comparison:

| Regime | Protects | Registration? | Duration | Core condition(s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Copyright** | Creative expression (art, music, text, **software**) | **No** — automatic on creation | Long (life + decades) | Originality / fixation in a work |
| **Patent** | Technological inventions | **Yes** — examined by Patent Authority | **20 years** from filing | Novelty + non-triviality + inventive step + applicability |
| **Trade secret** | Secret info with economic value | **No** — kept confidential | **Potentially forever** (while secret) | Secrecy + economic value + reasonable efforts to keep secret |
| **Trademark** | Brand signs (names, logos) | **Yes** — Trademarks registry | Renewable (effectively indefinite) | Distinctiveness + not descriptive/misleading |
| **Design** | Visual/aesthetic features of products | **Yes** — Designs registry | Limited period | Novel, unique visual character |

> [!tip] How to choose — the founder's decision tree
> - Is it **code, content, or a creative work**? → **Copyright** (you already have it, for free).
> - Is it a **technical invention** you can disclose and want a 20-year monopoly on? → **Patent**.
> - Is it a **technical advantage you can keep hidden** and would lose value if disclosed? → **Trade secret** (don't patent it — patenting *publishes* it).
> - Is it your **brand / name / logo**? → **Trademark**.
> - Is it the **look** of a physical product? → **Design**.

## 4. Copyright

> [!info] Definition — Copyright ^copyright
> ==Copyright== is an old legal field protecting **creators of works** in the arts, culture, and software.
> **Purposes:** (1) give creators **exclusive protection** over their works; (2) **encourage creativity** by granting an **economic incentive**.

The economic logic is the recurring justification across all of IP: creation is costly and easily copied, so the law grants a temporary **exclusive right** to let the creator recoup investment — and thereby keeps the incentive to create alive.

### Protected works

- **Literary works** — e-books, blogs and digital posts (*and a live question: content generated by AI?*)
- **Musical works** — TikTok creations, electronic music, video-game sound effects
- **Dramatic works** — including augmented reality
- **Artistic works** — digital works, NFTs
- **Modern additions** — computer software, apps, **UI/UX**, computer games

> [!tip] Copyright is automatic — no registration needed ^copyright-automatic
> Unlike patents and trademarks, copyright protection **arises the moment the work is created** — no formal registration, no fee, no examination. This is why a startup's source code is copyright-protected from the first commit. (Registration exists in some countries as *evidence*, but it is **not constitutive** of the right.)

## 5. Patents

> [!info] Definition — Patent ^patent
> A ==patent== protects **inventors of technological innovations**.
> **Purposes:** (1) **encourage technological innovation**; (2) provide a **high economic return** on the inventor's investment.

The patent is the classic IP bargain: the state grants a **20-year monopoly** in exchange for the inventor **publicly disclosing** how the invention works (so society can build on it once the patent expires). Disclosure is the price of protection — which is exactly why some inventions are better kept as trade secrets.

### Registering a patent in Israel — the three stages

> [!example] Stage 1 — Preliminary examination (do it before you file) ^patent-prelim
> Before submitting, check the invention against the **Patents Law, 1967** requirements. The three substantive screens:
> - **Novelty** — is it *new*? Not previously published or known to the public.
> - **Non-triviality / inventive step** — does it go beyond the obvious? It must contain an ==inventive step==.
> - **Technological applicability** — does it relate to a *technological field* with a practical application?
>
> Search existing patents first in databases like the **WIPO** patent database or the **Israeli** patent database — a prior identical patent destroys novelty.

> [!example] Stage 2 — File with the Patent Office
> The application goes to the **Israel Patent Authority** at the **Ministry of Justice** and must contain:
> - **Full description** of the invention (including drawings if needed)
> - **Claims** — these *define the scope* of the protection requested (the most important part: they draw the legal boundary)
> - **Abstract**
> - **Required fees**
>
> **Patent examiners** then check novelty, non-triviality and inventive step, and may demand **clarifications and amendments** from the inventor.

> [!example] Stage 3 — Approval, registration & maintenance
> If approved, the patent is entered in the **patent register** and protection runs for **20 years from the filing date**. The inventor must keep paying **maintenance fees** to keep it alive.

> [!warning] Patents expire — and then competitors flood in ^patent-expiry
> When a patent expires the technology becomes free to copy. Lecture examples:
> - **Viagra** — from 2013 many firms produced **generics**, causing a large price drop.
> - **LCD screens.**
> - **Nespresso capsules.**
>
> For a founder this cuts both ways: your own moat has a 20-year clock, *and* expired patents are a legitimate opportunity to enter a market.

### Characteristics of a patent — the trade-off

| | Patent |
|---|---|
| **Protection period** | 20 years from filing |
| **Conditions for grant** | Novelty, non-triviality, inventive step, technological applicability |
| **Advantage** | **Strong** protection lets inventors recoup **huge** investments (e.g. developing COVID-19 vaccines) |
| **Challenge** | Can **stifle innovation** — it locks others out of the technology for a long time |

> [!tip] Intuition — the patent dilemma
> The same feature that makes patents valuable to the holder (a 20-year exclusive lock) is what makes them socially costly (everyone else is locked out). IP policy is a permanent balancing act between **rewarding the creator** and **keeping knowledge free** — this tension reappears in every regime and again in the AI section.

## 6. Trade Secrets

> [!info] Definition — Trade secret ^trade-secret
> A ==trade secret== is protection over **secret information with economic value** that is **kept confidential**.
> **Purposes / advantages:**
> - **Broader and simpler** protection than a patent.
> - Preserves **strategic information without a time limit**.
> - Protection **without any state examination**.
> - **May last forever** — as long as the information stays secret.

> [!warning] The fragility of secrecy ^trade-secret-fragility
> The trade secret's strength is also its weakness: protection lasts **only while the information remains secret**. The moment it is disclosed — reverse-engineered, leaked, independently discovered — the protection **evaporates**, with no 20-year fallback like a patent. Classic example: the **Coca-Cola formula**, protected for over a century by secrecy rather than by a (long-expired) patent.

> [!tip] Patent vs trade secret — the founder's real choice
> These two regimes are *substitutes* for the same kind of technical asset, and choosing between them is a genuine strategic decision:

| | Patent | Trade secret |
|---|---|---|
| **Requires disclosure?** | **Yes** — published to the world | **No** — must stay hidden |
| **State examination?** | Yes (costly, slow) | None |
| **Duration** | 20 years, then public | Forever — *until* it leaks |
| **Protects against independent discovery?** | **Yes** | **No** — a rival who independently invents it is free |
| **Best for** | Inventions that **can be reverse-engineered** anyway | Advantages you can **genuinely keep hidden** (recipes, processes, algorithms) |

## 7. Trademarks

> [!info] Definition — Trademark ^trademark
> A ==trademark== protects **names and signs that identify goods and services**.
> **Purposes:** (1) **differentiation and unique branding** of products/services; (2) **incentivising investment in branding**.
> In Israel, trademarks are governed by the **Trademarks Ordinance [New Version], 1972**, which protects symbols, words, logos and other identifying signs and **stops competitors using them in ways that mislead the public**.

### What can be a trademark?

A trademark is a **sign used to identify the goods/services of a particular trader**, distinguishing them from another trader's. The sign may be:

- **Words, names, or letters**
- **Numbers, logos, or graphic symbols**
- **3D shapes** or **combinations** of these

### Conditions for registration

> [!example] The three registration tests ^trademark-conditions
> 1. **Registrability** — the sign must be **unique and not directly descriptive** of the product/service. It **cannot be generic, misleading, or infringe existing rights**.
>    - ✗ *"Orange Juice"* for orange juice — descriptive.
>    - ✗ *"Bread"* for a bakery, *"Computer"* for a computer store — generic.
> 2. **Distinctiveness** — sufficiently distinguishable from other signs in the same field (e.g. *iPhone*, *Starbucks*).
> 3. **Actual use or intent to use** — must show the sign is in use or genuinely intended to be used soon.

### Rights granted on registration

- **Legal protection** against unauthorised use.
- **Exclusivity** over the sign within the **registered goods/services class**.
- The ability to **bring legal proceedings** on infringement.

> [!warning] When a trademark will be refused ^trademark-refusal
> - It **misleads the public** (e.g. *"OrganicPure"* on a non-organic product).
> - It is **similar/identical to an existing registered mark** in the same field (e.g. *"Nikee"* riding on Nike).
> - It includes **official symbols, flags, or the state emblem**.
> - It **lacks distinctive character** — i.e. it directly describes the product/service (e.g. *"Leather Shoes"* for a shoe brand).

> [!tip] Intuition — the "spectrum of distinctiveness"
> Trademark law rewards signs that are **arbitrary or invented** (*Apple* for computers, *Kodak*) and refuses signs that are **descriptive or generic** (*"Computer"* for a computer shop). The further your brand sits from merely *describing* the product, the stronger and more registrable it is. Founders naming a company should lean **distinctive**, not descriptive.

## 8. Designs (Industrial Designs)

> [!info] Definition — Design ^design
> A ==design== protects **industrial designs with unique visual features** — the *appearance* of a product, not how it works.
> **Purposes:** (1) **encourage innovative product design**; (2) grant **exclusivity for a limited period**.

> [!example] Designs in practice
> - The field was **renewed by the new Designs Law**.
> - Protected examples: **chairs, jewellery, window profiles, wedding dresses**.
> - **Challenges:**
>   - **Fashion-industry piracy** is rampant — which paradoxically *encourages rapid innovation* (designers stay ahead by constantly producing new designs).
>   - Despite the legislation, **design registration is not widely used** in practice.

> [!tip] Design vs patent vs copyright — don't confuse them
> All three can touch a product, but on different layers:
> - **Patent** → how it *works* (function).
> - **Design** → how it *looks* (aesthetic form).
> - **Copyright** → an artistic *work* (which can overlap with a design for some creative objects).
> A smartphone can carry **patents** (the chip), a **design** (the casing shape), **trademarks** (the logo) and **copyright** (the OS code) simultaneously.

## 9. Intellectual Property in the AI Era

This is the lecture's frontier: AI breaks the assumption baked into every regime above — that a **human** creates the work or invention.

> [!warning] The three open challenges ^ai-ip-challenges
> 1. **Ownership of AI-generated works** — who owns an output the machine produced?
> 2. **Balancing** the encouragement of innovation **against** the protection of *traditional* copyright (e.g. when models train on protected works).
> 3. **Using AI to monitor and enforce** copyright at scale.

### Key case law

> [!example] How different jurisdictions are answering ^ai-caselaw
> **China** — the **Beijing court** recognised, for the first time, **copyright in an AI-generated image**. Decisive fact: the plaintiff supplied **significant human input** — tailored prompts and parameter coordination — to create the image. *(Human creative contribution → protectable.)*
>
> **United States**
> - A **machine cannot be a copyright owner**; there is **no automatic recognition** of copyright in AI-generated works.
> - **"Fair use"** is assessed when protected works are used to **train models**.
> - *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.* — does using **training data based on protected works** infringe? Court emphasised the line between **data analysis** and **direct creative use** of copyrighted works.
> - *The New York Times* case — same training-data battleground.
> - **Patents:** *Thaler v. Vidal* — only a **"natural person"** can be an inventor, but the door is left open to patents created **with AI in collaboration with humans**.
>
> **European Union** — focus on **transparency about training data** and a **risk-management model**.
>
> **Israel** — **no court ruling yet** recognising copyright in AI-created content.

> [!tip] The pattern across jurisdictions
> The emerging rule everywhere is the same: **the more substantive the human creative contribution, the more likely protection attaches.** Pure machine output (no meaningful human authorship) tends to fall outside copyright; meaningful human prompting/curation tends to bring it back in. This mirrors the foundational IP principle from §2 — the law protects a *human creative act*, not the raw existence of an output.

## Summary

> [!summary] One-screen revision ^summary
> - **IP is a property right**, so it is **strong**, **asset-attached**, and **enduring** — and crucially valid **against the whole world** (*in rem*), not just between contracting parties (*in personam*). ^s-foundation
> - **Ideas are free; protected *forms* are owned.** IP's hardest job is drawing the boundary between an unprotected abstract idea and a protectable invention/expression/sign. ^s-boundary
> - **Five regimes, pick the right one:** ==Copyright== (creative works/software, **automatic, no registration**), ==Patent== (inventions, **registered, 20 yrs, requires disclosure**), ==Trade secret== (confidential info, **no registration, lasts forever while secret**), ==Trademark== (brand signs, **registered, must be distinctive**), ==Design== (product appearance, **registered, limited term**). ^s-five
> - **Patent ↔ trade secret is a real strategic choice:** patent = disclose + 20-yr monopoly + protection against independent invention; trade secret = stay hidden + potentially forever + **no** protection if a rival independently discovers it. ^s-patent-secret
> - **Patent test:** novelty + non-triviality/**inventive step** + technological applicability; filed at the **Israel Patent Authority** (Patents Law 1967); **claims define the scope**; 20 yrs from filing; needs maintenance fees. ^s-patent-test
> - **Trademark test:** **distinctive**, not descriptive/generic/misleading; refused if it misleads, copies an existing mark, uses state emblems, or lacks distinctive character. ^s-trademark-test
> - **AI era:** the consistent thread is **human creative contribution** — China recognised copyright where prompts/parameters were substantial; the US refuses machine *inventors/owners* (*Thaler v. Vidal*) and litigates **training data as fair use** (*Andersen v. Stability AI*, *NYT*); Israel has **no ruling yet**. ^s-ai
