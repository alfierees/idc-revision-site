# Company Law & Corporate Structures

> Part of: [[Legal and Tax Aspects]] | Key concepts: [[Types of Legal Entities]], [[Articles of Association]], [[Limited Liability]], [[Share]], [[Bond]], [[Option (Security)]], [[Corporate Governance]], [[Agency Problem]], [[Separate Legal Personality]], [[Piercing the Corporate Veil]]

> **Session 3** — Legal and Tax Aspects of Entrepreneurship, Dr. Yuval Reinfeld (Reichman University). Statutory references are to the **Israeli Companies Law (1999)** unless stated otherwise.

## 🎯 Why This Session Exists

[[Lec_02-Legal Entrepreneurship — Fundamental Concepts|Session 2]] introduced the *vocabulary* of legal entrepreneurship and named "improper corporate structure" as one of the five core risks. This session drills into the **single most consequential structural choice a founder makes** — *what legal vessel to pour the venture into* — and then opens the vessel up: how a company is built, what it can issue (shares, bonds, options), who controls it, and — crucially — **when the law will let creditors reach past the company to the people behind it**.

> [!tip] The thread running through the whole session
> Everything here orbits one idea: a company is a ==separate legal person== that the law conjures into existence so that **risk can be contained**. Limited liability is the headline benefit; the agency problem, minority oppression, and veil-piercing are all the *prices and limits* of that benefit. Read every topic as either "how the separation is built" or "when the separation breaks down."

## 🏛️ Part 1 — Types of Legal Entities

Before "company" there is a **menu** of vehicles. Israeli law is unusually rich here because several entities are historical relics of the pre-state *Yishuv* (e.g. the cooperative society). The founder's job is to pick the vehicle whose **liability, taxation, and control** profile fits the venture.

| Entity | Liability | Registration | Taxation | Typical use |
|--------|-----------|--------------|----------|-------------|
| ==Sole Proprietor== (*Osek Murshe/Patur*) | **Personal** — no separation | Simple, no lawyer needed | **Simple, single-layer** (personal income tax); expenses deductible | Most small businesses, freelancers, clinics, shops |
| ==Partnership== | **Personal, joint & several** | Not required (though technically mandatory) — very easy | Pass-through: each partner files personally | Law/accounting firms; ≤ 20 partners (except professional) |
| ==Association== (*Amuta*) | Separate (like a corporation) | Registrar of Associations | **Tax benefits**, donations under Section 46 | Non-profits; purposes must be *social*; profits cannot be distributed |
| ==Cooperative Society== (*Agudah Shitufit*) | Members bear larger responsibility on liquidation | Registrar of Cooperative Societies | Profits shared by **participation**, not capital | Kibbutzim, moshavim, Egged; "one member, one vote" |
| ==Statutory Corporation== | Defined by statute; **no owner** | Created **by law** | n/a | Public bodies (Academy of the Hebrew Language, Council for Higher Education) |
| ==Company (Ltd.)== | **Limited** to investment | Registrar of Companies | **Double taxation** (corporate tax + dividend tax) | Startups, businesses raising capital, risky ventures |

### Sole Proprietor — the simplest default

> [!info] Definition
> A ==sole proprietor== (*Osek Murshe* or, below a revenue threshold, *Osek Patur*) is **not a separate legal entity** — the business *is* the person. Its appeal is **simple, single-layer taxation** (unlike a company's double taxation) and the fact that **business expenses are deductible**.

The rule of thumb taught in the course: once expected annual revenue exceeds **~500,000 NIS**, or you take on partners, need investors, or run real operational risk, you should consider switching to a company. ^sole-proprietor

### Partnership — easy to form, dangerous on liability

> [!info] Definition (Section 1, Partnerships Ordinance)
> A ==partnership== is a legal association of two or more persons (or companies) who **run a business together to make a profit**, and who are not incorporated in another form (company, cooperative). Its purpose under Israeli (and English) law is **running a business**.

Three defining features explain why partnerships are risky:

- ==Fiduciary duty== between partners — each must act in good faith toward the others.
- **Personal, joint and several liability** — every partner is fully liable for *all* the partnership's obligations.
- ==Mutual agency== — every partner acts as an **agent** of the partnership and of all other partners, and can **bind** them through ordinary-course actions. (Contrast a company, which acts only through its **board and CEO** under the Companies Law and its articles.)

> [!warning] The agency trap of partnerships
> Because each partner can bind all the others *and* is personally liable for everything, **one partner's mistake can bankrupt the rest personally**. A company solves both problems at once: it acts only through defined organs, and it shields shareholders behind limited liability.

Partnerships require no registration, are limited to **20 partners** (except professional ones), and can be dissolved easily. ^partnership

> [!example] Law firm — why partnerships persist
> A law firm is **traditionally an unlimited partnership**. A *limited* partnership is possible (so long as at least one partner is not exempt), and even a private company (Ltd.) is possible under strict limits (e.g. all shareholders must be lawyers). But note: **professional liability is always personal** — a lawyer cannot hide malpractice behind a corporate veil. This is *why* personal-accountability professions keep using partnerships.

### Cooperative society — the egalitarian "hybrid creature"

> [!info] Definition
> A ==cooperative society== (*Agudah Shitufit*) is a uniquely Israeli entity blending economic activity with **communal, egalitarian** principles. Legally it is a "strange hybrid" — part non-profit, part profit-distributing business.

Its distinguishing features:

- **Objectives:** promote self-help, cooperation, and improving members' living conditions.
- **Equality:** *each member has equal rights regardless of how much they invested* — there are **no controlling shareholders and no shares**.
- **Profit distribution:** based on **participation** in the cooperative's activity, not on capital contributed.
- **Governance:** the democratic principle — ==one member, one vote==.
- **Supervision:** the **Registrar of Cooperative Societies**, *not* the Companies Law.
- **Capital limits:** caps on how much any one member can hold, to **prevent concentration of power**.

This egalitarian structure becomes legally decisive in the **Pri HaEmek** case below. ^cooperative

### Company vs. Partnership — the three axes of difference

This comparison is the hinge of Part 1. A company differs from a partnership along **three axes**:

| Axis | Partnership | Company (Ltd.) |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Legal structure** | A combination of ≥2 persons; formed by simply agreeing to partner (registration rarely enforced) | A constructed legal structure: **shareholders + board of directors + officers**; needs articles, directors, share allocation, registration |
| **Legal liability** | Fully **transparent** — every partner personally liable for all debts, and binds the others | A **separate legal body**; shareholders generally not liable beyond their investment |
| **Taxation & bookkeeping** | Like a sole proprietor — simple books, each partner files personally at personal rates | **Double-entry books, audited annual report**; pays **corporate tax**, shareholders pay dividend/capital-gains tax |

> [!tip] The trade-off in one line
> A partnership is **cheap and simple but personally dangerous**; a company is **costly and formal but protective and investable**. Founders accept the company's overhead precisely to *buy* limited liability and the ability to issue shares. ^company-vs-partnership

## 🏢 Part 2 — Forming & Structuring a Company

### Why establish a company?

Two drivers recur: **the mission** (operating a real business with growth ambitions) and **investors** (you cannot sell equity in a sole proprietorship). Add **risk-shielding** and **tax planning** at higher profit levels, and the company becomes the natural vehicle.

### How to establish a company — the two steps

**Step 1 — Choose the company type:**

| Type | Defining feature |
|------|------------------|
| ==Limited company (Ltd.)== | Shareholders' liability is **limited** to their investment |
| **Private company** | **Not** traded on a stock exchange |
| **Public company** | Shares **offered to the public** (traded on an exchange) |
| **Single-member company** | Owned by **one** person |

**Step 2 — File the required documents:** an application to register, the ==Articles of Association==, a **declaration of first directors**, and a one-time **registration fee**. On completion the Registrar issues a ==Certificate of Incorporation==. ^formation

### The Articles of Association — the company's constitution

> [!info] Definition
> The ==Articles of Association== (*Takanon*) are the company's foundational governing document — its internal constitution. They are **mandatory** (Section 15).

The Companies Law splits the contents into mandatory and optional:

| **Mandatory content (Section 18)** | **Optional content (Section 19)** |
|------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Company name | Number of directors |
| Company objectives | Rules for corporate governance |
| Details of registered share capital | Powers of shareholders and directors |
| Details on limitation of liability | Rights and duties of shareholders and directors |
| | Conditions for transfer of shares |

> [!info] The Articles are a *contract* (Section 18 / 15)
> Critically, the Articles **constitute a contract** between (a) **the company and its shareholders**, and (b) **the shareholders among themselves**, governing all the rights and obligations they specify. This "articles-as-contract" idea is the legal lever in the **Pri HaEmek** case — you cannot unilaterally rewrite the deal between members. ^articles

### Limited liability & the structure of the company

> [!info] Limited liability
> ==Limited liability== means a shareholder's exposure is **capped at the amount they invested** in their shares. If the company collapses, they lose their shares — but creditors generally **cannot pursue their personal assets**.

The company's internal architecture is taught with a **political analogy**:

| Organ | Political analogy | Role |
|-------|-------------------|------|
| **Shareholders** (general meeting) | The **Knesset** (legislature) | Ultimate owners; vote at the general meeting; hold **minority rights** (equality, transparency, protection from oppression); receive dividends |
| **Officers** (CEO + Board of Directors) | The **government** (executive) | Run the company; set and execute strategy |
| **Employees** | The civil service | Carry out operations |

### Minority rights — protecting the small shareholder

Because majority shareholders control the vote, the law arms the minority with defensive rights:

- **Right to information** (financial statements, major transactions).
- **Voting rights** at the general meeting.
- Right to file a ==derivative suit== if officers harm the company.
- Right to **petition the court** in cases of minority **oppression**.
- Right to propose topics for the general meeting (in some cases).

These rights are the law's answer to the **majority-vs-minority agency problem** discussed in Part 5. ^minority-rights

### Private vs. Public company

| Dimension | Private company | Public company |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Board size / chair** | May have **1 director**; chair optional and may also be CEO | **≥ 4 directors**; chair **mandatory** and generally **cannot** also be CEO (without general-meeting approval) |
| **CEO** | Optional — board can run it directly | **Must** appoint at least one CEO |
| **External directors** | Not required | **≥ 2 external (independent) directors** |
| **Reporting** | To the **Registrar of Companies** only | To the Registrar **+ Securities Authority + the exchange** (cf. *Paz vs. Yellow*) |
| **Disclosure** | Limited | ==Principle of full disclosure== — detailed quarterly & annual reports |
| **Going public** | — | Becomes public via an ==IPO== (public offering with a prospectus) |

> [!tip] Why the public company carries so much regulation
> A public company sells shares to dispersed investors who **don't run the company and can't monitor it**. The value of their shares depends on management's performance, so the law forces **transparency (full disclosure)** as the price of access to public capital — only an informed investor can make a rational decision. The heavier governance (external directors, mandatory CEO/chair separation) protects that public interest. ^private-vs-public

## 💵 Part 3 — Securities: Shares, Bonds & Options

A company funds itself by **issuing securities**. The three core instruments differ in one fundamental way: **shares = ownership, bonds = debt, options = a right to buy**.

### Shares — units of ownership

> [!info] Definition
> A ==share== is a **security granting a unit of ownership** in the company. It gives participation rights, **influence over management**, and **entitlement to profits**.

A share bundles together five rights:

| Right | Source | Meaning |
|-------|--------|---------|
| **Voting rights** | Section 188 | Vote at general meetings; influence major decisions (director appointments, major transactions) |
| **Dividends** | Section 190 | Share in distributed profits |
| **Liquidation rights** | — | Proportional claim on **residual assets after all debts are paid** |
| **Value per Articles** | — | The Articles fix the rights/value of **each class** of share |
| **Distribution by investment** | — | Bigger investment → more shares → more rights |

> [!example] One-share-one-vote vs. share classes
> In a **private** company, the default is often "**one share = one vote**." In a **public** company, **different share classes** may carry different voting power — letting founders keep control while raising capital (super-voting shares). ^share-rights

> [!info] Dividends — the solvency guardrail (Section 190)
> A ==dividend== is a distribution of company profits (cash or stock) to shareholders. It may **only** be paid when **(1) the company has sufficient profits** *and* **(2) the distribution doesn't endanger solvency**. This protects creditors: shareholders cannot drain a fragile company.

> [!example] Liquidation & distribution by investment
> On liquidation, if **1M NIS** remains after debts, shareholders split it **proportionally**. And rights track investment: if **A invests 500,000 NIS → 5 shares** and **B invests 100,000 NIS → 1 share**, then **A receives 5× the profits/dividends** of B.

### Bonds — lending to the company

> [!info] Definition
> A ==bond== is essentially a **loan from the investor to the company** (or government): a **contractual obligation** to repay **principal + interest**. The company is the **borrower**; the bondholder is a ==creditor==, *not* an owner.

> [!example] Bond cash flow
> Invest **1,000 NIS** at **5%/year** → receive **50 NIS** interest annually, plus your 1,000 NIS back at maturity. A real-estate firm needing project finance might issue a **10,000 NIS** bond promising repayment in 5 years at **3% (300 NIS/year)** — instead of a bank loan.

| | **Government bonds** | **Corporate bonds** |
|--|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Issuer** | The government | Private/public companies |
| **Risk** | Generally **low** | **Higher** |
| **Interest** | Typically **lower** | Often higher, or **collateral-backed** |

**Types of private (corporate) bonds:** ==tradable== (sold on the exchange), ==non-tradable== (private placement to banks/institutions), ==convertible== (can convert into shares under set conditions), and ==collateral-backed== (secured against company assets to reduce investor risk). ^bonds

> [!tip] Bond vs. share — the one distinction to remember
> **Bondholder = lender**, not a partner in ownership; gets fixed repayment + interest, ranks **ahead** of shareholders on liquidation. **Shareholder = owner**, participates in control and upside, but is **last in line** if the company fails. Debt is safer but capped; equity is riskier but unlimited. ^bond-vs-share

### Options — the right, not the obligation, to buy

> [!info] Definition
> An ==option== is a **derivative**: a contract giving you the **right (but not the obligation)** to buy (or sell) an asset — usually a share — at a **pre-agreed price** (the ==strike price==) within a **fixed period** (until the ==expiration date==). Its value is *derived* from the underlying share.

> [!example] Why "no obligation" is the whole point
> You buy an option to purchase a share at a **strike of 500 NIS**, paying a **premium of 50 NIS**.
>
> - **Scenario 1 — share rises to 600 NIS:** exercise the option, buy at 500, sell at 600. Gross gain 100 NIS, **net +50 NIS** after the premium.
> - **Scenario 2 — share falls to 400 NIS:** you **don't exercise** (buying at 500 would lose money). Your loss is **only the 50 NIS premium**.
>
> The payoff is therefore **capped on the downside** (the premium) and **open on the upside**:
> $$\text{Payoff at expiry} = \boxed{\max(S_T - K,\ 0) - \text{premium}}$$
> where $S_T$ is the share price at expiry and $K$ is the strike. This asymmetry — limited loss, unlimited gain — is exactly why options are the backbone of **employee equity (ESOP)**: an employee can only win.

## ⚖️ Part 4 — Corporate Governance: Who Holds Which Power?

The Companies Law **allocates powers** among the organs and limits how far they can be delegated. This is the machinery that turns "the company" into concrete decisions.

### Powers of shareholders (Section 57)

The general meeting holds the **constitutional** powers it cannot give away:

- **Appoint the board of directors.**
- Approve transactions with **interested parties**.
- **Increase/decrease share capital** (allocate shares).
- Appoint the company's **auditor** and **internal auditor**.

### Powers of the board of directors (Section 92)

> [!info] The board's mandate
> The ==board of directors== is **appointed by shareholders**, sets **strategy and supervision**, **appoints and dismisses the CEO**, and **approves the financial statements**. The recurring question: *which of these can be delegated down to the CEO?*

| **Can be delegated to CEO** | **Cannot be delegated** |
|-----------------------------|-------------------------|
| Ongoing administrative powers | Appointment/dismissal of the CEO |
| Executing board-approved strategy / action plans | Approval of financial statements |
| Negotiating defined matters | Determining general business strategy |
| Pre-defined professional tasks (project management, small contracts) | Dividend decisions & significant capital changes |
| *e.g. running the budget up to a ceiling* | Substantive structural change or **IPO** |

> [!tip] The logic of the delegation line
> Anything **operational and reversible** can flow to the CEO; anything **constitutional, irreversible, or conflict-prone** (who runs the company, what the accounts say, how to deploy capital) stays with the board. The board exists to **monitor** the CEO — it cannot delegate away the very decisions that constitute that monitoring. ^delegation

### Limitations of the board — why boards underperform (Section 92)

The slides are unusually candid about the board's **real-world weaknesses**:

- Often **appointed by the controlling shareholder** (so not truly independent).
- **Superficiality** — directors don't dig deep.
- **Limited information** — they rely on the CEO for what they know.
- Acting as a **"rubber stamp."**

The law's structural answer: **external directors**. In a private company the articles govern (minimum **one** director); in a public company there must be **≥ 2 external (independent) directors**, and at least one must have **accounting and financial expertise** (Sections 239–240; see also 240(b)). External directors exist to **represent the company's interest — including the minority's — against the controlling shareholder**. ^board-limits

## 🎭 Part 5 — The Agency Problem

> [!info] Definition (Section 192(a))
> The ==agency problem== arises when an ==officer== (CEO, director, senior manager) is **legally bound to act for the company's benefit** but has **personal interests** that pull the other way. Section 192(a) imposes a **duty of loyalty + duty of care**: the officer must act **only** for the company, not for himself or a third party. Under the Companies Law his actions **count as the corporation's actions** — which is exactly why his loyalty matters so much.

The session maps **three distinct agency conflicts**, each with its own victim:

| Conflict | Who's tempted | Who's harmed | Classic example |
|----------|---------------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Shareholders ↔ management** | Managers spend the company's money on **perks** (executive jet, big car) instead of profitable investment | Shareholders | A CEO signs a costly contract with a **friend's** firm despite worse terms — acting for the friend, not the company |
| **Majority ↔ minority shareholders** | Controllers make decisions that **benefit themselves at the minority's expense** | Minority shareholders | (Why minority rights & external directors exist) |
| **Shareholders ↔ creditors** | Shareholders push **excessive risk** for big upside, knowing **they're shielded** if it fails | Creditors | Thin-capitalization gambles (→ Northern Chicks) |

> [!warning] The shareholders-vs-creditors conflict is the subtle one
> Because shareholders enjoy **limited liability**, they capture the **upside** of risky bets but offload the **downside** onto creditors if the company is liquidated. This asymmetry **encourages reckless risk-taking** — and is the precise mischief the **subordination doctrine** (Part 7) was built to correct. ^agency-problem

> [!example] Spot-the-agency-problem
> A director wants to invest a large sum in a project run by **another company owned by his relative**, even though **better proposals exist**. The conflict between his **personal tie** and the **company's interest** is a textbook agency problem and a breach of the Section 192(a) duty of loyalty.

## 🧱 Part 6 — Separate Legal Personality & the Corporate Veil

This is the doctrinal heart of company law.

> [!info] The rule (Section 4)
> A company has ==separate legal personality==: it is **capable of any right or obligation consistent with its nature** — it can own, contract, sue, be sued, and even bear **criminal liability** (the *Modiin* case). There is a ==corporate veil== separating the company's debts from its shareholders' personal debts.

The consequence is **limited liability**: a shareholder who paid **10 NIS** for shares can **never be sued for more than 10 NIS**, even if the company collapses into debt. The company alone bears its obligations. ^separate-personality

### The foundation: *Salomon* (England, 1897)

> [!info] *Salomon v. Salomon & Co.*
> The 1897 English ruling is the **bedrock** of separate legal personality. It held that a company is a **distinct legal entity** from its shareholders **even when the main shareholder runs it in practice**. Incorporation creates a genuine legal wall, not a sham.

### Organ theory — giving the company a "mind"

> [!info] Definition
> ==Organ theory== lets the law **attribute the human characteristics** needed for liability (intent, conduct) **to the corporation itself**. The mental and behavioural elements that exist in an **organ** (a senior officer) are treated **as the corporation's own**.

> [!info] *Tzuk Or* (CA 407/89) — the philosophical justification
> Justice Barak rejected the idea that a corporation is "artificial." If the corporation is artificial, then *so are ownership, rights, and contracts*. The corporation is a **real object of rights and duties** — but it exists **only through human beings**: "there is no corporation without law, and no law without human beings." The corporation acts **only through the actions of people**, and organ theory is what lets those actions *be* the company's actions.

> [!info] *Modiin* — criminal liability, but bounded
> Justice Barak held organ theory **can impose criminal liability** on a company — but only **where justified**. For offences a corporation *typically* commits (e.g. **tax offences**) liability is appropriate; for offences like **bigamy** it makes little sense; many fall in a **gray area**. "The mental element in the organ is captured as the mental element of the corporation." ^organ-theory

### Why we grant separate personality at all

> [!tip] The justifications
> Separate legal personality (and the limited liability it carries) exists to **encourage reasonable risk-taking** — it offers **opportunity without ruinous personal risk**, capping the shareholder's exposure at their investment. Without it, almost no one would fund innovation. The doctrines below are the **safety valves** that stop this shield from being abused. ^veil-justifications

### Piercing the corporate veil (Section 6)

> [!info] The exception
> ==Piercing the corporate veil== ("raising the curtain") lets a court **look through** the company to its shareholders/officers and impose **personal liability**. Under **Section 6**, the court may do so where the separate personality is **abused**.

> [!warning] When the court deviates from limited liability
> Section 6 permits piercing where there is:
>
> - **Use of the company for fraud or to wrong creditors.**
> - **Harm to the company's purpose** by taking **unreasonable risk** about repaying debts.
> - **Awareness or willful blindness** by a shareholder to improper conduct.
>
> The hard question the slides pose — *"Where is the boundary?"* — has no bright line. Piercing is **exceptional**; courts protect the *Salomon* principle and only lift the veil for genuine abuse. ^piercing

## 🐔 Part 7 — Subordination Doctrine: the *Northern Chicks* Case

> [!info] Case
> **CA 4263/04 — Kibbutz Mishmar HaEmek v. Adv. Tomer Manor (liquidator of Northern Chicks).** The question: **is thin capitalization alone enough to subordinate a shareholder's debt** to the company's other creditors?

> [!info] Thin capitalization
> ==Thin capitalization== is when a company is financed **mainly by loans (often from its owners)** rather than **equity**. It leaves the company exposed — there is **no equity safety cushion** in a crisis.

**Facts:** Northern Chicks was formed in 1998 by *Ravaya HaEmek* (66%) and *Ramot Yohanan Hatchery* (33%) with share capital of only **190 NIS**. It marketed chicks on **thin capitalization**, and within two years collapsed (a feed-quality failure, a problematic market, and conflicted mismanagement). The owners had funded it largely via **shareholder loans** and then **filed debt claims** to recover those loans **as ordinary creditors**.

> [!info] Credit hierarchy (who gets paid first)
> 1. **Secured creditors** (mortgage, liens)
> 2. **Priority creditors** (employees, taxes)
> 3. **Unsecured creditors** (regular creditors, then subordinated creditors, and **last — the shareholders**)

> [!info] Subordination doctrine (Section 6(c), per Justice Rubinstein)
> ==Subordination of debt== is "the **suspension of a shareholder's right** to repayment of his debt from the company **until after the company has fully paid all its obligations to other creditors**." The court treated the **shareholder loans as if they were equity**, so external creditors are paid **in full first**.

**Ruling:** in cases of **thin capitalization + improper management**, repayment to shareholders can be **delayed** until external creditors are fully paid. Profits that *should* have been the company's safety cushion had been routed to owners as loans — so those loans were **subordinated**.

> [!tip] Subordination vs. piercing — a gentler tool
> Subordination is an **alternative doctrine to "raising the curtain."** Piercing **destroys** the separation (shareholders become personally liable); subordination **preserves** it but merely **re-orders the queue** — shareholders still only risk what they put in, but they **can't jump ahead of real creditors** by dressing up equity as debt. It is the **proportionate** response to the shareholders-vs-creditors agency problem. (Critique noted in the judgment: it effectively *requires more equity* though the law doesn't say so, and the required amount is unclear.) ^subordination

## 🌾 Part 8 — *Pri HaEmek*: Can a Majority Strip Limited Liability?

> [!info] Case
> The ==Pri HaEmek== cooperative (kibbutzim + moshavim) hit **insolvency** in a severe economic crisis. Its general meeting voted — **kibbutz representatives outvoting the moshav representatives** — to **waive limited liability** and **attribute the cooperative's debts personally to its members**.

**Question:** *Is the general meeting authorized to impose the cooperative's debts on its members?*

- **District Court:** **No** — you cannot attribute the cooperative's debts to members **against their consent**.
- **Supreme Court (Justice Barak, majority):** The cooperative's **separate legal personality applies even without limited liability**. Attributing personal debts is a **substantive change** to the foundational bargain, so it requires the **unanimous consent of all members** — not a mere majority. **Result:** the general-meeting decision was **cancelled**; the debts remained the **cooperative's** alone.

> [!info] The doctrinal toolkit *Pri HaEmek* deploys
> Separate legal personality · agency relationships between the cooperative and its members · piercing the veil · limited liability · **amendment of the foundational documents** (and the **prohibition** on amendments outside the normative framework) · the ==articles-as-contract== (between the corporation and its members, and between members *inter se*).

> [!tip] Why this matters beyond cooperatives
> Barak stressed that **here there is no difference between a company and a cooperative** despite their substantive differences. The principle generalises: the **articles are a contract**, and a **majority cannot unilaterally rewrite the most basic term — who bears the debts**. Limited liability is part of the deal each member signed; stripping it is a **substantive deviation** needing **everyone's** agreement. This is the direct application of the "articles-as-contract" idea from Part 2. ^pri-haemek

## 📝 Summary

- **Entity menu:** Israeli law offers sole proprietor, partnership, association, **cooperative society**, statutory corporation, and the **company (Ltd.)**. The choice trades off **liability, taxation, and control**. Sole proprietors and partnerships expose you **personally**; the company buys **limited liability** at the cost of double taxation and formality. ^entity-types
- **Company vs. partnership** differ on three axes: **legal structure** (constructed organs vs. a handshake), **liability** (separate body vs. personal & transparent), and **tax/books** (corporate tax + audited accounts vs. pass-through). ^company-vs-partnership
- **Formation:** choose the type, then file articles + first-directors declaration to get a Certificate of Incorporation. The ==Articles of Association== are **mandatory (s.15)**, carry mandatory (s.18) and optional (s.19) content, and are a **contract** between the company and shareholders and among shareholders. ^articles
- **Structure:** shareholders (≈ Knesset) own and vote with **minority protections**; officers (≈ government: CEO + board) run it; **public companies** carry far heavier governance and **full-disclosure** duties than private ones. ^company-structure
- **Securities:** a ==share== = **ownership** (voting s.188, dividends s.190, residual liquidation rights); a ==bond== = **debt** (principal + interest, creditor ranks ahead of shareholders); an ==option== = a **right to buy** at a strike, with **capped loss and open upside** ($\max(S_T-K,0)-\text{premium}$). ^options
- **Governance:** shareholders hold un-delegable powers (s.57: appoint board, auditor, alter capital); the board (s.92) sets strategy and **hires/fires the CEO** but **cannot delegate** the constitutional decisions; **external directors** (s.239–240) counter weak, controller-captured boards. ^delegation
- **Agency problem (s.192(a)):** officers owe **loyalty + care**; conflicts run **management↔shareholders**, **majority↔minority**, and **shareholders↔creditors** — the last fuelled by limited liability encouraging excessive risk. ^agency-problem
- **Separate legal personality (s.4):** the company is a real legal person (*Salomon* 1897); **organ theory** (*Tzuk Or*; *Modiin* for crime) attributes human intent to it. The veil can be **pierced (s.6)** for fraud, unreasonable debt-risk, or willful blindness. ^piercing
- **Safety valves on limited liability:** **subordination** (*Northern Chicks*, s.6(c)) re-orders shareholder loans *behind* creditors in thin-cap + mismanagement cases without destroying the veil; **Pri HaEmek** holds that **stripping limited liability needs unanimous member consent** because the articles are a binding contract. ^subordination
