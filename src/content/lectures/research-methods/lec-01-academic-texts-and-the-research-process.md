# Lec 01: Academic Texts and the Research Process

> Part of: [[Research Methods]]
> Key concepts: [[Academic Text]], [[Non-Academic Source]], [[Literature Review]], [[Gap in the Literature]], [[Research Question]], [[Hypothesis]], [[Research Design]], [[Quantitative Research]], [[Qualitative Research]], [[Mixed Methods]], [[Abstract]], [[Plagiarism]]

> [!info] What this note covers
> The deck "Introduction to and principles of academic research". Three parts: what makes a text academic, the seven stages of empirical research and how they map onto a paper, and why we cite. The BeeHero case is the in-class exercise.

## 1. Why the academic text matters

The lecture opens with the tree falling in a forest: if nobody hears it, does it make a sound? The academic version is that **if research has not been written up, it is as if it was not done**. The published source is the end product of the academic process, and science cannot build on work it cannot read.

## 2. The five criteria of an academic text

> [!important] Learn this list
> **Author, Language, References, Content, Structure.** The 2023 sample exam asks for exactly these five ([[PP_01-Sample Exam (2023 Moed B)|PP 01, Q19]]).

| Criterion | An [[Academic Text]] has |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Affiliation to an academic institute |
| **Language** | Formal, concise, professional, not personal |
| **References** | Credible, valid and reliable sources |
| **Content** | A basis in scientific research |
| **Structure** | The rules of academic writing for that type of research |

## 3. Non-academic sources and credibility

A [[Non-Academic Source]] is not automatically useless. The question is how far it rests on **evidence rather than opinion or fake news**. The deck lists these types, and the summary lesson gives a real example of each:

| Type | Example from the course |
|---|---|

| Private research centers | IED (European Institute for Enterprise Development) |
| Primary sources | Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics |
| Online non-supervised websites | Crunchbase, PitchBook |
| Media | Calcalist, TheMarker, Forbes |

## 4. The seven stages of empirical research

1. **Reviewing academic literature.** The [[Literature Review]] is the platform of scientific knowledge the study stands on. It justifies why the study matters and leads to the question.
2. **Formulating a research question and hypotheses.** The [[Research Question]] tries to understand the relationship between variables. The [[Hypothesis|hypotheses]] propose the nature and process of that relationship. Both come out of the literature.
3. **Designing the research method**, including sample, tools and procedure.
4. **Carrying out the research and collecting data**, through manipulation or experiment, [[Questionnaire|questionnaires]], [[Interview|interviews]] or [[Observation|observation]].
5. **Analyzing the data and getting results.** Are the findings statistically significant? Do they support or reject the hypotheses?
6. **Reaching conclusions.** Interpret the results scientifically, compare them with previous work, and state theoretical and practical implications and future research.
7. **Writing the academic text.**

In the social sciences, the purpose of a research question is **to identify the interaction of multiple variables in order to predict a given outcome in a given context**.

### Designing the method

| Part of the [[Research Design]] | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Sample** | Free of confounding influences, with appropriate recruitment and sampling techniques |
| **Tools** | Valid and reliable for assessing the specific variables |
| **Procedure** | Manipulated or naturalistic, within or between subjects, random assignment or not |
| **Analysis** | Chosen to suit the methodology |

Three types of method:

- [[Quantitative Research]]: identifies and numerically describes variables to establish the relationship between them.
- [[Qualitative Research]]: recognises processes not readily measured in quantity, amount or frequency (Carney et al., 1997).
- [[Mixed Methods]]: combines the two.

## 5. How the research maps onto the paper

| Stage of research | Section of the paper | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Reviewing literature | Title, authors, [[Abstract]] | Use the title and abstract to decide quickly whether a paper is relevant. The publication tells you it is an academic source. |
| Reviewing literature | Literature review | Builds your theoretical background. Look up relevant, recent sources from it. |
| Question and hypotheses | Questions and hypotheses | Start thinking critically. What [[Gap in the Literature\|gaps]] could lead to new questions? |
| Designing the method | Method: sample, tools, procedure | Why were these chosen? Could the study be repeated, and in what context? What would you do? |
| Analyzing data | Results | Do the results validate or reject the hypotheses? |
| Reaching conclusions | Conclusion and discussion | Usually the best part to cite, and where authors suggest further questions. |
| Writing | Bibliography | A good place to find more material for your own review. |

## 6. Citing and searching

References acknowledge earlier work: "standing on the shoulders of giants". Three words to know:

- [[Plagiarism]]: "stealing" someone's idea.
- [[In-Text Citation]]: giving credit within the paper.
- [[Reference List|References]]: giving credit at the end of the paper.

The full rules are in [[Lec_02-APA Citing and Referencing]]. For database searching, the deck points to the RUNI library tutorial on the library website.

## 7. In-class case: BeeHero

BeeHero is a technology company focused on improving beekeeping and pollination. In teams, students:

1. Give an overview of the company's background, founders, products and services.
2. Formulate specific, researchable research questions on its entrepreneurship, technology and sustainability.
3. Suggest data collection tools.
4. Suggest ways of analyzing the data.
5. Raise [[Ethical Considerations|ethical considerations]] for research done inside the company.
6. Assess how its approach aligns with entrepreneurship principles.
