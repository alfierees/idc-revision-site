## 1. Qualitative data

[[Qualitative Data]] describes qualities or characteristics, usually in narrative form. **Subjective perspective is central**, since each person can perceive the same thing differently. The data includes words, body language, sighs, pauses and facial expressions.

It is also called **categorical data**, because the information can be sorted into themes and sub-themes. It tells us about people's **motivations, insights, emotions and views: what they feel and why**.

### Four ways of collecting it

1. [[Interview|Interviews]]: let the flow of the conversation lead, rather than asking question after question.
2. [[Document Analysis]]: examine verified documents such as governmental or historical records.
3. [[Observation]]: watch participants in their surroundings, using notes or recordings.
4. [[Case Study]]: an intensive study of the subject for comprehensive understanding.

### Two ways of analyzing it

| | [[Deductive Analysis\|Deductive]] | [[Inductive Analysis\|Inductive]] |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** | Top-down | Bottom-up |
| **Starting point** | A prior theory | The information itself |
| **Categories** | Already formed from theory, then applied | Constructed as themes emerge |
| **Sequence** | Theory → hypothesis → data collection → confirmation | Information → pattern → hypothesis → theory |

Forms of inductive analysis include [[Open Coding|open coding]], [[In Vivo Coding|in vivo coding]] (codes from participants' own words) and [[Constant Comparative Analysis|constant comparative analysis]].

### The process

1. Conduct the research and collect information.
2. Arrange and organize it clearly.
3. [[Coding|Code]] it into themes and sub-themes.
4. Analyze it to reach meaningful insights.
5. Draw conclusions, composing a descriptive story of the experiences.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **In-depth analysis** of the subject | **Time-consuming:** interviewing, transcribing, analyzing |
| **Understanding:** deep insights | **Hard to generalize:** smaller samples |
| **Rich data:** open questions reveal topics the researcher never considered | **Subjective:** depends on the researcher's ability to draw conclusions |

### Qualitative versus quantitative

| | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Non-numerical views and emotions | Numerically analyzed data |
| **Collection** | Open-ended surveys, unstructured interviews, observations | Closed-ended surveys, experiments |
| **Used for** | Underlying reasons, opinions, motivations | Measurable data for theories and facts |
| **Analysis** | Categorical | Statistical |
| **Population** | Small, varied | Large, representative |
| **Question** | Why did you buy that stock today? | Did you buy the Tesla stock today? Yes/No |
| **Data** | "I saw Musk tweet about Tesla today..." | 77% of participants bought the stock today |

## 2. Interview types

| | [[Structured Interview\|Structured]] | [[Semi-Structured Interview\|Semi-structured]] | [[Unstructured Interview\|Unstructured]] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Questions** | Set, prepared list, mostly closed | Prepared and ordered, but the interviewer may change them | General themes, no fixed questions |
| **Flexibility** | Low: "what you ask is what you get" | Medium to high | Highest |
| **Patterns across answers** | Easy to detect | Harder to find | Hardest |
| **Bias and engagement** | Reduces self-bias, can lower engagement | Balances reliability and validity | Risk of leading questions if inexperienced |
| **Training needed** | Minimal | Moderate to substantial | Extensive |
| **Emphasis** | Reliability | Balance | Validity |

The **semi-structured** interview is the most common.

## 3. Population and recruitment

Qualitative research does not usually generalize about its sample. It aims for **versatility**: variety in age, gender, education, experience and position, with a small number of participants (single to double digits). Sampling can happen at any phase.

Access may need approval from a [[Gatekeeper]], an authority figure who can facilitate permission, especially in large organizations.

| Method | How | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Snowball Sampling\|Snowball]] | First interviewees recommend others | Reaches hard-to-access, highly defined groups | Introduces bias into the sample |
| [[Advertising Sampling\|Advertising]] | Notices in public places, forums, group chats, websites | Reduces population bias | May not guarantee variety |

## 4. Six types of interview question

| Type | Asks about | Example from the deck |
|---|---|---|
| **Background** | Personal characteristics, often collected beforehand | Age, gender, nationality |
| **Experience** | Behavior in a scenario | "Describe what you did when you heard about the merger." |
| **Opinion** | Thoughts on a subject | "Tell me about your views on the recent election." |
| **Emotional** | What they felt | "How did you feel when you heard about the recent increase in employee productivity?" |
| **Knowledge** | What they know | "Tell me what you know about Felix's incursions." |
| **Sensory** | What they saw, heard, touched, tasted or smelled | "What do you recall about the taste of Agnes' famous shepherd's pie?" |

### How to conduct the interview

Know your topic. Be sensitive and empathetic, attentive, clear and coherent. Make sure you understand what is said, balance your talking, be warm, listen actively ("Wow", "That's amazing"), welcome silence, stay flexible and stay focused.

## 5. Content analysis

[[Content Analysis]] identifies patterns by systematically working through text. It quantifies the repetition of words, phrases, topics or ideas. These repetitions become **themes or codes**, which allow inferences about meaning and the relationships between themes.

1. **Choose the content** to analyze, based on your topic.
2. **Define the categories:** which words, phrases or concepts belong together.
3. **Develop coding rules** for sorting units of meaning into categories.
4. **Code the text,** placing supporting text in its category, which can produce new sub-themes.
5. **Analyze the results** and draw conclusions about the research question.

> [!example] A coding agenda (from the deck)
> One variable, "anxiety", with three codes and a rule for each:
>
> | Code | Definition | Example | Coding rule |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Much anxiety** | Strong feelings of worry or threat in learning | "I have to study very much to get a good grade" | Over 80% of diary entries support it |
> | **Some anxiety** | Some aspects or moderate feelings | "I had many mistakes" | Under 80% of diary entries point to it |
> | **No anxiety** | Absolutely no anxiety | "Nothing, I understood all" | Concrete statements of no anxiety |

The deck also shows the coding ladder from raw text upward: **meaning unit → open code → subcategory → category**.

## 6. Recording

Keep a journal of your thoughts, views and observations during the process. This is [[Memoing|memoing]], and it can be the first form of data analysis. Use audio or video, because writing everything down is very difficult and recording lets you focus on the conversation.

> [!summary] The interview study, start to finish
> Theory → research question → hypothesis formulation → sampling → data collection → coding scheme → coding → findings and conclusions.
