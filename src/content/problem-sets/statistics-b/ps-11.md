    text: |
      **Jordan's die and bag of balls.** Jordan rolls a 4-sided die. He then draws out $n$ balls (with replacement) out of a bag with 50% white balls and 50% black balls and counts the number of black balls he drew, where $n$ is the number he got on the die. For example, if he rolled a 3, he will take out 3 balls and count how many black balls he got.

      Mark $X$ = number on the die, $Y$ = number of black balls drawn.

      What is the correlation coefficient between $X$ and $Y$, $\rho_{x,y}$?
    solution: |
      **Step 1: the joint distribution.** Given the die shows $X=k$, the black-ball count is binomial with $n=k$ trials and $p=0.5$ ([[Binomial Distribution]]):

      $$
      Y \mid X=k \;\sim\; B(k,\,0.5)
      $$

      The binomial probability mass function gives

      $$
      p(Y=l \mid X=k)=\binom{k}{l}\,0.5^{\,l}\,0.5^{\,k-l}
      $$

      The die is fair, so $p(X=k)=\tfrac14$ for $k=1,\dots,4$. The joint probability of each pair is the product:

      $$
      p(X=k,\,Y=l)=p(X=k)\cdot p(Y=l\mid X=k)=\frac14\binom{k}{l}\,0.5^{\,l}\,0.5^{\,k-l}
      $$

      > [!example] One cell
      > $X=1,\ Y=0$: $\dfrac14\cdot\dbinom{1}{0}\cdot0.5^0\cdot0.5^1=\dfrac14\cdot1\cdot1\cdot0.5=0.125$.

      Filling every cell gives the [[Joint Distribution]] table:

      | | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ | $Y=2$ | $Y=3$ | $Y=4$ |
      |---|---|---|---|---|---|
      | $X=1$ | 0.125 | 0.125 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
      | $X=2$ | 0.0625 | 0.125 | 0.0625 | 0 | 0 |
      | $X=3$ | 0.03125 | 0.09375 | 0.09375 | 0.03125 | 0 |
      | $X=4$ | 0.015625 | 0.0625 | 0.09375 | 0.0625 | 0.015625 |

      ![](/images/statistics-b/ps11-q1-joint-distribution-heatmap.png)

      **Step 2: the marginals.** The [[Marginal Distribution]] is the sum of each row (for $X$) or each column (for $Y$):

      | | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ | $Y=2$ | $Y=3$ | $Y=4$ | $p(X=k)$ |
      |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
      | $X=1$ | 0.125 | 0.125 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0.25 |
      | $X=2$ | 0.0625 | 0.125 | 0.0625 | 0 | 0 | 0.25 |
      | $X=3$ | 0.03125 | 0.09375 | 0.09375 | 0.03125 | 0 | 0.25 |
      | $X=4$ | 0.015625 | 0.0625 | 0.09375 | 0.0625 | 0.015625 | 0.25 |
      | $p(Y=l)$ | 0.234375 | 0.40625 | 0.25 | 0.09375 | 0.015625 | |

      **Step 3: expected values.**

      $$
      E(X)=0.25\,(1+2+3+4)=2.5
      $$

      $$
      E(Y)=1\cdot0.40625+2\cdot0.25+3\cdot0.09375+4\cdot0.015625=1.25
      $$

      **Step 4: standard deviations.**

      $$
      Var(X)=0.25\left[(1-2.5)^2+(2-2.5)^2+(3-2.5)^2+(4-2.5)^2\right]=0.25\,(2.25\cdot2+0.25\cdot2)=1.25
      $$

      $$
      SD(X)=\sqrt{1.25}=1.118
      $$

      $$
      Var(Y)=0.234375(0-1.25)^2+0.40625(1-1.25)^2+0.25(2-1.25)^2+0.09375(3-1.25)^2+0.015625(4-1.25)^2=0.9375
      $$

      $$
      SD(Y)=\sqrt{0.9375}=0.968
      $$

      **Step 5: covariance.** Use the working formula $Cov(X,Y)=E(XY)-E(X)E(Y)$. Grouping cells with equal probability, the solution computes

      $$
      E(XY)=0.125(1\cdot1+1\cdot2)+0.09375(1\cdot3+2\cdot3+2\cdot4)+0.0625(1\cdot4+2\cdot2+3\cdot4)+0.03125\cdot3\cdot3+0.015625\cdot4\cdot4=3.75
      $$

      (each bracket lists the products $x\cdot y$ of the cells that share that probability).

      $$
      Cov(X,Y)=3.75-2.5\cdot1.25=0.625
      $$

      **Step 6: correlation.**

      $$
      \rho_{x,y}=\frac{Cov(X,Y)}{SD(X)\,SD(Y)}=\frac{0.625}{1.118\cdot0.968}=0.578
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $\rho_{x,y}=0.578$: a moderate-to-strong positive linear relationship (see [[Lec_11-Joint Distributions, Correlation and Linear Regression]], section 6).

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > A bigger die roll means more balls drawn, so more black balls on average: $E(Y\mid X=k)=k/2$. That is why the correlation is clearly positive. It is not close to 1 because, for a fixed number of balls, the black count is still random.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > A shortcut that gives the same number: since $E(Y\mid X)=X/2$, we get $Cov(X,Y)=Cov(X,X/2)=Var(X)/2=0.625$, and $Var(Y)=E[Var(Y\mid X)]+Var(E[Y\mid X])=E(X)/4+Var(X)/4=0.9375$. The exact value is then $1/\sqrt3=0.5774$, which the solution rounds to 0.578. This check is mine, not in the source.

      Create a simulation that repeats Jordan's experiment 50 times and calculates the correlation between $X$ and $Y$. Use only arithmetic operators and `=Average()`, `=Sum()`, `=Sqrt()`. Hint: `=BINOM.INV(n,p,RAND())` draws from a binomial.
    solution: |
      **Method (from the official workbook, sheets "Q1 part ii" and "Q1 part iii").**

      | Column | Formula | Meaning |
      |---|---|---|
      | X | `=RANDBETWEEN(1,4)` | the die roll |
      | Y | `=BINOM.INV(X,0.5,RAND())` | black balls among $X$ draws |
      | X deviation, Y deviation | `=X-AVERAGE`, `=Y-AVERAGE` | $x_i-\bar X$, $y_i-\bar Y$ |
      | squared deviations and product | `=dev^2`, `=devX*devY` | building blocks |
      | correlation | sum of products over the square root of the product of the two sums of squares | Pearson's $r$ |

      The correlation is built only from `Average`, `Sum` and `Sqrt`, as the question demands:

      $$
      r_{x,y}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar X)(y_i-\bar Y)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar X)^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\bar Y)^2}}
      $$

      The workbook checks itself against Excel's built-in correlation, and the two agree to many decimals.

      > [!note] One simulated run
      > In the workbook's saved run the 50-repetition sheet gives $r=0.509$, against the true $\rho=0.578$. This is a single draw. The solution reports that across repeated 50-repetition runs the correlations come out about **0.28 to 0.74**, a wide range.

      | Day | TLV100 | Dow Jones |

      Calculate a prediction equation to predict the value of TLV100 from the value of the Dow Jones. Use the `=COVARIANCE.S()` and `=VAR.S()` Excel functions. You can check your answers by using the `=SLOPE()` and `=INTERCEPT()` functions.

      Mark TLV100 as $Y$ and Dow Jones as $X$. The sample regression line is $\hat Y=bX+a$ with

      $$
      b=\frac{Cov(X,Y)}{Var(X)},\qquad a=\bar Y-b\,\bar X

      (see [[Linear Regression]] and [[Lec_11-Joint Distributions, Correlation and Linear Regression]], section 9.5). Here both $Cov$ and $Var$ are the sample versions with divisor $n-1$.

      > [!example] Working
      > $Var(X)=15411.24$ and $Cov(X,Y)=6969.14$.
      > $b=\dfrac{6969.14}{15411.24}=0.452$.
      > $\bar X=11100.71$ and $\bar Y=1232.43$.
      > $a=1232.43-0.452\cdot11100.71=-3787.44$.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $\hat Y=0.452\,X-3787.44$

      The workbook confirms with `=SLOPE()` and `=INTERCEPT()`: the same $b$ and $a$.

      ![](/images/statistics-b/ps11-q2-regression-line.png)

      a=\bar Y-b\,\bar X=0-r_{x,y}\cdot0=0

      | Direction | Slope |
      |---|---|

      | X from Y (Dow from TLV100) | 0.856 |

      > Both equations are $\hat Y=0.856\,X$ (intercept 0, slope $=r_{x,y}=0.856$): the equation does **not** depend on which index is predicted.

      > [!warning] Reading the solution
      > The solution says "the prediction equations will be the same".       $X$ is `=RAND()` in every part except (d). The correlation is `=PEARSON(X,Y)`.

      | Part | Formula for $Y$ | Why it behaves as required | Observed $r$ (one run) |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | a | `=X+RAND()` | $Y$ rises with $X$ but carries extra independent noise | 0.701 |

      where $U$ is the independent `RAND()` term. This theoretical value is my addition; the observed 0.701 is close to it.

      Same setup as 3a, except that here $X$ is built so that it is symmetric around zero.

      Simulate $Y$ so that $X$ and $Y$ are perfectly dependent but not correlated. What formula did you use, what correlation did you get, and how close is it to your expectation?
    solution: |
      Take $X$ symmetric around zero, $X=$ `RAND()-0.5` (uniform between $-0.5$ and $0.5$), and $Y=X^2$. Then $Y$ is **completely determined** by $X$, yet the positive and negative halves of the curve cancel in the covariance:

      $$
      Cov(X,Y)=E(X^3)-E(X)\,E(X^2)=0-0=0
      $$

      so the true correlation is 0. The observed 0.078 is again sampling noise around 0.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > [[Correlation Coefficient]] only detects **linear** association. A U-shape is as dependent as a relationship can be, but a straight line fits it no better than a flat one. **Zero correlation does not imply independence** ([[Independence of Random Variables]]); independence does imply zero correlation.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $Y=X^2$ with $X=$ `RAND()-0.5`. Observed $r=0.078$ (one run), close to the expected 0. Across all four parts the observed correlations were close to expectations: 0.70 (about 0.71), $-0.69$ (about $-0.71$), $-0.06$ (about 0) and 0.08 (about 0), in this one run.

      $Y=(2X)^2+$ `NORM.INV(RAND(),0,0.2)` is a clean parabola. The scatterplot is an obvious U-shape, yet the **linear trendline is almost flat** (fitted slope $-0.134$, intercept 3.932, one run) because the left and right arms cancel.

      > [!success] Answer
      > Related-but-not-linearly data: a straight-line fit and Pearson's $r$ (about $-0.03$ in this run) badly **understate** the dependence. **Always plot the data** before trusting a correlation or a regression line.
