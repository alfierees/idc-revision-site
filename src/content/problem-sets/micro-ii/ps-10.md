      **Question 1 (noise externality).** Factory A produces air conditioners and Factory B, located next to it, produces bottles. Noise from A's production breaks some of B's bottles, which raises B's production cost. Let $a$ be the quantity produced by A and $b$ the quantity produced by B. The costs are

      $$
      C_A(a) = a^2, \qquad C_B(b,a) = b^2 + b\cdot a,
      $$

      and the prices are $p_a = 40$ and $p_b = 80$.

      **(a)** What are the quantity of air conditioners and the quantity of bottles in a competitive equilibrium, and what is each firm's profit?
    solution: |
      **Firm A** has no externality on itself:

      $$
      \pi_A = 40a - a^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{d\pi_A}{da} = 40 - 2a = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a^* = 20,
      $$

      $$
      \pi_A = 40\cdot 20 - 20^2 = 400.
      $$

      **Firm B** takes $a$ as given, even though $a$ hurts it:

      $$
      \pi_B = 80b - (b^2 + ba) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{d\pi_B}{db} = 80 - 2b - a = 0.
      $$

      With $a^*=20$: $80 - 2b - 20 = 0$, so $b^* = 30$.

      $$
      \pi_B = 80\cdot 30 - 30^2 - 30\cdot 20 = 2400 - 900 - 600 = 900.
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $a^* = 20,\ b^* = 30$; profits $\pi_A = 400$, $\pi_B = 900$ (total $1300$).

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > A price-taker maximises its **own** payoff and treats the other party's choice as a constant. So the externality term drops out of A's first-order condition. The social planner keeps it. The gap between the two FOCs *is* the externality.

      **Question 1 (noise externality).** Factory A produces air conditioners and Factory B, located next to it, produces bottles. Noise from A's production breaks some of B's bottles, which raises B's production cost. Let $a$ be the quantity produced by A and $b$ the quantity produced by B. The costs are

      $$
      C_A(a) = a^2, \qquad C_B(b,a) = b^2 + b\cdot a,
      $$

      and the prices are $p_a = 40$ and $p_b = 80$.

      **(b)** What are the quantities produced in the efficient allocation?
    solution: |
      The planner maximises **joint** profit, so A's output now shows up in B's cost:

      $$
      \max_{a,b}\; 40a - a^2 + 80b - (b^2 + ba).
      $$

      FOCs:

      $$
      \frac{\partial}{\partial a}: \; 40 - 2a - b = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial b}: \; 80 - 2b - a = 0.
      $$

      From the second, $a = 80 - 2b$. Substitute into the first:

      $$
      40 - 2(80-2b) - b = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 3b = 120 \;\Rightarrow\; b^* = 40,\ a^* = 0.
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > Efficient allocation: $a = 0,\ b = 40$. Joint profit $= 0 + 80\cdot 40 - 40^2 = 1600 > 1300$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > Each air conditioner earns A at most $40$ at the margin but costs B $b = 40$ in extra breakage when B sells $40$ bottles. At the efficient $b$ the marginal private gain is entirely cancelled by the marginal damage, so the efficient $a$ is the corner-like value $0$. The competitive outcome over-produces $a$ ($20$ instead of $0$) and under-produces $b$ ($30$ instead of $40$).

      > [!tip] Check
      > The Hessian of the joint-profit function is $\begin{pmatrix}-2&-1\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, negative definite (determinant $3>0$, leading entry negative), so the FOC solution is the maximum.

      ![](/images/micro-ii/ps10-q1-externality-equilibrium-vs-efficient.png)

      *The blue line is B's condition (identical in the private and planner problems). The solid red line is A's private best response ($a=20$), meeting blue at the competitive point. The dashed red line is A's condition once the damage $b$ is included, meeting blue at the efficient point $(0,40)$.*

      **Question 1 (noise externality).** Factory A produces air conditioners and Factory B, located next to it, produces bottles. Noise from A's production breaks some of B's bottles, which raises B's production cost. Let $a$ be the quantity produced by A and $b$ the quantity produced by B. The costs are

      $$
      C_A(a) = a^2, \qquad C_B(b,a) = b^2 + b\cdot a,
      $$

      and the prices are $p_a = 40$ and $p_b = 80$.

      Now firm A needs a "noise permit" for each unit it produces. Permits trade in a competitive market at price $r$. In the initial endowment firm A receives $\omega_A$ permits and firm B receives $\omega_B$, and $\Omega = \omega_A+\omega_B$.

      **(d)** Write the maximisation problem of firm A and of firm B.
    solution: |
      **Firm A** is endowed with $\omega_A$ permits it can sell at $r$, and buys one permit per unit produced:

      $$
      \max_a\; 40a - a^2 + r\omega_A - ra.
      $$

      - $r\omega_A$ is a lump-sum wealth term, independent of choices.
      - $-ra$ is the cost of the permits A must hold to produce $a$.

      **Firm B** holds $\omega_B$ permits. Let $y$ be the number of permits B **keeps** (retires, so they are not used for noise). Noise equals the permits others use, $\Omega - y$:

      $$
      \max_{b,\,y}\; 80b - b^2 - b(\Omega - y) + r\omega_B - ry.
      $$

      - $b(\Omega-y)$ is the breakage cost: more permits kept by B means less noise.
      - $r\omega_B$ is lump-sum wealth.
      - $-ry$ is the opportunity cost of not selling permits.

      > [!info] Setting up the permit market
      > Permits are a property right over noise. A and B trade them just like any other good, which is the [[Coase Theorem]] logic: define the right, let a market price it, and the externality is internalised.

Noise from A's production breaks some of B's bottles, which raises B's production cost. Let $a$ be the quantity produced by A and $b$ the quantity produced by B. The costs are

      $$
      C_A(a) = a^2, \qquad C_B(b,a) = b^2 + b\cdot a,
      $$

      and the prices are $p_a = 40$ and $p_b = 80$.

      Now firm A needs a "noise permit" for each unit it produces. Permits trade in a competitive market at price $r$. In the initial endowment firm A receives $\omega_A$ permits and firm B receives $\omega_B$, and $\Omega = \omega_A+\omega_B$.

      **(e)** Find the equilibrium in the market: what is the equilibrium price $r$? How many units of good A and good B are produced? Does the initial allocation of permits ($\omega_A$ and $\omega_B$) change the equilibrium quantities?
    solution: |
      **FOCs.** For firm A:

      $$
      40 - 2a - r = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a = 20 - \frac{r}{2}. \tag{1}
      $$

      For firm B, with respect to $b$ and $y$:

      $$
      80 - 2b - (\Omega - y) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Omega - y = 80 - 2b, \tag{2}
      $$

      $$
      b - r = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; b = r. \tag{3}
      $$

      **Why (3):** one more kept permit lowers B's breakage cost by $b$ (the units it makes) and costs it $r$ in forgone sales.

      **Combine.** Substituting (3) into (2) gives $\Omega - y = 80 - 2r$. The permit market clears when the permits A uses equal the permits not kept by B, $a = \Omega - y$:

      $$
      20 - \frac{r}{2} = 80 - 2r \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{3r}{2} = 60 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 40.
      $$

      Then $a^* = 20 - 20 = 0$, $b^* = r = 40$, and $y^* = \Omega$ (B keeps every permit).

      > [!success] Answer
      > $r = 40$, $a^* = 0$, $b^* = 40$. This is exactly the efficient allocation from (b), and $r$ equals the Pigouvian tax $t=40$. The initial split $(\omega_A,\omega_B)$ does **not** change the quantities: $\omega_A$ and $\omega_B$ enter profits only as lump-sum terms $r\omega_i$, which move income but not marginal incentives. Quantities depend only on the total $\Omega$ (and here, not even on that).

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > The permit price $r$ plays the role of the tax. The difference is who gets the money: with a tax the government collects $ta$; with permits, whoever holds the endowment does. That redistribution is the lever in (f).

      **Question 2 (consumption externalities).** A production economy with two agents, Homer (H) and Barney (B), and two goods, C and W. Each enjoys their own bundle but is hurt by the *other's* bundle:

      $$
      U_H(c_H, w_H, c_B) = 10 + c_H^{1/2}w_H^{1/2} - c_B^{2/3}w_B^{1/3}
      $$

      $$
      U_B(c_B, w_B, c_H) = 10 + c_B^{1/2}w_B^{1/2} - c_H^{2/3}w_H^{1/3}
      $$

      Initial endowments are $(\omega_c, \omega_w) = (18, 20)$ for each agent.

      **(a)** Find the equilibrium allocation and prices, and calculate the agents' utilities in equilibrium.
    solution: |
      > [!tip] How to handle the externality term
      > The other agent's bundle appears only in the $-c_B^{2/3}w_B^{1/3}$ term. The consumer **cannot control** it, so it is a constant in the demand problem. Each agent therefore behaves like an ordinary Cobb-Douglas consumer with $U = c^{1/2}w^{1/2}$. Take W as numeraire ($p_w = 1$) and let $p_c$ be the price of C.

      Each agent solves

      $$
      \max_{c,w}\; c^{1/2}w^{1/2} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad p_c c + w = 18p_c + 20.
      $$

      Set $MRS = $ price ratio:

      $$
      \frac{w}{c} = p_c \;\Rightarrow\; w = p_c c. \tag{1}
      $$

      Substitute into the budget constraint:

      $$
      p_c c + p_c c = 18p_c + 20 \;\Rightarrow\; c = 9 + \frac{10}{p_c}.
      $$

      Agents are identical, so total demand is $c^T = 18 + \dfrac{20}{p_c}$. The C-market clears at total supply $36$:

      $$
      18 + \frac{20}{p_c} = 36 \;\Rightarrow\; p_c = \frac{10}{9}.
      $$

      Then $c = 9 + 9 = 18$ and, from (1), $w = \frac{10}{9}\cdot 18 = 20$.

      **Utility:** each agent consumes $(18,20)$, and so does the other, so

      $$
      U = 10 + 18^{1/2}\cdot 20^{1/2} - 18^{2/3}\cdot 20^{1/3} \approx 10 + 18.97 - 18.64 = 10.33.
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $p_c = 10/9$ (with $p_w=1$); each agent consumes $(c,w) = (18,20)$ (their own endowment, no trade); $U_H = U_B \approx 10.33$.

      **Question 3 (public goods).** An economy with $N$ agents and two goods, a public good $G$ and money $Y$. Agent $i$ contributes $g_i$ to the public good (one dollar per unit), total provision is $G = \sum_i g_i$, and

      $$
      U_i = V(G) + y_i = V(G) + (I - g_i),
      $$

      where $V$ is the same valuation function of the public good for all agents, and all agents have the same income $I$ (assume it is large enough never to bind).

      > [!info] Assumptions the solution uses implicitly
      > $V$ is increasing and concave ($V'>0$, $V''<0$), so $V'$ is decreasing and equations like $V'(g^o)=1$ have a unique solution.

      **(a)** Show that for any $N$ there exists a Nash equilibrium where all agents take the same action (a symmetric equilibrium), and prove that it is a Nash equilibrium. How does the overall quantity of the public good in equilibrium change as the number of agents in the economy increases?
    solution: |
      **Step 1: the one-person benchmark.** Define $g^o$ by

      $$
      V'(g^o) = 1.
      $$

      This is the amount of public good one selfish person would choose if they alone paid and chose $G$: marginal benefit $V'$ equals marginal cost $1$.

      **Step 2: best response.** Agent $i$ takes others' contributions $G_{-i} = \sum_{j\ne i} g_j$ as fixed and wants the total to be $g^o$, so contributes $g^o - G_{-i}$, but never a negative amount:

      $$
      BR_i(G_{-i}) = \begin{cases} g^o - G_{-i} & \text{if } G_{-i} < g^o \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
      $$

      **Step 3: symmetric equilibrium.** Suppose everyone gives $g$. Then $G_{-i} = (N-1)g$ and agent $i$ wants to top up to $g^o$:

      $$
      (N-1)g + g = g^o \;\Rightarrow\; g = \frac{g^o}{N}.
      $$

      **Proof that $g^o/N$ is a Nash equilibrium.** If all others give $g^o/N$, then $G_{-i} = (N-1)g^o/N < g^o$, so the best response is $g^o - (N-1)g^o/N = g^o/N$. That is exactly what agent $i$ is already doing, so no one wants to deviate. This holds for every $N$.

      $$
      G^{NE} = N\cdot\frac{g^o}{N} = g^o.
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > Symmetric equilibrium: each agent contributes $g^o/N$ where $V'(g^o)=1$. Total provision is $G^{NE} = g^o$, **independent of $N$**. Each person's share falls as $N$ grows, so more people means more free riding, but the total does not move.

      **Question 3 (public goods).** An economy with $N$ agents and two goods, a public good $G$ and money $Y$. Agent $i$ contributes $g_i$ to the public good (one dollar per unit), total provision is $G = \sum_i g_i$, and

      $$
      U_i = V(G) + y_i = V(G) + (I - g_i),
      $$

      where $V$ is the same valuation function of the public good for all agents, and all agents have the same income $I$ (assume it is large enough never to bind).

      > [!info] Assumptions the solution uses implicitly
      > $V$ is increasing and concave ($V'>0$, $V''<0$), so $V'$ is decreasing and equations like $V'(g^o)=1$ have a unique solution.

      **(b)** Derive the quantity that maximises social welfare. How does this quantity change as the number of agents increases? What does this say about how the market failure changes as $N$ grows?
    solution: |
      The planner maximises the sum of utilities (the $NI$ is a constant):

      $$
      \max_g\; \sum_{i=1}^N V(g) + NI - g = N\,V(g) + NI - g.
      $$

      FOC:

      $$
      N\,V'(g^*) - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; V'(g^*) = \frac1N.
      $$

      Each unit gives the benefit $V'$ to **all $N$** people but costs $1$ once.

      **Comparison with the Nash equilibrium.** $V'(G^{NE}) = 1$ while $V'(G^*) = 1/N < 1$. Since $V'$ is decreasing, a smaller marginal benefit requires a larger quantity:

      $$
      G^* > G^{NE} \quad (\text{for } N \ge 2).
      $$

      As $N$ rises, $1/N$ falls, so $G^*$ **rises**, while $G^{NE} = g^o$ stays fixed.

      > [!success] Answer
      > The efficient quantity solves $V'(g^*) = 1/N$ and **increases with $N$**. The market always [[Under-Provision|under-provides]], and because $G^{NE}$ is constant while $G^*$ grows, **the market failure gets larger as the economy gets larger.**

      ![](/images/micro-ii/ps10-q3-public-good-under-provision.png)

      *Schematic, not to scale. The blue line is one agent's marginal benefit $V'(G)$. The selfish equilibrium stops where $V'=1$ (cost of a unit). The planner goes on until $V'=1/N$, which sits lower and so further right. The plotted $V'$ and the example $N$ are illustrative only.*

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > The equilibrium counts only **one** person's benefit per unit ($V'$ vs cost $1$); the planner counts **all $N$** ($NV'$ vs $1$). With more people the gap between private and social marginal benefit widens: that is the [[Externalities]] story with $N-1$ beneficiaries instead of one.

      $$
      U_1 = V_1(G) + y_1 + \alpha\big(V_2(G) + y_2\big), \qquad \alpha \le 1,
      $$

      $$
      U_2 = V_2(G) + y_2 + \beta\big(V_1(G) + y_1\big), \qquad \beta \le 1.
      $$

      ($\alpha = 0$: selfish. $\alpha\in(0,1)$: partly altruistic. $\alpha=1$: fully altruistic.)

      > [!info] Assumptions
      > As in Q3: $V_i' > 0$ and $V_i'' < 0$, and $\alpha,\beta \ge 0$.

      **(a)** Derive what the possible equilibria are in this case.
    solution: |
      Agent 1 chooses $g_1$ and treats $g_2$, $I_1$, $I_2$ as constants. Substituting $y_1 = I_1 - g_1$ and $y_2 = I_2 - g_2$, the part of $U_1$ that depends on $g_1$ is

      $$
      V_1(G) + \alpha V_2(G) - g_1.
      $$

      FOC:

      $$
      V_1'(G) + \alpha V_2'(G) - 1 = 0.
      $$

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > I raise my contribution until my own marginal benefit **plus $\alpha$ times your marginal benefit** equals my marginal cost.

      Define $g'$ and $g''$ by

      $$
      V_1'(g') + \alpha V_2'(g') = 1, \qquad V_2'(g'') + \beta V_1'(g'') = 1.
      $$

      (The recitation slide writes the first as "$V_1'(g') + \alpha V_1'(g') = 1$"; the FOC derived on the previous slide has $V_2'$ in the second term, so that is a typo and this page uses $V_2'$.)

      Best responses:

      $$
      BR_1(g_2) = \begin{cases} g' - g_2 & g_2 < g' \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad BR_2(g_1) = \begin{cases} g'' - g_1 & g_1 < g'' \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
      $$

      > [!success] Answer: the equilibria
      > - If $g' = g''$: **any** pair with $g_1 + g_2 = g' = g''$ is an equilibrium (a continuum).
      > - If $g' > g''$: the unique equilibrium is $g_1 = g',\ g_2 = 0$ (agent 2 free rides).
      > - If $g' < g''$: the unique equilibrium is $g_1 = 0,\ g_2 = g''$ (agent 1 free rides).
      >
      > In every case the total provision is $G^{eq} = \max\{g', g''\}$.

      **(d)** Show that when either $\alpha = 1$ or $\beta = 1$ the equilibrium quantity is equal to the socially optimal quantity.
    solution: |
      If $\alpha = 1$, agent 1's FOC becomes

      $$
      V_1'(G) + V_2'(G) = 1,
      $$

      which is exactly the planner's condition (Samuelson: sum of marginal benefits equals marginal cost). Agent 1 behaves like a social planner. The same follows for $\beta = 1$ and agent 2.

      > [!success] Answer
      > With $\alpha=1$, $g' = G^*$ (the socially optimal quantity); with $\beta=1$, $g'' = G^*$.

      > [!tip] Check: why the max picks the planner's quantity
      > The recitation does not spell this out. Suppose $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta \le 1$. At $G^*$ we have $V_1'(G^*)+V_2'(G^*) = 1$, so $V_2'(G^*) + \beta V_1'(G^*) \le V_2'(G^*) + V_1'(G^*) = 1$, which means agent 2's FOC is already $\le 1$ at $G^*$ and so $g'' \le G^* = g'$. Hence $\max\{g',g''\} = g' = G^*$. (If $\beta<1$ and $V_1'>0$ the inequality is strict, so agent 2 does not contribute; agent 1 pays everything.)
