      **The cheese market.** The government has decided that the free-market price of cheese is too low.
    solution: |
      Parts a to c follow.

      > [!tip] Concepts you need
      > - A [[Price Ceiling]] is a legal maximum price. A [[Price Floor]] is a legal minimum price.
      > - A ceiling is **binding** when the equilibrium price is **above** it. A floor is binding when the equilibrium price is **below** it ([[Binding Constraint]]).
      > - A binding floor leaves price above equilibrium, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded ([[Surplus]]). A binding ceiling does the opposite ([[Shortage]]).
      >
      > Think "the rule only bites if it forbids the market price". A floor below equilibrium, or a ceiling above it, changes nothing.

  - id: "1a"
    text: |
      Suppose the government imposes a binding price floor in the cheese market. In the supply-and-demand diagram show the effect of this policy on the price of cheese and the quantity of cheese sold. Is there a shortage or surplus of cheese?
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer
      > Price rises from $P^*$ to $P^{floor}$. At that price buyers want $Q^d$ and sellers want to supply $Q^s$, with $Q^d < Q^* < Q^s$. There is a **surplus** of cheese.

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps10-q1a-price-floor-surplus.png)

      *Theory diagram (stylised curves, no numbers). The floor sits above $P^*$, so it is binding. The quantity actually sold is $Q^d$, the smaller of the two.*

      **Steps.**

      1. The floor is above $P^*$, so the market cannot settle at $P^*$.
      2. At $P^{floor}$ the demand curve gives $Q^d$ (less than $Q^*$), the supply curve gives $Q^s$ (more than $Q^*$).
      3. Surplus $= Q^s - Q^d > 0$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > Only $Q^d$ units can actually be sold, because a trade needs a willing buyer. Sellers wanted to sell $Q^s$, so the gap is unsold cheese.

  - id: "1b"
    text: |
      Producers of cheese complain that the price floor has reduced their total revenue. Is it possible that their revenue decreased? Explain. Reminder: revenue is $P \times Q$. Study what happened to $P$ and $Q$ following the change.
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer
      > Yes, it is possible. Price rises but quantity sold falls (from $Q^*$ to $Q^d$). Revenue falls if the fall in quantity outweighs the rise in price, which happens when demand is **elastic** (price elasticity of demand greater than 1 in absolute value).

      **Reasoning.**

      1. Producers see a higher price and a lower quantity sold.
      2. Revenue falls only if the percentage drop in quantity is larger than the percentage rise in price:

      $$
      \left|\frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}\right| > 1
      $$

      3. That ratio is the price elasticity of demand, so revenue falls when demand is elastic. In elastic demand, a price increase lowers [[Total Revenue]] (see [[Lec_08-Elasticity]]).

      > [!example] Numerical example from the solution
      > Suppose the floor is 10% above the equilibrium price and $Q^d$ is 15% below $Q^*$.
      >
      > Elasticity in absolute value is $0.15 / 0.10 = 1.5 > 1$.
      >
      > $$
      > P^{floor} = 1.1 \, P^*, \qquad Q^d = 0.85 \, Q^*
      > $$
      >
      > New revenue:
      >
      > $$
      > 1.1 \, P^* \times 0.85 \, Q^* = 0.935 \, P^* Q^*
      > $$
      >
      > This is below the initial revenue $P^* Q^*$, so revenue decreased.

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps10-q1b-revenue-areas.png)

      *Theory diagram. Initial revenue is the orange plus blue rectangles ($P^* \times Q^*$). Revenue after the floor is the orange plus yellow rectangles ($P^{floor} \times Q^d$). The blue rectangle (revenue lost from selling fewer units) is larger than the yellow one (revenue gained from the higher price), so revenue falls. This is the elastic case. The relative sizes are an illustration, not data.*

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > If demand were inelastic the yellow area would be the larger one, and producers would gain from the floor. The complaint in the question only makes sense in the elastic case.
    related_terms: ["total-revenue", "price-elasticity-of-demand", "price-floor", "elasticity"]

  - id: "1c"
    text: |
      In response to the cheese producers' complaints, the government agrees to purchase all the surplus cheese at the price floor. Compared to the initial price (without the price floor), who benefits from this new policy? Who loses? To answer, draw the consumer surplus, producer surplus and the cost to the government.
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer
      > **Producers benefit.** They now sell $Q^s$ units at $P^{floor}$, so their revenue is
      >
      > $$
      > P^{floor} \times Q^s
      > $$
      >
      > which is larger than the initial revenue $P^* \times Q^*$.
      >
      > **Consumers lose.** They pay a higher price and buy fewer units than in the free-market equilibrium, and their consumer surplus declines.

      **Reasoning.**

      1. Consumers still buy only $Q^d$. The extra cheese $Q^s - Q^d$ is bought by the government.
      2. The extra revenue producers receive is the government's payment for the surplus units:

      $$
      P^{floor} \times (Q^s - Q^d)
      $$

      3. Adding it to the revenue from consumers, $P^{floor} \times Q^d$, gives total revenue

      $$
      P^{floor} \times Q^s
      $$

      4. In the solution's diagram, this is the orange, yellow and green areas together.

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps10-q1c-government-purchase.png)

      *Theory diagram. Orange and yellow: revenue from consumers at $Q^d$. Green: the extra revenue paid by the government for the surplus units, $P^{floor} \times (Q^s - Q^d)$. No numbers.*

      > [!tip] Intuition (my addition, not in the solution)
      > The green area is also the **cost to the government**, which the booklet asks you to draw but the solution does not discuss. It is paid out of taxes, so whoever funds the programme (taxpayers) is a further loser. The solution itself names only producers (better off) and consumers (hurt).

      **Multiple choice, question 1.** Which of the following is the most likely explanation for the imposition of a price ceiling on the market for milk?

      - (a) Policymakers have studied the effects of the price ceiling carefully, and they recognize that the price ceiling is advantageous for society as a whole.
      - (b) Buyers of milk, recognizing that the price ceiling is good for them, have pressured policymakers into imposing the price ceiling.
      - (c) Sellers of milk, recognizing that the price ceiling is good for them, have pressured policymakers into imposing the price ceiling.
      - (d) Buyers and sellers of milk have agreed that the price ceiling is good for both of them and have therefore pressured policymakers into imposing the price ceiling.
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer: b
      > A ceiling lowers the price buyers pay, so buyers lobby for it. Sellers lose from it, so (c) and (d) are out. (a) is wrong because a ceiling creates a shortage and deadweight loss, so it is not advantageous for society as a whole ([[Lec_10-Price Controls]]).
