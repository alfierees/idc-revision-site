      **Changes in supply and in quantity supplied.** For each task, provide a graph, say whether supply increases, decreases or does not change, say whether the cause is the good's own price or a non-price determinant, and name the variable if it is a non-price determinant.

      Non-price determinants of supply: changes in technology, in the price of intermediate goods, and in expectations.

      **(a)** What would happen to the supply of foreign holidays if there were a fall in the price of foreign holidays? Show the change on the graph (the sheet gives S0, P0 and q0). Is this outcome the result of a change in the price of the product or of a non-price determinant?
    solution: |
      This is a **movement along the supply curve**. With a lower price, the **quantity supplied falls** (from q0 to q1). The supply curve itself does not shift. The cause is the **price of the product itself**, so there is no non-price determinant.

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps04-q1a-foreign-holidays-movement.png)

      *Figure: a theory diagram. The price falls from P0 to P1 and the market moves down the same curve S from q0 to q1.*
    related_terms: ["change-in-quantity-supplied", "supply-curve", "movement-along-a-curve"]

  - id: "1b"
    text: |
      **(b)** What would happen to the supply of electric cars if there were an improvement in the technology used to produce them? Show the change on the graph. Does supply increase, decrease or stay the same? Is this a change in the price of the product or in a non-price determinant, and which variable caused it?
    solution: |
      With better technology, producers can offer **more units at any given price**, so the supply curve **shifts to the right** (S0 to S1): supply **increases**. This is a **non-price determinant** change, and the variable that caused it is **technology**.

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps04-q1b-electric-cars-shift-right.png)

      *Figure: a theory diagram. At the same price P0, the new curve S1 lies to the right of S0.*
    related_terms: ["change-in-supply", "determinants-of-supply", "supply-curve"]
    source_doc_page: 2
  - id: "1c"
    text: |
      **(c)** What would happen to the supply of personal computers if there were a significant increase in the price of computer chips (which are necessary to build PCs)? Show the change on the graph. Does supply increase, decrease or stay the same? Is this a change in the price of the product or in a non-price determinant, and which variable caused it?
    solution: |
      A higher chip price raises the cost of producing **every** unit. To sell the same quantity q0, the producer needs a **higher market price**. Equivalently, at the same market price he would produce **fewer** units. So the supply curve **shifts to the left** (S0 to S1): supply **decreases**. This is a **non-price determinant** change, caused by the **price of an intermediate good** (the chips, an input).

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps04-q1c-computers-shift-left.png)

      *Figure: a theory diagram. At the same price P0 the new curve S1 lies to the left of S0.*
    related_terms: ["change-in-supply", "determinants-of-supply", "supply-curve"]
    source_doc_page: 3
  - id: "1d"
    text: |
      **(d)** What would happen to the supply of carrot soup if there were a shortage of carrots in the market? Show the change on the graph. Does supply increase, decrease or stay the same? Is this a change in the price of the product or in a non-price determinant, and which variable caused it?
    solution: |
      A shortage of carrots means the **price of carrots rises**, so the cost of producing every unit of soup rises. As in 1c, the producer needs a higher market price to sell q0, or at the same price he produces fewer units. The supply curve **shifts to the left** (S0 to S1): supply **decreases**. This is a **non-price determinant** change, caused by the **price of an intermediate good** (carrots).

      ![](/images/principles-of-micro/ps04-q1d-carrot-soup-shift-left.png)

      *Figure: a theory diagram, the same picture as 1c with a different input.*

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > 1c and 1d are the same story. The chain in 1d has one extra link: scarce carrots, so dearer carrots, so dearer soup to make, so a left shift.
    related_terms: ["change-in-supply", "determinants-of-supply", "supply-curve"]

  - id: "2a"
    text: |
      **Multiple choice 1.** When quantity supplied decreases at every possible price, we know that the supply curve has

      - a. shifted to the left.
      - b. shifted to the right.
      - c. not shifted; rather, we have moved along the supply curve to a new point on the same curve.
      - d. not shifted; rather, the supply curve has become flatter.
    solution: |
      **a.** Shifted to the left.

      A fall in quantity supplied *at every price* means that for each price the curve now sits at a smaller quantity, which is a leftward shift. A movement along the curve (option c) changes the quantity only because the price changed, and it never changes the quantity at a given price.

      > [!note] Source marking
      > The solution set marks answer a with a highlight. Its own numbering calls this question 5.
    related_terms: ["change-in-supply", "change-in-quantity-supplied", "supply-curve"]
    source_doc_page: 5
  - id: "2b"
    text: |
      **Multiple choice 2.** Matthew bakes apple pies that he sells at the local farmer's market. If the price of apples increases, the

      - a. supply curve for Matthew's pies will increase.
      - b. supply curve for Matthew's pies will decrease.
      - c. demand curve for Matthew's pies will increase.
      - d. demand curve for Matthew's pies will decrease.
    solution: |
      **b.** The supply curve for Matthew's pies will decrease.

      Apples are an input (an intermediate good), so dearer apples raise the cost of every pie and shift supply left, exactly as in 1c and 1d. Demand is unaffected, which rules out c and d.

      > [!note] Source marking
      > The solution set marks answer b with a highlight. Its own numbering calls this question 6.

      **Market equilibrium (pizza).** Adapted from Problem 8, Chapter 4, of the textbook. The market for pizza has these demand and supply schedules (prices in USD per pizza):

      | Price (USD) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |

      | 6 | 80 | 80 |

      **(a)** Graph the demand and supply curves in the same diagram.
    solution: |
      Plot each schedule with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Demand slopes down (130 at a price of 4 falling to 30 at a price of 9) and supply slopes up (30 at a price of 4 rising to 120 at a price of 9). They cross at a price of 6.

      > Read both columns at a price of 5. Demand is 100 and supply is 50, so quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.

      > $$
      > \text{shortage} = Q_D - Q_S = 100 - 50 = 50
      > $$
      >
      > The price of 5 is below the equilibrium price of 6, which is why there is a shortage rather than a surplus. See [[Shortage]].

**What the set is about.** A change in the price of the good itself moves you *along* the supply curve ([[Change in Quantity Supplied]]). A change in anything else (technology, input prices, expectations) *shifts* the whole curve ([[Change in Supply]]). The last question reads equilibrium and a shortage off a demand and supply schedule.

| Part | Tool | One-line answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Movement along S | Price fall lowers quantity supplied; no shift |
| 1b | Shift right | Better technology increases supply |
| 1c | Shift left | Dearer chips (intermediate good) decrease supply |
| 1d | Shift left | Carrot shortage raises carrot price, decreases supply |
| 2a | Shift vs movement | Less supplied at every price is a left shift (a) |
| 2b | Input price | Dearer apples shift pie supply left (b) |
| 3b | Schedule | $P_E = 6$, $Q_E = 80$ |
| 3c | Schedule | At price 5: $Q_D = 100$, $Q_S = 50$, shortage of 50 |
