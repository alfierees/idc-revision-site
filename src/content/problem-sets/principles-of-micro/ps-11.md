    text: |
      What happened when the price of soda increased in Philadelphia according to the Time magazine article? By how much did sales change? Why did the authors argue that the drop in sales is lower?
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer
      > The tax was an excise tax of 1.5 cents per ounce on sugar- and artificially sweetened drinks, starting January 2017. In the year after, sweetened drink purchases in Philadelphia dropped by just over 50%. But sales **rose in bordering zip codes**: some shoppers left the city to buy soda. Allowing for that, the researchers estimated the total fall in sales at about **38%** (almost a billion fewer ounces sold). In Baltimore (no tax) sales also fell slightly, in line with the general decline in US soda drinking, but far less: about 13.3 million ounces.
      >
      > The authors argued the true drop is **lower than 50%** because part of the "lost" sales just moved across the city line.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > The nearby untaxed market is a **substitute**: it makes demand for taxed Philadelphia soda more elastic (see [[Lec_08-Elasticity]]), so the city's recorded drop overstates the fall in consumption.

> [!tip] The five-step tax recipe
> 1. A **per-unit tax T on sellers** shifts the **supply** curve **up** by T: new supply $P = (\text{old intercept} + T) + \text{slope} \cdot Q$. A **tax on buyers** shifts **demand down** by T. Which side is legally taxed does not change the result (see [[Lec_11-Taxes]]).
> 2. Solve demand = new supply for the new quantity $Q_T$.
> 3. The **buyers' price** $P_B$ comes from the demand curve at $Q_T$; the **sellers' price** $P_S$ from the original supply curve at $Q_T$.
> 4. Check: $P_B - P_S = T$ (the tax wedge).
> 5. Incidence: buyers bear $P_B - P_0$, sellers bear $P_0 - P_S$. They add up to T.
>
> A **subsidy** is a negative tax: supply shifts **down** (or demand up) and the wedge flips sign.
