      **Part B, Q1. Where scarcity comes from.** The phenomenon of scarcity stems from the fact that

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|
      | a | Most economies' production methods are not very good. |

      | c | Governments restrict production of too many goods and services. |
      | d | Resources are limited. |

      **Part B, Q2. "No such thing as a free lunch".** The adage "There is no such thing as a free lunch" means

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|
      | a | Even people on welfare have to pay for food. |
      | b | The cost of living is always increasing. |
      | c | All costs are included in the price of a product. |

      > Even a lunch with a zero price costs something: the time and the next-best use of that time (the [[Opportunity Cost]]). Cost is more than the money price, which is why c is wrong too.

      **Part B, Q3. Which statement shows opportunity cost.** Which of the following statements best represents the principle of opportunity cost?

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|

      **Part B, Q5. Redistribution.** If government taxes the wealthy at a higher rate than the poor, and develops programs to redistribute the tax revenue from the wealthy to the poor, this redistribution of wealth is

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|
      | a | Equal but not necessarily efficient. |
      | b | Efficient but not more equal. |
      | c | Not efficient and not equal. |
      | d | None of the above. |
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer: c (as highlighted in the solution)
      > The solution adds a note: you might be tempted by (a) because the purpose is a more equal income distribution. In practice redistribution does make the distribution **more equal than before**, but society **never reaches an equal society**. So "equal" is not a fair description, which the solution uses to rule out (a).

      > [!warning] Awkward key
      > The highlighted answer c says redistribution is "not efficient". The solution's own note, though, only argues about why "equal" fails; it does not explain the "not efficient" half.       **Part B, Q6. High-school athletes who skip college.** High-school athletes who skip college to become professional athletes

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|
      | a | Obviously do not understand the value of a college education. |
      | b | Usually do so because they cannot get into college. |
      | c | Understand that the opportunity cost of attending college is very high. |
      | d | Are not making a rational decision since the marginal benefits of college outweigh the marginal costs of college for high-school athletes. |
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer: c
      > For a star athlete the best forgone alternative to college is a professional career, so the [[Opportunity Cost]] of college is very high. A [[Rational Decision Maker]] compares this cost with the benefit, so skipping college can be rational, which rules out a and d.

    text: |
      **Part B, Q7. Arguing for trade.** Senator Bright, who understands economic principles, is trying to convince workers in her district that trade with other countries is beneficial. Senator Bright should argue that trade can be beneficial

      | Option | Statement |
      |---|---|
      | a | only if it allows us to obtain things that we couldn't make for ourselves. |
      | b | because it allows specialization, which increases total output. |
      | c | to us if we can gain and the others involved in the trade lose. |
      | d | in only a limited number of circumstances because others are typically self-interested. |
    solution: |
      > [!success] Answer: b
      > [[Gains from Trade]]: each party specialises in what it produces at lower opportunity cost, which raises total output so both sides can be better off. Trade is not a zero-sum game (rules out c) and is useful even for goods we could make ourselves (rules out a).
