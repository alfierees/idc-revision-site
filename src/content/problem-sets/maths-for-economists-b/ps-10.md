    text: |
      **Min and max on an ellipse.** Use Lagrange multipliers to find the minimal and maximal value of $f(x,y)=4x^3+y^2$ under $2x^2+y^2=1$.
    solution: |
      > [!info] Working written for this site; the final answer is from the official sheet.

      $$
      \mathcal L=4x^3+y^2-\lambda\,(2x^2+y^2-1)
      $$

      $$
      \mathcal L_x:\;12x^2=4\lambda x\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=0\ \text{or}\ \lambda=3x
      $$

      $$
      \mathcal L_y:\;2y=2\lambda y\quad\Rightarrow\quad y=0\ \text{or}\ \lambda=1
      $$

      Check the cases against the constraint:

      | Case | Points | $f$ |
      | --- | --- | --- |
      | $y=0$ | $x=\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ | $\pm\sqrt2$ |
      | $x=0$ | $y=\pm1$ | $1$ |
      | $\lambda=1$, $x=\tfrac13$ | $y=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt7}{3}$ | $\tfrac{25}{27}$ |

      ![](/images/maths-for-economists-b/ps10-q1-ellipse-candidates.png)

      *All six critical points on the ellipse with their values; the extremes sit at the ends of the $x$-axis.*

      > [!success] Answer
      > Maximum $\sqrt2$ and minimum $-\sqrt2$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > The ellipse is closed and bounded, so extremes exist and are the largest and smallest of the candidate values.

      **A law firm's efficiency.** A firm employs $x$ interns and $y$ lawyers, with daily efficiency $f(x,y)=x^{\alpha}y^{1-\alpha}$.

      When $x=y$, the marginal efficiency of one more intern is twice that of one more lawyer. Find $\alpha$ and plug it in.
    solution: |
      > [!info] Working written for this site; the final answer is from the official sheet.

      $$
      f_x=\alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha},\qquad f_y=(1-\alpha)x^{\alpha}y^{-\alpha}
      $$

      At $x=y$ both reduce to $f_x=\alpha$ and $f_y=1-\alpha$. The condition $f_x=2f_y$ gives $\alpha=2(1-\alpha)$, so $\alpha=\tfrac23$.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $\alpha=\tfrac23$, so $f(x,y)=x^{2/3}y^{1/3}$.

  - id: "2b"
    text: |
      Each intern costs 600 dollars a day, each lawyer 1,200, and the budget is 72,000 per day. With $f(x,y)=x^{2/3}y^{1/3}$ from part (a), find the maximal efficiency under the budget, i.e. how many interns and lawyers to employ.
    solution: |
      > [!info] Working written for this site; the final answer is from the official sheet.

      Constraint: $600x+1200y=72000$, i.e. $x+2y=120$.

      $$
      \mathcal L=x^{2/3}y^{1/3}-\lambda\,(600x+1200y-72000)
      $$

      $$
      \tfrac23x^{-1/3}y^{1/3}=600\lambda,\qquad \tfrac13x^{2/3}y^{-2/3}=1200\lambda
      $$

      Dividing the first by the second: $\dfrac{2y}{x}=\dfrac12$, so $x=4y$. Then $4y+2y=120$ gives $y=20$, $x=80$.

      ![](/images/maths-for-economists-b/ps10-q2b-budget-tangency.png)

      *The highest efficiency contour that still touches the budget line does so at (80, 20).*

      > [!success] Answer
      > 80 interns and 20 lawyers.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > For Cobb-Douglas $x^{\alpha}y^{1-\alpha}$ the optimal spending share on each input equals its exponent: $\tfrac23$ of the 72,000 on interns is 48,000, which is $80\times600$.

    text: |
      **Gauss-Jordan elimination.** Write the system as an augmented matrix and solve it by Gauss-Jordan elimination.

      $$
      \begin{cases} x-2y-3z=-1\\ 2x-y-2z=2\\ 3x-y-3z=3 \end{cases}
      $$
    solution: |
      > [!info] Working written for this site; the final answer is from the official sheet.

      $$
      \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&-2&-3&-1\\2&-1&-2&2\\3&-1&-3&3\end{array}\right]
      $$

      Eliminate below the first pivot ($R_2-2R_1$, $R_3-3R_1$):

      $$
      \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&-2&-3&-1\\0&3&4&4\\0&5&6&6\end{array}\right]
      $$

      Scale $R_2$ by $\tfrac13$, then $R_3-5R_2$:

      $$
      \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&-2&-3&-1\\0&1&\tfrac43&\tfrac43\\0&0&-\tfrac23&-\tfrac23\end{array}\right]
      $$

      Scale $R_3$ by $-\tfrac32$, then clear above: $R_2-\tfrac43R_3$, $R_1+3R_3$, $R_1+2R_2$:

      $$
      \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&0&0&2\\0&1&0&0\\0&0&1&1\end{array}\right]
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $(x,y,z)=(2,\,0,\,1)$
