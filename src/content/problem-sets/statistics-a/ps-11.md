    text: |
      **Ranking five students.** Scores in a national mathematics exam have an expected value of 70 and a standard deviation of 5.

      - Tamar got 60 on the exam.
      - Micah got 80 on the exam.
      - Mira's standardized score on the exam was 1.3.
      - Gali's score was one standard deviation higher than Mira's.
      - Ron's standardized score was 0.

      Rank the five students from the lowest to the highest scoring.
    solution: |
      Let $X$ be the exam score, with $\mu=70$ and $\sigma=5$. The ranking only needs everyone on **one scale**: either all raw scores $X$ or all standardized scores $Z$. Convert each student in the direction the information is given.

      $$
      Z=\frac{X-70}{5} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X=5Z+70
      $$

      | Student | Given | $X$ | $Z$ |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Tamar | $X=60$ | $60$ | $\frac{60-70}{5}=-2$ |
      | Micah | $X=80$ | $80$ | $\frac{80-70}{5}=2$ |
      | Mira | $Z=1.3$ | $5\cdot 1.3+70=76.5$ | $1.3$ |
      | Gali | one SD above Mira | $5\cdot 2.3+70=81.5$ | $1.3+1=2.3$ |
      | Ron | $Z=0$ | $5\cdot 0+70=70$ | $0$ |

      > [!example] Gali, step by step
      > "One standard deviation higher" is a statement on the standardized scale: $Z_{\text{Gali}}=1.3+1=2.3$. In raw points that is $5$ points more than Mira, so $76.5+5=81.5$. Both routes agree.

      Ordering by $X$ ($60<70<76.5<80<81.5$) or by $Z$ ($-2<0<1.3<2<2.3$) gives the same ranking.

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps11-q1-ranking-on-normal-curve.png)

      *All five students placed on the same $N(70,5^2)$ curve. Moving right is moving up in both $X$ and $Z$.*

      > [!success] Answer
      > Tamar $<$ Ron $<$ Mira $<$ Micah $<$ Gali

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > $Z$ simply counts "how many standard deviations from the mean". Standardizing is monotonic (a larger $X$ always gives a larger $Z$), so it never changes a ranking, it only puts differently-scaled scores on a common footing.

      **Heights in a college.** In a given college, students' height is distributed normally, with an expected value of 175 cm and a standard deviation of 15.

      What percent of students in this college are taller than 195 cm?
    solution: |
      Let $X$ be the height of a random student: $X\sim N(175,15^2)$.

      $$
      P(X>195)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{195-175}{15}\right)=1-\Phi(1.33)=1-0.9082=0.0918
      $$

      > [!example] Steps
      > 1. Standardize: $\frac{195-175}{15}=\frac{20}{15}\approx 1.33$.
      > 2. Table: $\Phi(1.33)=0.9082$ is the area **below** 195.
      > 3. The question asks for the area **above**, so subtract from 1.

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps11-q2-height-tails.png)

      *Both right tails of $N(175,15^2)$. The left panel is this part; the right panel gives the 15.87% used to scale 300 students up to the whole college in the next part.*

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(X>195)=0.0918$, about $9.18\%$

    text: |
      In a given college, students' height is distributed normally, with an expected value of 175 cm and a standard deviation of 15.

      How many students are in this college given that 300 students are taller than 190 cm?
    solution: |
      $$
      P(X>190)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{190-175}{15}\right)=1-\Phi(1)=1-0.8413=0.1587
      $$

      The 300 students taller than 190 cm are $15.87\%$ of the college. Let $S$ be the number of students in the college:

      $$
      \frac{300}{S}=0.1587
      $$

      $$
      S=\frac{300}{0.1587}=1890.359\ldots\approx 1890
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(X>190)=0.1587$, so about $1890$ students

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > This is a proportion problem hidden inside a normal problem: the normal curve tells you what **fraction** is above 190, and the head count tells you how big the whole must be for that fraction to equal 300.

      **IQ cutoffs.** IQ scores are distributed normally with an expected value of 100 and a standard deviation of 15.

      What score are 75% of IQ scores lower than?
    solution: |
      Let $X$ be a random person's IQ: $X\sim N(100,15^2)$. Each part asks for a cutoff $C_a$ with a given area below it, so we solve

      $$
      \frac{C_a-100}{15}=Z_a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_a=15\,Z_a+100
      $$

      $$
      \frac{C_{0.75}-100}{15}=Z_{0.75}=0.67 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_{0.75}=15\cdot 0.67+100=110.05
      $$

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps11-q3-iq-quantiles.png)

      *Three different cutoffs, shaded by the area given in the question. In every panel the cutoff is the value that leaves exactly that shaded area.*

    text: |
      **Recovering the mean from two percentiles.** A test's scores are distributed normally. 99% of people score less than 95, and 12% of people score more than 82. What is the expected value of scores in this test?
    solution: |
      Let $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with **both** $\mu$ and $\sigma$ unknown. Each percentile gives one equation. In quantile notation:

      $$
      C_{0.99}=95, \qquad C_{0.88}=82
      $$

      The second one is because $12\%$ above $82$ means $88\%$ below $82$. Standardizing each cutoff:

      $$
      \frac{95-\mu}{\sigma}=Z_{0.99}=2.33, \qquad \frac{82-\mu}{\sigma}=Z_{0.88}=1.17
      $$

      Rearranging each to isolate $\mu$:

      $$
      \text{I:}\quad \mu=95-2.33\,\sigma
      $$

      $$
      \text{II:}\quad \mu=82-1.17\,\sigma
      $$

      Setting the right-hand sides equal eliminates $\mu$:

      $$
      95-2.33\,\sigma=82-1.17\,\sigma
      $$

      $$
      \sigma=11.2069
      $$

      Substituting back into equation II:

      $$
      \mu=82-1.17\cdot 11.2069=68.888
      $$

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps11-q4-two-cutoffs.png)

      *Two tail areas (12% above 82, 1% above 95) fix where the curve sits and how wide it is.*

      > [!success] Answer
      > $\mu\approx 68.888$ (with $\sigma\approx 11.2069$)

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > One percentile is not enough: a normal has two unknowns, so you need two pieces of information. Each cutoff becomes a line $\mu=\text{cutoff}-Z\sigma$, and the answer is where the two lines meet.

      **Appliance lifespans.** The lifespan of a certain appliance is distributed normally with an expected value of 10 years and a standard deviation of 2.

      The producer has to replace any appliances that stop working during the warranty period. If the producer wants to replace only 3% of appliances, how long should the warranty period be?

      $$

    text: |
      The lifespan of a certain appliance is distributed normally with an expected value of 10 years and a standard deviation of 2. A company bought 7 of these appliances. Assume each appliance's lifespan is independent.

      What is the probability exactly 4 of the 7 will last more than 14 years?
    solution: |
      Let $Y$ be the number of appliances out of the 7 that last more than 14 years. Each appliance independently has success probability $p=0.0228$ (from the previous part), so $Y\sim B(7,0.0228)$:

      $$
      P(Y=4)=\binom{7}{4}\,0.0228^{4}\,0.9772^{3}=8.83\cdot 10^{-6}
      $$

      > [!example] Pieces
      > $\binom{7}{4}=35$, $\;0.0228^4\approx 2.70\cdot 10^{-7}$, $\;0.9772^3\approx 0.933$, and $35\cdot 2.70\cdot 10^{-7}\cdot 0.933\approx 8.83\cdot 10^{-6}$.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(Y=4)=8.83\cdot 10^{-6}$

      **Qualifying accounting exam.** Scores on a qualifying accounting exam are distributed normally with an expected value of 80 and a standard deviation of 24. To pass the exam, one has to score at least 60.

      What percent of people who take the test pass?
    solution: |
      Let $X$ be a random person's score: $X\sim N(80,24^2)$.

      $$
      P(X\ge 60)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{60-80}{24}\right)=1-\Phi(-0.83)=1-\big(1-\Phi(0.83)\big)=\Phi(0.83)=0.7967
      $$

      > [!example] Why the sign flips
      > The table is printed for positive $Z$, so use symmetry $\Phi(-0.83)=1-\Phi(0.83)$. The two minus signs cancel and the pass probability is simply $\Phi(0.83)$.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(X\ge 60)=0.7967$, about $79.67\%$
