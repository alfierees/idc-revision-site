    text: |
      **Test grades, sum and average of 40 takers.** Grades on a test have an expected value of 80 and a standard deviation of 7. Consider a sample of 40 test takers chosen randomly.

      What is the probability the sum of their grades will be greater than 3500?
    solution: |
      Let $X_i$ be the score of test taker $i$. Given:

      $$
      E(X_i)=\mu=80,\qquad Var(X_i)=\sigma^{2}=7^{2}=49
      $$

      Let $S=\sum_{i=1}^{40}X_i$. Expected value and variance of the sum:

      $$
      E(S)=n\mu=40\cdot80=3200
      $$

      $$
      Var(S)=n\sigma^{2}=40\cdot7^{2}=\left(\sqrt{40}\cdot7\right)^{2}=44.272^{2}
      $$

      Since $n=40\ge30$, the [[Central Limit Theorem]] lets us treat the sum as approximately normal:

      $$
      S\sim N\!\left(3200,\;44.272^{2}\right)
      $$

      Standardize:

      $$
      P(S>3500)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{3500-3200}{44.272}\right)=1-\Phi(6.776)\approx1-1=0
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(S>3500)\approx0$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > 3500 is more than 6.7 standard deviations above the mean sum of 3200, which is far beyond the end of the Z table. An average of $3500/40=87.5$ across 40 people is essentially impossible.

      Grades on a test have an expected value of 80 and a standard deviation of 7. Consider a sample of 40 test takers chosen randomly.

      What is the probability their average grade will be less than 79?
    solution: |
      With $\mu=80$ and $\sigma^{2}=49$ for a single grade:

      $$
      E(\bar{X})=\mu=80
      $$

      $$
      Var(\bar{X})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}=\frac{49}{40}=1.107^{2}
      $$

      Again $n\ge30$, so $\bar{X}$ is approximately normal:

      $$
      \bar{X}\sim N\!\left(80,\;1.107^{2}\right)
      $$

      $$
      P(\bar{X}<79)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{79-80}{1.107}\right)=\Phi(-0.9)=1-\Phi(0.9)=0.1841
      $$

      > [!warning] Rounding
      > The exact $z$ is $-1/1.107=-0.903$, which the solution rounds to $-0.9$ before reading the table. Using the unrounded value would give about $0.183$. Use the table value 0.1841, as the solution does.

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps12-q1-average-below-79.png)

      > Theory diagram. The sample average of 40 grades is centred on 80 with standard deviation 1.107; the shaded left tail below 79 is the answer.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(\bar{X}<79)=0.1841$.

      **A discrete cohort, then samples of 50 and of 2.** A large cohort took a test. 16% scored 90, 33% scored 80 and 51% scored 70.

      What are the expected value and variance of scores on this test?
    solution: |
      Let $X$ be the score of a random test taker.

      | $x_i$ | 70 | 80 | 90 |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | $P(X=x_i)$ | 0.51 | 0.33 | 0.16 |

      $$
      E(X)=70\cdot0.51+80\cdot0.33+90\cdot0.16=76.5
      $$

      Variance by the shortcut $Var(X)=E(X^{2})-\left(E(X)\right)^{2}$:

      $$
      E(X^{2})=70^{2}\cdot0.51+80^{2}\cdot0.33+90^{2}\cdot0.16=2499+2112+1296=5907
      $$

      $$
      Var(X)=5907-76.5^{2}=5907-5852.25=54.75
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $E(X)=76.5$ and $Var(X)=54.75$.

      A large cohort took a test. 16% scored 90, 33% scored 80 and 51% scored 70.

      Of this cohort, we randomly choose a sample of 50 people. What is the probability that the average score in the sample will be between 74 and 79?
    solution: |
      Let $X_i$ be the score of the $i$-th person sampled, so $E(X_i)=76.5$ and $Var(X_i)=54.75$.

      $$
      E(\bar{X})=76.5,\qquad Var(\bar{X})=\frac{54.75}{50}=1.095=1.046^{2}
      $$

      Since $n=50\ge30$, $\bar{X}$ is approximately normal even though a single score takes only three values:

      $$
      \bar{X}\sim N\!\left(76.5,\;1.046^{2}\right)
      $$

      $$
      P(74<\bar{X}<79)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{79-76.5}{1.046}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{74-76.5}{1.046}\right)=\Phi(2.39)-\Phi(-2.39)
      $$

      $$
      =0.9916-0.0084=0.9832
      $$

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps12-q2-score-distribution-and-average.png)

      > Theory diagram. Left: the three-point distribution of one score. Right: the average of 50 scores is approximately normal around 76.5 with standard deviation 1.046, and the shaded region between 74 and 79 holds about 98% of it.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(74<\bar{X}<79)=0.9832$.

    text: |
      A large cohort took a test. 16% scored 90, 33% scored 80 and 51% scored 70.

      Of this cohort, we randomly choose 2 people. What is the probability that the average of their scores will be between 74 and 79?
    solution: |
      With $n=2$ the CLT does **not** apply, so list the possible pairs instead. The average of two scores from $\{70,80,90\}$ is:

      | Pair | Average |
      |---|---|
      | 70 and 70 | 70 |
      | 70 and 80 | 75 |
      | 70 and 90 | 80 |
      | 80 and 80 | 80 |
      | 80 and 90 | 85 |
      | 90 and 90 | 90 |

      Only $\{70,80\}$ gives an average between 74 and 79 (it is exactly 75). The two people can be chosen in either order, and the draws are independent (the cohort is large):

      $$
      P(74<\bar{X}<79)=P(X_1=70\cap X_2=80)+P(X_1=80\cap X_2=70)
      $$

      $$
      =0.51\cdot0.33+0.33\cdot0.51=0.3366
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(74<\bar{X}<79)=0.3366$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > Compare parts (b) and (c): the same event has probability 0.98 for $n=50$ but only 0.34 for $n=2$. A bigger sample pulls the average tightly towards 76.5, which is exactly what the [[Law of Large Numbers]] says.

      **Proportion of successful procedures.** The probability a medical procedure will succeed is 0.9. What is the probability that in a sample of 100 people who undergo the procedure, the proportion of successful procedures will be between 0.84 and 0.95?
    solution: |
      Let $\hat{P}$ be the sample proportion of successes, with $p=0.9$ and $n=100$.

      $$
      E(\hat{P})=p=0.9
      $$

      $$
      Var(\hat{P})=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}=\frac{0.9\cdot0.1}{100}=0.009=0.03^{2}
      $$

      Check the conditions for the normal approximation:

      $$
      np=100\cdot0.9=90\ge5,\qquad n(1-p)=100\cdot0.1=10\ge5
      $$

      Both hold, so $\hat{P}\sim N\!\left(0.9,\;0.03^{2}\right)$ approximately.

      $$
      P(0.84\le\hat{P}\le0.95)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{0.95-0.9}{0.03}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{0.84-0.9}{0.03}\right)=\Phi(1.67)-\Phi(-2)
      $$

      $$
      =0.9525-0.0228=0.9297
      $$

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(0.84\le\hat{P}\le0.95)=0.9297$.

      **Accepting or rejecting a drug claim.** A drug company created a new medication which they claim cures condition X 80% of the time. To test their claim, a hospital decides to give the new medication to a randomly chosen sample of 100 people with condition X. If at least 75 of the people in the sample are cured, the hospital will accept the company's claim and start using the new medication.

      What is the probability that the hospital will reject the claim if the claim is in fact true (the medication cures the condition 80% of the time)?
    solution: |
      Let $p$ be the chance the medication works for one person and $X$ the number cured in the sample, so $X\sim B(100,p)$. The hospital **accepts** when $X$ is large and **rejects** when it is small. The two parts compute the probability of a wrong decision under two different values of $p$.

      Here $p=0.8$. The hospital rejects when $X<75$, so we need $P(X<75)$ for $X\sim B(100,0.8)$.

      $$
      E(X)=np=100\cdot0.8=80
      $$

      $$
      Var(X)=np(1-p)=100\cdot0.8\cdot0.2=16=4^{2}
      $$

      Conditions: $np=80\ge5$ and $n(1-p)=20\ge5$, so use the [[Normal Approximation of the Binomial]]: $X\sim N\!\left(80,\;4^{2}\right)$.

      $$
      P(X<75)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{75-80}{4}\right)=\Phi(-1.25)=0.1056
      $$

      ![](/images/statistics-a/ps12-q4-accept-reject-regions.png)

      > Theory diagram. The same cutoff of 75 cured patients cuts both curves. Blue: if the claim is true, the left tail (0.1056) is a wrong rejection. Red: if only 70% are cured, the right tail (0.1379) is a wrong acceptance.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(\text{reject}\mid p=0.8)=0.1056$.

    text: |
      A drug company created a new medication which they claim cures condition X 80% of the time. To test their claim, a hospital decides to give the new medication to a randomly chosen sample of 100 people with condition X. If at least 75 of the people in the sample are cured, the hospital will accept the company's claim and start using the new medication.

      What is the probability that the hospital will accept the claim if the claim is false and the medication instead works in just 70% of cases?
    solution: |
      Now $X\sim B(100,0.7)$ and the solution computes $P(X>75)$.

      $$
      E(X)=100\cdot0.7=70
      $$

      $$
      Var(X)=100\cdot0.7\cdot0.3=21=4.583^{2}
      $$

      Conditions: $np=70\ge5$ and $n(1-p)=30\ge5$. So $X\sim N\!\left(70,\;4.583^{2}\right)$.

      $$
      P(X>75)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{75-70}{4.583}\right)=1-\Phi(1.09)=1-0.8621=0.1379
      $$

      > [!warning] "At least 75" versus "more than 75"
      > The hospital accepts on "at least 75", which is $X\ge75$, while the solution writes $P(X>75)$. With the plain normal approximation (no continuity correction, the lecture's method) the two are the same number, so the answer 0.1379 stands. The wording only matters for an exact binomial calculation.

      > [!success] Answer
      > $P(\text{accept}\mid p=0.7)=0.1379$.

      > [!tip] Intuition
      > This is the logic of hypothesis testing in miniature: a cutoff trades one kind of error against the other. Moving the cutoff up makes false acceptance rarer but wrong rejection more common.

      Dori is flipping a fair coin. For each of the following, decide (without computations) whether it is more likely if he flips the coin 10 times or 100 times:

      The number of heads is equal to exactly 50%.
    solution: |
      There are fewer possible outcomes in the smaller sample, so the probability is spread over fewer values and exactly 5 heads out of 10 is more likely than exactly 50 heads out of 100.

      > In (a) the target is a **range** of proportions, which gets easier to hit as $n$ grows.       **Two hospitals.** In a city, there are two hospitals. In the larger hospital there are 45 births each day. In the smaller hospital there are 15 births each day. In general 50% of babies are born male and 50% are female, but some days the percent of male babies is higher than 50%.       > [!success] Answer
      > The **smaller hospital** (15 births per day).

      > Daily births are a small "sample" from the 50/50 process. Small samples are noisy; large ones average the noise out. The figure under Question 5(a) shows the same picture.
