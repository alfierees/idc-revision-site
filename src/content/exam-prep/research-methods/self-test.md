    text: |
      Two students argue about how to tell whether an article is an academic text. Which list gives the course's five criteria?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Author, number of citations, language, structure, research design
        why: |
          The number of citations isn't a criterion, and "research design" isn't on the list.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Journal ranking, language, author, references, quantitative findings
        why: |
          Journal ranking and quantitative findings sound academic but aren't the course's criteria. A qualitative study can be fully academic.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Author, language, references, content, structure
        correct: true
        why: |
          Exactly the five: an academic **author**, formal **language**, credible **references**, **content** based on research, academic **structure**.
      - label: d
        text: |
          Content, structure, the ability to generalise, author, language
        why: |
          The ability to generalise depends on the sample, not on whether the text is academic.
    solution: |
      Remember **A-L-R-C-S**: Author, Language, References, Content, Structure ([[Lec_01-Academic Texts and the Research Process|Lec 01]]). The 2023 sample exam's Q19 asks exactly this.
  - id: "2"
    title: "2 · Citing a secondary source"
    text: |
      You read Cohen's 2020 book, which describes a study by Levy from 2000. You did not read Levy's study. How do you cite it in APA?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          (Levy, 2000 in Cohen, 2020), with Cohen in the reference list
        correct: true
        why: |
          The original goes first, the source you read in parentheses after "in", and only the source you read goes in the list.
      - label: b
        text: |
          (Cohen, 2020) only, as if the idea were Cohen's
        why: |
          That credits Cohen with Levy's study.
      - label: c
        text: |
          (Levy, 2000), with Levy in the reference list
        why: |
          You didn't read Levy, so you can't list it as your source.
      - label: d
        text: |
          A footnote naming both works
        why: |
          APA does not use footnotes for citing.
    solution: |
      Secondary source: `(Levy, 2000 in Cohen, 2020)`, and the secondary source (Cohen) goes in the bibliography ([[Lec_02-APA Citing and Referencing|Lec 02]]).
  - id: "3"
    title: "3 · Three authors in the text"
    text: |
      A 2010 article was written by Smith, Jones and Lee. How do you cite it in the text?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          (Smith, Jones & Lee, 2010)
        why: |
          Listing every name is for one or two authors only.
      - label: b
        text: |
          (Smith & et al., 2010)
        why: |
          There's no "&" before et al.
      - label: c
        text: |
          (Smith, 2010)
        why: |
          That hides the co-authors.
      - label: d
        text: |
          (Smith et al., 2010)
        correct: true
        why: |
          Three or more authors: the first author's surname plus "et al.", from the first citation.
    solution: |
      One author: `(Cohen, 1988)`. Two: both names every time, `(Bergman & Rappaport, 2021)`. Three or more: `(Smith et al., 2010)`.
  - id: "4"
    title: "4 · Order a reference list"
    text: |
      Put these five entries in APA order:

      - Levy, B., & Tamir, H. (1967)
      - Cohen, H. (1984)
      - Levy, B. (1988)
      - Cohen, B. (1989)
      - Levy, B., & Cohen, A. (1983)
    solution: |
      1. Cohen, B. (1989)
      2. Cohen, H. (1984)
      3. Levy, B. (1988)
      4. Levy, B., & Cohen, A. (1983)
      5. Levy, B., & Tamir, H. (1967)

      **Why:** surname first (Cohen before Levy); for the two different Cohens, the **first initial** decides (B before H), not the year; Levy alone comes **before** Levy with co-authors; among Levy's co-authored works the **second author's** surname decides (Cohen before Tamir). The year only matters when the authors are the same.

    title: "5 · DOI or URL?"
    text: |
      An online journal article has both a DOI and a URL. What goes at the end of its reference?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Both the DOI and the URL
        why: |
          With a DOI, the URL isn't needed.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Only the DOI, formatted like a URL
        correct: true
        why: |
          A DOI is persistent; if a work has one, use only the DOI, with no "DOI:" label.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Only the URL, because it's an online source
        why: |
          The URL is used only when there is no DOI.
      - label: d
        text: |
          "DOI: 10.xxxx" followed by the date you retrieved it
        why: |
          The label isn't needed, and a retrieval date is normally not either.
    solution: |
      DOIs start with 10. Include one for every work that has it, print or online; use a URL only when there's no DOI ([[Lec_02-APA Citing and Referencing|Lec 02]]).
  - id: "6"
    title: "6 · The PAIR framework"
    text: |
      Name the four steps of PAIR. In which step do you record your prompts, and which prompts do you record?
    solution: |
      **P**roblem formulation (focus, scope, what you expect of the tool), **A**I tool selection (explore, compare, choose, and justify), **I**nteraction (experiment with inputs and outputs, judge accuracy), **R**eflection (how the tool helped or hindered, how to improve).

      Prompts are documented in **Interaction**, both the **successful and the unsuccessful** ones. That's also where you check for the three failures: **fabricated citations, false statistics, inaccurate content** ([[Lec_03-Generative AI Skills and the PAIR Framework|Lec 03]]).
  - id: "7"
    title: "7 · Question or hypothesis?"
    text: |
      Which of these is a hypothesis?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Founders who have a mentor will raise more seed funding than founders who don't.
        correct: true
        why: |
          A **statement**, made before data, about a relationship and its **direction**, and it can be measured.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Do mentors help founders raise seed funding?
        why: |
          That's the research **question** the hypothesis answers.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Mentoring is an important topic in entrepreneurship.
        why: |
          No variables related, nothing testable.
      - label: d
        text: |
          All three are hypotheses.
        why: |
          Only one is a testable statement of a relationship. "All" isn't automatically right.
    solution: |
      A hypothesis states the **existence, direction or magnitude** of a relationship, before data collection, and needs **operational definitions** (how "mentor" and "seed funding" are measured) ([[Lec_04-Research Problems, Questions and Hypotheses|Lec 04]]).
  - id: "8"
    title: "8 · When can you say 'caused'?"
    text: |
      A report says: "Startups that use our analytics tool grow faster, so the tool causes growth." What three conditions would the company need to meet to claim causation?
    solution: |
      1. **Time order:** the tool was adopted **before** the growth.
      2. **No alternative explanation:** rule out third variables, such as better-funded startups both buying tools and growing faster.
      3. **An experimental design:** for example, randomly give the tool to some startups (experimental group) and not others (control group), then compare growth.

      As written, it's a **correlation** ([[Lec_04-Research Problems, Questions and Hypotheses|Lec 04]], [[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]]).
  - id: "9"
    title: "9 · Name the variables"
    text: |
      A startup tests whether offering a free 14-day trial increases the number of paid sign-ups. What are the variables?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Paid sign-ups: independent; free trial: dependent
        why: |
          Reversed: sign-ups are the outcome.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Both are dependent variables
        why: |
          One of them is what the startup changes.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Free trial: control variable; paid sign-ups: independent
        why: |
          A control variable is held constant. The trial is the thing being varied.
      - label: d
        text: |
          Free trial: independent; paid sign-ups: dependent
        correct: true
        why: |
          The trial is the cause being tested, sign-ups the effect.
    solution: |
      In "X **increases** Y", X is the IV and Y the DV. The 2023 exam's Q1 tests the same skill with crises and venture success.
  - id: "10"
    title: "10 · Mediator or moderator?"
    text: |
      A study finds that years of experience raise salary, but the effect is stronger for men than for women. What is gender in this study?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          A mediator
        why: |
          A mediator is the **process** in between (experience → skills → salary). Gender isn't caused by experience.
      - label: b
        text: |
          A moderator
        correct: true
        why: |
          It changes the **strength** of the experience → salary link. This is the lecture's own example.
      - label: c
        text: |
          A control variable
        why: |
          A control variable is held constant; here gender is the thing that changes the result.
      - label: d
        text: |
          The dependent variable
        why: |
          Salary is the DV.
    solution: |
      **Mediator = how** (caused by the IV, affects the DV: sleep quality → alertness → grades). **Moderator = for whom, how strongly** (changes the strength or direction). Moderators can be categorical (gender, religion) or quantitative (age, income) ([[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]]).

    title: "11 · Ice cream and drowning"
    text: |
      In a coastal city, months with higher ice cream sales also have more drownings. What best explains this?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Ice cream causes drowning
        why: |
          Covarying isn't causing.
      - label: b
        text: |
          The directionality problem: drownings make people buy ice cream
        why: |
          Neither direction is plausible here; something else drives both.
      - label: c
        text: |
          The third variable problem: hot weather raises both
        correct: true
        why: |
          Like the storks and babies (season), a third variable makes two unrelated things move together.
      - label: d
        text: |
          There is no correlation
        why: |
          There is one, and it's positive. It just isn't causal.
    solution: |
      **Third variable:** something else affects both measured variables. **Directionality:** they may be causally linked, but you can't tell which way ([[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]]).
  - id: "12"
    title: "12 · Which way does it run?"
    text: |
      Employees who receive more training have higher performance ratings. Give two directional explanations and one possible third variable.
    solution: |
      - **Training → performance:** the training improves skills, so ratings rise.
      - **Performance → training:** managers send their best performers to more training.
      - **Third variable:** for example, motivation or seniority, which could lead both to more training and to better ratings.

      So the finding is a **positive correlation**; on its own it can't say which explanation is right ([[Lec_09-Course Summary|Lec 09]] has more practice).
  - id: "13"
    title: "13 · Reading a correlation"
    text: |
      A study reports a correlation of r = −0.62 between hours on social media and exam scores, with p = 0.003. Which statement is correct?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          It is a negative, sizeable and significant correlation: more hours tend to go with lower scores
        correct: true
        why: |
          The sign gives the **direction**, the size of the coefficient the **strength**, and p < 0.05 means **significant**.
      - label: b
        text: |
          It is a positive correlation, because 0.62 is large
        why: |
          The minus sign makes it negative.
      - label: c
        text: |
          It isn't significant, because p is below 0.05
        why: |
          Below 0.05 is exactly what significant means.
      - label: d
        text: |
          It proves social media lowers exam scores
        why: |
          A correlation can't prove causation (third variables, direction).
    solution: |
      Three properties of any correlation: **strength** (coefficient), **direction** (sign), **significance** (p-value against 0.05). Strength is how tightly the points cluster around the line, not the slope ([[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]]).

    title: "14 · Three judges agree"
    text: |
      At a pitch competition, three judges score each startup independently and their scores are almost identical. What does this show?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Test-retest reliability
        why: |
          That's the same measure repeated **over time**.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Internal consistency
        why: |
          That's agreement between **items** on one scale.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Construct validity
        why: |
          Validity asks whether the score measures what it should, not whether judges agree.
      - label: d
        text: |
          Inter-rater reliability
        correct: true
        why: |
          Agreement between **observers**, which protects against one person's bias.
    solution: |
      Reliability: test-retest (time), internal consistency (items), inter-rater (observers). Validity: external, content, construct, internal. A reliable measure isn't always valid ([[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]]).

    text: |
      Which of these variables is measured on an interval scale?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Number of children in a family
        why: |
          It has a true zero (no children), so it's ratio.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Temperature in °C
        correct: true
        why: |
          Equal gaps between degrees, but 0 °C isn't "no temperature" and values go below zero.
      - label: c
        text: |
          Satisfaction rated 1 to 5
        why: |
          Ranked without measurable gaps: ordinal (Likert).
      - label: d
        text: |
          Country of birth coded 1, 2, 3
        why: |
          The numbers are labels: nominal.
    solution: |
      Nominal (names) → ordinal (+ order) → interval (+ equal gaps) → ratio (+ absolute zero) ([[Lec_07-Questionnaire Design|Lec 07]]).

      A retail chain lengthens staff break time in all its stores for a month and records how productive staff are in the real stores. What kind of experiment is this?

      - label: a
        text: |
          A field experiment
        correct: true
        why: |

      - label: b
        text: |
          A controlled experiment with experimental and control groups
        why: |

      - label: c
        text: |

  - id: "19"
    title: "19 · Fix the wording"
    text: |
      Say what is wrong with each question and rewrite it.

      1. "How satisfied are you with the price and delivery speed of our service?"
      2. "Since our app is the best on the market, how likely are you to recommend it?"
      3. "Which of our premium features do you use most often?"
    solution: |
      1. **Two questions in one** (double-barrelled). Split it: "How satisfied are you with the price?" and "How satisfied are you with the delivery speed?", each on a 1-5 scale.
      2. **Biased:** it states an opinion as fact and pushes the answer. "How likely are you to recommend our app to a friend?" (1 = not at all likely, 5 = very likely).
      3. **Assumes behaviour:** not every user has premium features. Ask first: "Do you use any premium features? Yes / No", then the follow-up only for those who answer yes.

      Then **pilot** the questionnaire to catch anything else ([[Lec_07-Questionnaire Design|Lec 07]]).

  - id: "22"
    title: "22 · Design the study"
    text: |
      The university wants to know whether its new entrepreneurship course **increases** students' intention to start a business. Outline a study: the type of study, the method, the IV and DV, how you'd measure the DV and on what scale, the sample, and one risk of bias. How could you also learn **why** the course works?
    solution: |
      - **Type:** causal, because the question is whether the course **increases** intention.
      - **Method:** an **experiment**. An experimental group takes the course; a control group, as similar as possible, doesn't. Ideally students are assigned at random. Measure intention **before and after** in both groups.
      - **IV:** taking the course. **DV:** entrepreneurial intention.
      - **Measure:** a questionnaire with Likert items ("I intend to start a business within five years", 1-5), which is **ordinal**. Several items on the same idea let you check **internal consistency**.
      - **Sample:** students from several faculties, not only business students, so the results can generalise.
      - **Bias:** if students choose the course themselves, the most entrepreneurial ones join (a **sample** bias, a third variable). Students who didn't like the course may skip the survey (**non-response**). Leading wording ("How much has the course inspired you?") would bias the answers.
      - **Why it works:** add **semi-structured interviews** with a small, varied group of participants and code the transcripts into themes. Combining the two is **mixed methods**.
