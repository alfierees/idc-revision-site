> **Game plan.** Every question is multiple choice, and most open with a short case. For each one: (1) underline what the question actually asks (variables? method? flaw? scale?); (2) name the concept **before** looking at the options; (3) read **all four** options, because "all the answers are correct" was the best answer four times out of 19; (4) cross out options that reverse roles or use words that aren't in the course. Example questions below are from the [[PP_01-Sample Exam (2023 Moed B)|2023 sample exam]] unless marked otherwise.

---

## A · Name the variables (Q1)

**Steps**
1. Find the **outcome** the study cares about: that's the **DV** (success, grades, BMI, sign-ups).
2. Find what **helped, was changed or was compared**: that's the **IV**.
3. Something that explains **how** the IV works is a **mediator**; something that makes the link **stronger, weaker or reversed** for some people is a **moderator**.
4. Reject options that call a varying thing "not a variable", or swap IV and DV.

**Worked:** "Social connections and positive language helped entrepreneurs deal with crises… the successes of the ventures." → Success = **DV**; connections and positive language = **IVs** (option b). Lec 09 adds: "More police officers will reduce crime" → IV number of officers, DV crime rates.

## B · Correlation or causation? (Q2, Q4)

**Steps**
1. Direction: do they move **together** (positive) or **opposite** (negative)?
2. Is there a **third variable** in the story (season, age, fewer sugary drinks), or could the direction run the other way? Then it's **correlational**, not causal.
3. Causal needs all three: A **before** B, alternatives **ruled out**, an **experimental** design. A 10-year observation of habits is not an experiment.

**Worked:** more coffee went with longer life, and researchers then suggested the real reason was fewer sugary drinks → **a positive correlational relationship** (b). "A negative causal relationship" is wrong twice.

## C · What method can test this? (Q5, Q11)

**Steps**
1. If the options are a list of **legitimate methods** (an experiment, a questionnaire, interviews) and the question asks what **can** be done, the answer is usually **"all the answers are correct"**.
2. If it asks for the **best** method, use the goal: cause → experiment; how common → survey; why → interviews.
3. A good experiment option names the **comparison**: "a team with a mentor versus a team without".

**Worked (Q11):** to test "mentors make student ventures succeed": an experiment with and without a mentor, questionnaires to past teams, and interviews with current teams all give empirical evidence → **all correct** (c).

## D · Critique or improve a study (Q3, Q9)

**Checklist of flaws**

| Flaw | Clue in the case | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sample doesn't match the population | "designed for Jews **around the world**… sample of young Jews **from Israel**" | A more **diverse, representative** sample |
| "Experiment" with nothing manipulated | "experiment for the **entire population**… comparing successful and unsuccessful" | It's not an experiment: no IV is manipulated, no control group |
| Can't generalise | A special sample (Excellency students), insignificant results, support given after the outcome | Don't generalise; repeat with a proper sample and timing |
| Causal claim from correlation | "X is linked to Y, so X causes Y" | Rule out a third variable; run an experiment |
| No control group | Before-and-after on one group | Add a control group |

**Worked (Q9):** the most appropriate critique is **"an experiment as a research method is not suitable for the current study"** (a).

## E · A good research question, a hypothesis, a premise (Q6, Q10)

**Steps**
1. A research problem: a **relationship between variables**, put as a **clear question**, **empirically testable**.
2. A good question: comes **from the literature**, lets you **measure** the variables, and is the **basis for the hypotheses**: if all three appear as options, pick "all".
3. A hypothesis is a **statement** about existence, direction or magnitude ("will be negatively associated with…"); a question ends in a question mark.
4. A **premise** is what the text takes for granted. Read the purpose sentence and list what it assumes.

**Worked (Q10):** "the findings will be useful… to design targeted interventions… encouraging more women and men to start businesses" assumes gender differences in motivation, that policymakers should design interventions, and that more people should be encouraged → **all correct** (b). This one is debatable because the passage says "women **and** men".

## F · Which scale? (Q7, Q16)

**Steps**
1. Only names or categories → **nominal**.
2. Ranked but the gaps aren't measurable (satisfied / very satisfied, Likert, education level) → **ordinal**.
3. Equal gaps, no true zero (temperature) → **interval**.
4. True zero (money, weight, BMI, number of employees) → **ratio**.
5. Interval and ratio values **must be numerical**.

**Worked (Q16):** "Please rate your satisfaction with our services" → **ordinal** (a). "Nominal combined with interval" is a made-up distractor.

## G · Spot the bias (Q8)

**Steps**
1. Does the wording **state an assumption** ("most people like…", "as people do not like…")? → a preconceived notion / biased question.
2. Does it **assume behaviour** ("your favourite way of eating ice cream")? → an assumption.
3. Does it ask **two things** at once? → double-barrelled.
4. Does the sample leave out a group (urban only), or will some people not answer (unhappy staff)? → **sample** or **non-response** bias.

**Worked:** "Most people like to ski in the winter." → **there is a preconceived notion that people like to ski in the winter** (a). Reject "can only be identified after reading the literature": the bias is in the wording itself.

## H · Sampling (Q12, Lec 09)

**Steps**
1. "Equal chance", "randomly selected", "every 10th on the list" → **random** (simple or systematic).
2. "Volunteered", "from one dealership", "people who know the researcher", "recommended by" → **non-probability** (convenience, snowball).
3. Random sampling is about **equal chance**, not about the population being small or accessible.

**Worked (Q12):** "Random sampling ensures that all entrepreneurs in the population have an **equal chance** of being selected" (d). Lec 09: "100 recent luxury car buyers from one dealership who volunteered" → quantitative, **non-random**.

## I · Academic texts, sources and paper sections (Q13, Q14, Q19)

**Steps**
1. Five criteria: **A**uthor, **L**anguage, **R**eferences, **C**ontent, **S**tructure. Reject lists that add number of citations, journal ranking or quantitative findings.
2. The **method** section: sample (participants), tools, procedure. The literature review, hypotheses and conclusions are other sections.
3. Non-academic sources are fine as **data** (a blog's stories as a sample) and usable as background when evidence-based.

**Worked (Q14):** Anna sampled stories from a blog; a reviewer objects. Is the critique justified? **No, because non-academic sources can form the basis of a research sample** (b).

## J · Interview types and questions (Q17, Q18)

**Steps**
1. Fixed list, fixed order → **structured** (reliability). Prepared but flexible → **semi-structured** (the most common). Themes only, interviewee leads → **unstructured** (validity).
2. Question types: background, experience, opinion, emotional, knowledge, **sensory** (taste, smell, touch, sight, hearing).
3. Name the method from the activity: tasting stations to trace preferences → a survey with **sensory** questions.

**Worked (Q17):** Ikey prepared nothing and let interviewees lead; Soren kept a fixed list and order → **Ikey unstructured, Soren structured** (a). Option c swaps them.

## K · Qualitative or quantitative? (Q15, Lec 09 mini quiz)

**Steps**
1. Numbers, closed questions, Likert, large random sample, statistics → **quantitative**.
2. Words, open questions, in-depth interviews, small varied sample, themes → **qualitative**.
3. Quantitative's weakness: **personal, descriptive detail is lost**; qualitative's: **hard to generalise**, time-consuming, subjective.
4. "Quantitative only for natural sciences, qualitative only for social" → **false**.

**Worked (Q15):** the disadvantage of a quantitative method is that **the personalised aspects of the participants are lost** (b). It **reduces** personal bias, so "it increases personal bias" is the reverse.

---

## L · Reliability or validity? (taught in Lec 05)

**Steps**
1. Same result **again**? → reliability: over time (test-retest), across items (internal consistency), across judges (inter-rater).
2. Measures **the right thing**? → validity: generalises (external), experts judge coverage (content), captures a construct (construct), IV truly caused it (internal).
3. A reliable measure can still be invalid (a scale that's always 2 kg off).

---

## M · APA (taught in Lec 02)

**Steps**
1. Count authors: 1 or 2 → names; 3+ → "et al.".
2. Read someone through someone else → `(Original, year in Secondary, year)`; only the secondary in the list.
3. Interviews and personal communication → text only. Footnotes → never.
4. A DOI and a URL → **DOI only**.
5. Ordering a list: surname → initial → single author first → second author → year.
