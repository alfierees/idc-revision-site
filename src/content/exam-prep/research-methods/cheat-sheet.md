## 1. Academic texts and the research process ([[Lec_01-Academic Texts and the Research Process|Lec 01]])

"If research has not been written up, it is as if it was not done."

**The five criteria of an [[Academic Text]]** (asked word for word in Q19):

| Criterion | The text has |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Affiliation to an academic institute |
| **Language** | Formal, concise, professional, not personal |
| **References** | Credible, valid and reliable sources |
| **Content** | A basis in scientific research |
| **Structure** | The rules of academic writing for that type of research |

Not on the list (distractors in Q19): number of citations, journal ranking, quantitative findings, ability to generalise.

**[[Non-Academic Source|Non-academic sources]]** are judged by how far they rest on evidence rather than opinion: professional databases (Israel Innovation Authority), private research centres (IED), primary sources (Central Bureau of Statistics), unsupervised websites (Crunchbase, PitchBook), media (Calcalist, TheMarker, Forbes). They can also be the **research material itself**: stories from a blog can form a sample (Q14).

**The seven stages of empirical research**

1. Review the academic literature (the platform the study stands on, which justifies it).
2. Formulate the research question and hypotheses (both come out of the literature).
3. Design the method: **sample, tools, procedure** (and analysis).
4. Carry out the research and collect data (experiment, questionnaire, interview, observation).
5. Analyse the data (significant? hypotheses supported or rejected?).
6. Reach conclusions (compare with past work, theoretical and practical implications, future research).
7. Write the academic text.

| Research design part | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Sample** | Free of confounding influences, appropriate recruitment and sampling |
| **Tools** | Valid and reliable for the variables |
| **Procedure** | Manipulated or naturalistic; within or between subjects; random assignment or not |
| **Analysis** | Suits the methodology |

**Three types of method:** [[Quantitative Research|quantitative]] (describes variables numerically to establish relationships), [[Qualitative Research|qualitative]] (processes not readily measured in amount or frequency), [[Mixed Methods|mixed]] (both).

**Where each stage sits in a paper**

| Stage | Section of the paper |
|---|---|
| Literature | Title, authors, abstract; literature review |
| Question, hypotheses | Questions and hypotheses (look for the gaps) |
| Method | **Method: sample (participants), tools, procedure** (Q13) |
| Analysis | Results |
| Conclusions | Conclusion and discussion (the best part to cite) |
| Writing | Bibliography |

## 2. APA 7 in one box ([[Lec_02-APA Citing and Referencing|Lec 02]])

Full formats are on the [[APA & Scales Reference Sheet|reference sheet]].

- **In text:** (Surname, Year). Two authors: both names every time, `(Bergman & Rappaport, 2021)`. Three or more: `(Smith et al., 2010)`. Several: alphabetical, semicolons. Direct quote: add the page, `(Levy, 2009, p. 4)`.
- **Secondary source:** `(Levy, 2000 in Cohen, 2020)`; only the source you read (Cohen) goes in the reference list.
- **Personal communication / interview:** in the text only, not in the list. **No footnotes** in APA.
- **Reference list:** every source cited and only those, alphabetical by first author's surname; single author before the same author with others; then by second author; then by year.
- **DOI:** starts with 10; format it like a URL with no "DOI:" label; if a work has a DOI and a URL, use **only the DOI**.

## 3. Generative AI: PAIR ([[Lec_03-Generative AI Skills and the PAIR Framework|Lec 03]])

| Step | Do | Document |
|---|---|---|
| **P** Problem formulation | Define focus, scope, components, parameters; state what you expect of the tool | The problem and constraints |
| **A** AI tool selection | Explore, compare, evaluate, choose | Why tools were chosen or rejected |
| **I** Interaction | Experiment with inputs and outputs; judge accuracy | Successful **and** unsuccessful prompts |
| **R** Reflection | How the tool helped or hindered | Challenges, fixes, how to improve |

AI's three failures: **fabricated citations, false statistics, inaccurate content**: check every source. Seven prompting techniques: creating connections (give context), conversing with a character (act as…), defining the task, selecting a tone, using a style, limiting length, comparative examples.

## 4. Research problems, questions and hypotheses ([[Lec_04-Research Problems, Questions and Hypotheses|Lec 04]])

**A [[Research Problem]] must** (1) express a relationship between two or more variables, (2) be clearly formulated as a question, (3) be **empirically testable** (the variables can be measured).

**A good research question** (Q6, "all correct"): arises from the literature, enables measuring the variables, and forms the basis for the hypotheses.

**A [[Hypothesis]]** is a **statement**, made **before** data collection, about a relationship's **existence, direction or magnitude**. It must be testable, which needs [[Operational Definition|operational definitions]] (how each concept is measured), and it stems from theory.

> Question: Is trait victimhood related to entrepreneurship?
> Hypothesis: Trait victimhood **will be negatively associated** with entrepreneurship.

| Study type | Purpose | Hypotheses |
|---|---|---|
| [[Exploratory Research\|Exploratory]] | Get to know a phenomenon | Helps **find** them; doesn't test |
| [[Descriptive Research\|Descriptive]] | Characteristics, frequency, strength of a relationship | Relationships stay **correlational** |
| [[Causal Research\|Causal]] | Does A cause B? | Tests them with an **experimental design** |

**To claim A caused B:** A came **before** B; **alternative explanations** (a third variable) are ruled out; an **experimental design** was used.

A **premise** is what the researcher takes for granted before the study (Q10): "the findings will help design interventions encouraging more women and men…" assumes gender differences exist, that more founders should be encouraged, and that targeted interventions are worthwhile.

## 5. Variables ([[Lec_05-Variables, Correlation and Causation|Lec 05]])

| Variable | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [[Independent Variable]] (IV) | The **cause**; in an experiment the researcher changes it | Social connections, positive language (Q1) |
| [[Dependent Variable]] (DV) | The **effect**; depends on the IV | Venture success (Q1) |
| [[Mediator]] | The **process**: caused by the IV, influences the DV | Sleep quality → **alertness** → grades |
| [[Moderator]] | Changes the **strength or direction** of IV → DV | Experience → salary, moderated by **gender** |
| [[Control Variable]] | Held constant to isolate cause and effect | Same room, same time of day |

- **Full mediation:** without the mediator there is no relationship. **Partial:** some direct link remains.
- A moderator can be **categorical** (ethnicity, religion, health status) or **quantitative** (age, weight, income).
- Spotting them: in "X **will increase / decrease / improve** Y", X is the IV and Y the DV (physical activity → BMI, study time → test scores, financial incentives → organ donors).

## 6. Correlation versus causation (Lec 05)

- **[[Correlation]]:** the variables **covary**. It helps **predict**.
- **[[Causation]]:** one **brings about** change in the other. It helps **explain why**.
- **Correlation does not imply causation; causation always implies correlation.**

| Direction | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Positive** | Increase or decrease **together** (Q4) |
| **Negative** | One rises as the other falls |
| **Non-significant** | A change in one doesn't go with a change in the other |

A correlation has **strength** (the coefficient; how tightly points hug the line, not the slope), **direction** (its sign) and **significance** (the [[P-Value|p-value]], significant when **p < 0.05**).

| Problem | What happens | Course examples |
|---|---|---|
| [[Third Variable Problem]] | A third variable drives both | Storks and babies (**season**); foot size and IQ (**age**); coffee and long life (**fewer sugary drinks**, Q2); low sales of an innovative product (**a complicated website**) |
| [[Directionality Problem]] | Which causes which? | Motivation ↔ grades; violent videos ↔ violent behaviour; entrepreneurs ↔ no respect for authority |

**Q2 in one line:** more coffee went with longer life, but a third variable explains it, so the relationship is **positive and correlational**, not causal.

**The mentors / accelerators / courses table:** even if academic courses show the most unicorns, you **cannot generalise** if the findings are insignificant, the sample is special (Excellency Program students) or the support came two years after launch.

**Metrics:** vanity (feel good) vs actionable (change what you do); exploratory (find unknowns) vs reporting (track operations); correlated vs causal; qualitative vs quantitative.

## 7. Reliability and validity (Lec 05)

| | [[Reliability]] | [[Validity]] |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Same result again under the same conditions? | Does it measure what it should? |
| Relation | Reliable is **not always** valid | Valid is generally reliable |

| Type | Means | Picture it |
|---|---|---|
| [[Test-Retest Reliability\|Test-retest]] | Consistent **over time** | Same test, two weeks apart |
| [[Internal Consistency]] | Consistent **across items** of one scale | Two questions on one concept, both right or both wrong |
| [[Inter-Rater Reliability\|Inter-rater]] | **Observers agree** | Seven people judge the meal, not one |
| [[External Validity\|External]] | Generalises beyond the sample | |
| [[Content Validity\|Content]] | Experts judge it covers the construct | |
| [[Construct Validity\|Construct]] | Captures a construct (depression: oversleeping, appetite, concentration) | |
| [[Internal Validity\|Internal]] | The IV really produced the effect | |

## 8. Data and the four scales ([[Lec_06-Quantitative Data Collection|Lec 06]], [[Lec_07-Questionnaire Design|Lec 07]])

**Quantitative data** answers how much, how many, how often. **Discrete** is counted (family members); **continuous** is measured (weight, length, GPA). Age and money are listed both ways in the slides: go by how the variable is measured.

| Scale | Named | Ordered | Equal gaps | True zero | Example |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| [[Nominal Scale\|Nominal]] | ✓ | | | | Religion; 1 Morning, 2 Afternoon, 3 Night |
| [[Ordinal Scale\|Ordinal]] | ✓ | ✓ | | | **Likert**, satisfaction rating, education level |
| [[Interval Scale\|Interval]] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | | Temperature (can go below zero) |
| [[Ratio Scale\|Ratio]] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Money, weight, height, BMI |

Interval and ratio values **must be numerical** (Q7); nominal numbers are only labels.

## 9. Sampling (Lec 06, [[Lec_08-Qualitative Data and Interviews|Lec 08]])

A **sample** stands in for a **population** that is too big to study (saves time and money); it should be **representative**.

| Family | How | Methods |
|---|---|---|
| **Random (probability)** | Every unit has an **equal chance** (Q12) | Simple random; systematic (every k-th) |
| **Non-probability** | Chosen by the researcher or other subjective means | Convenience; snowball |

Qualitative recruitment (Lec 08) aims for **versatility** (variety in age, gender, education, experience), single to double-digit numbers, sometimes through a **[[Gatekeeper|gatekeeper]]**.

| Method | Plus | Minus |
|---|---|---|
| **Snowball** (interviewees recommend others) | Reaches hard-to-access groups | Biases the sample |
| **Advertising** (notices, forums, group chats) | Reduces population bias | May not guarantee variety |

Q3's fix: the app is for Jews worldwide but only young Israelis were surveyed, so survey **a more diverse population** (age, gender, country).

## 10. Surveys and experiments (Lec 06)

- **[[Survey]] = a method** (collect and analyse data from a sample). **[[Questionnaire]] = the set of questions** inside it.
- **[[Experiment]]:** tests a hypothesis by **manipulating** one or more IVs and measuring the DV.

| Setting | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Natural** | Observe **without** manipulating the IV |
| **Controlled** | [[Experimental Group\|Experimental group]] (IV manipulated) vs [[Control Group\|control group]] (not); identical otherwise |
| **Field** | A real-world experiment (lengthen office breaks, watch productivity) |

**Experimental method:** observe → hypothesis → design and run experiment → analyse → accept or reject → if rejected, a new hypothesis.

**Q9:** "an experiment for the entire population… comparing successful and unsuccessful entrepreneurs" manipulates nothing and has no control group, so the best critique is that **an experiment is not a suitable method** (it's a comparison of existing groups).

| Quantitative: advantages | Quantitative: disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Extensive analysis through numbers | **Insufficient information: personal, descriptive aspects are lost** (Q15) |
| Reduces personal bias in answers | Misleading conclusions from researcher bias |
| Objective, reliable, manageable | |

## 11. Questionnaire design (Lec 07)

**Eight steps:** information required → define the population → method of reaching them → question content → wording → order and format → **pilot** → final survey.

| Design | Shows |
|---|---|
| [[Longitudinal Design\|Longitudinal]]: one group, repeated over months or years | **Change over time** (twin studies) |
| [[Cross-Sectional Design\|Cross-sectional]]: same questionnaire to several groups | **Differences between groups** (customers in different countries) |

**Question types:** open-ended (qualitative data), dichotomous (yes/no), multiple-choice, pictorial, **dummy** (disguises the study's purpose), scaling (Likert). Include a question only if it serves the hypothesis.

| Wording rule | Bad example |
|---|---|
| One thing at a time | "Favourite **type and form** of ice cream?" |
| Simple, familiar words | "…countermeasures stipulated on the consumption of gelato?" |
| No assumptions | "Which is your favourite way of eating ice cream?" (assumes you eat it) |
| No bias | "As people do not like ice cream, why don't you?" |

**Order:** easy questions first, group related ones, vary the types. **Pilot** on a representative group: understood? serves its purpose? phrasing and order? length? instructions?

## 12. Qualitative research and interviews (Lec 08)

**[[Qualitative Data]]**: narrative, subjective, also called categorical (sorted into themes); tells you **motivations, emotions, views: what people feel and why**. Collected by **interviews, document analysis, observation, case study**.

| | Deductive | Inductive |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Top-down | Bottom-up |
| Starts from | A prior theory, categories ready | The data; themes emerge |
| Forms | | Open coding, in vivo coding (participants' words), constant comparison |

**Process:** collect → organise → **code** into themes → analyse → conclude with a descriptive story. Plus: depth, understanding, rich data. Minus: time-consuming, **hard to generalise**, subjective.

| Interview | Questions | Emphasis | Training |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Structured Interview\|Structured]] | Fixed list, fixed order, mostly closed | **Reliability** | Minimal |
| [[Semi-Structured Interview\|Semi-structured]] (most common) | Prepared and ordered, may change | Balance | Moderate |
| [[Unstructured Interview\|Unstructured]] | Themes only; interviewee leads | **Validity** | Extensive |

**Six question types:** background, experience, opinion, emotional, knowledge, **sensory** (saw, heard, touched, tasted, smelled).

**[[Content Analysis]] in 5 steps:** choose content → define categories → coding rules → code the text → analyse. Coding ladder: **meaning unit → open code → subcategory → category**. **[[Memoing]]** (a journal of your thoughts) is the first analysis; record audio or video.

| | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| Asks | Why did you buy that stock? | Did you buy Tesla stock today? Yes / No |
| Collection | Open surveys, unstructured interviews, observation | Closed surveys, experiments |
| Analysis | Categorical | Statistical |
| Population | Small, varied | Large, representative |

Quantitative isn't only for natural sciences, nor qualitative only for social sciences (false).

## 13. Bias ([[Lec_09-Course Summary|Lec 09]])

| Where | Example | Why it's biased |
|---|---|---|
| Question wording | "Is the new technology a complete waste of time?" | Implies the answer |
| An opening statement | "Most people like to ski in the winter." (Q8) | A **preconceived notion** |
| Interview question | "Will your age be a hindrance?" | Assumes age is a problem |
| Sample | Only urban residents | Not representative of rural consumers |
| Non-response | Unhappy employees skip the survey | Results miss part of the population |
| Snowball recruitment | Friends of friends | Similar people |

## 14. Method picker

| You want to… | Method | Data |
|---|---|---|
| Show that A **causes** B | **Experiment** with experimental and control groups | Quantitative |
| Measure how common or strong something is in a large group | **Survey / questionnaire**, random sample | Quantitative |
| Understand **why**, motivations, experiences | **Interviews** (semi-structured), observation | Qualitative |
| Study records, reports, blogs, media | **Document / content analysis** | Qualitative |
| Understand one company or group in depth | **Case study** | Qualitative |
| Track change in one group over time | **Longitudinal** design | Either |
| Compare groups at one time | **Cross-sectional** design | Either |
| Both how much and why | **Mixed methods** | Both |

When a case asks "what type of research **can** examine…" and the options are an experiment, a questionnaire and an interview, each answers it from a different angle: the expected answer is **all of them** (Q5, Q11).
