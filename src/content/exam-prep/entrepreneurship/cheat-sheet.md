## 1. Innovation and its four types (Class 1)

**Entrepreneurship** is pursuing opportunities "without regard to the resources they currently control" (Stevenson, HBS). **Intrapreneurship** is acting like an entrepreneur inside an established company (MIT Sloan).

| Term | Definition | Complete when… |
|---|---|---|
| Creativity | Production of **novel and useful** ideas (Sternberg & Lubart; Csikszentmihalyi) | The idea exists |
| Invention | Something new, which may never be used (Wilson 2016) | The thing exists |
| Innovation | **Successful implementation** of creative ideas within an organization (Amabile et al. 1996) | It is implemented and delivers benefit |

"Novel" means new **within its context**, not new to the world. An invention nobody uses is **not** an innovation.

**Four types of innovation** (Kraaijenbrink):

| | Operational | Strategic |
|---|---|---|
| **Outward** (market) | Product | Business model |
| **Inward** (organization) | Process | Management |

**Use it when** the question gives a scenario and asks for its type. Ask: who is it aimed at (market or the organization itself)? Does it change a tangible offering or way of working (operational), or the overall logic of value or management (strategic)?

> [!example] Worked: classify
> "A software company stops selling licences and moves to a monthly subscription." Aimed at the market (outward); changes how value is captured (strategic). **Business model innovation.**
> "Front-line staff are given authority to make decisions." Aimed at the organization (inward); changes how people are managed (strategic). **Management innovation**, which often implies decentralization.

The slide's six scenarios have no official answers; see [[classify-an-innovation|Classify an innovation]].

**Startup to organization:** founding, seed, Series A, Series B and C, exit. The shift is from **flexibility and efficiency** to **scalability and coordination**, which is why established firms struggle to stay innovative.

## 2. Structure (Class 2)

**Organisational structure** is the arrangement of **roles, responsibilities and relationships**; it decides how activity is coordinated toward goals. For innovation it fixes three things: **decision rights** (who can say yes), **information flow** (who hears what) and **accountability** (who owns the outcome).

| Structure | Pros | Cons | Innovation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hierarchical | Clear chain of command, easy to implement, clear advancement | Slow decisions, little cross-collaboration, base staff far from leadership | Low |
| Functional | Grouped by expertise, easy to measure and develop people | Silos, less innovation | Low to medium |
| Flat | Lean, fast decisions, job satisfaction, more innovation | Hard to scale, less mentoring, unclear advancement | High |
| Matrix | Cross-functional problem solving, communication | Two bosses, power struggles, reporting cost | High |

**Innovation design** (where innovation lives): **centralized** (one innovation department, strategy at the centre: **Apple**), **decentralized** (each business unit owns it: **Johnson & Johnson**), **hybrid** (corporate strategy linked to each unit: **Procter & Gamble**).

**Use it when** the question asks how a firm's structure helps or hurts innovation. Name the structure, then argue through decision rights, information flow and accountability.

> [!warning] "Flat is best" is not an answer
> Flat structures generate ideas but struggle to commercialise and scale them. Argue from **fit**: mechanistic structures suit stable environments, organic ones suit fast-changing ones (Burns & Stalker 1961, an enrichment in the notes rather than a slide). Big firms resolve exploration vs exploitation with an **ambidextrous** design: a protected innovation unit beside an efficient core.

## 3. Strategy (Class 3)

| | Business model | Business strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | How a business produces, offers and **captures value** | The initiatives a company pursues to create value and gain **competitive advantage** (HBS) |
| Netflix | Monthly subscription for streamed content | Exclusive original content to lock in subscribers |

**Red Ocean:** existing industries, known rules, demand **fought over** (airlines, fashion retail). **Blue Ocean:** industries not yet in existence, demand **created**, competition irrelevant (AI-enabled categories). Blue oceans can come from a new **combination** of value, not only new technology, and they turn red when imitators arrive.

**Porter's Five Forces** (Porter 1979) set an industry's competition and so its **profitability and attractiveness**: threat of new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, rivalry. Strong forces drain profit; an attractive industry has mostly **weak** forces.

**Use it when** the question asks how attractive or profitable an industry is, or why margins are thin. Rate each force (low, medium, high) with a reason, then read out the overall picture.

> [!example] Worked: fast food vs streaming (class examples)
> | Force | Fast food | Streaming |
> |---|---|---|
> | New entrants | Medium (franchising easy; brand, scale, sites are barriers) | Medium (capital; content libraries, tech are barriers) |
> | Suppliers | **Low** (bulk buying, many suppliers) | **High** (studios demand premium deals or launch platforms) |
> | Buyers | High (many choices, easy switching) | High (cancel easily) |
> | Substitutes | High (home cooking, meal kits, delivery) | High (YouTube, gaming, TikTok) |
> | Rivalry | Very high | Very high |
>
> The difference is **supplier power**, which is why streaming is squeezed from both ends. Suppliers sell to you (upstream); buyers buy from you (downstream). Mixing them up is the common error.

Worked steps: [[porters-five-forces-analysis|Analyse an industry with Porter's Five Forces]].

## 4. Culture (Class 6)

**Organisational culture** is the shared values, beliefs and behaviours that shape how members think, act and interact. A survey of 270 leaders (HBR, Kirshner) found culture a major obstacle to innovation in large firms: **cultural resistance** (operational excellence breeds resistance to disruption) and **historical baggage** (staff remember past failures). The fixes: protected **innovation subcultures**, and new **incentives** plus **diverse** talent.

**OCAI** (Cameron & Quinn):

| Culture | Held together by | Leaders | Innovation | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clan | Loyalty, tradition; family-like | Mentors | Supportive, collaborative; surfaces ideas | Lego |
| Adhocracy | Innovation, experimentation; risk-taking | Innovators | **Most conducive to radical** innovation | Nvidia |
| Market | Winning, goals; results | Hard drivers | Incremental, customer-driven | General Electric |
| Hierarchy | Rules, procedures; formal | Coordinators | Limits radical; supports incremental cost-cutting | McKinsey |

**Use it when** the question names a culture or asks which culture suits radical or incremental innovation. Adhocracy and clan lean to exploration; market and hierarchy to exploitation.

> [!example] Worked: Google's culture
> Founded by Brin and Page (Stanford); two founding goals: organise the world's information and be the best company in the world, deliberately "thinking differently" (Steiber 2014). **Campus-like** workplace; **Googliness** (adjusting to the culture) reduces the need for formal rules; the **20% rule** (by 2005, half of new ideas came from 20% time); a **"YES" culture**. Read as OCAI: an adhocracy at scale, with clan elements.

## 5. Barriers to innovation (Class 6 and reading)

A barrier prevents or hinders innovative activity. **Radical innovation** (fundamentally new products, services or processes) faces the steepest barriers: higher risk, uncertainty and complexity. Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos (2014):

| Level | Category | Barriers |
|---|---|---|
| External | Specific actors | Customer resistance, unsupportive government, shortage of external finance, rivalry |
| External | Macro environment | Undeveloped network/ecosystem, technological turbulence, inappropriate infrastructure, restrictive local culture |
| Internal | Restrictive mindset | Fear of change, preference for the status quo |
| Internal | Lack of competences | **Discovery** (identify opportunities), **incubation** (develop into a proposal), **acceleration and commercialisation** (scale) |
| Internal | Insufficient resources | Internal finance, skills, information |
| Internal | Unsupportive structure | Hierarchy impedes coordination and communication |

**Use it when** the question asks what stops a firm innovating, or asks for a barrier and a solution (the class exercise). Internal barriers can be **fixed** by the firm (culture, structure, hiring, budget); external ones must be **managed** (partnerships, lobbying, timing).

> [!example] Worked: a barrier and a fix
> A bank's staff remember a failed app launch and dismiss new ideas. Barrier: **restrictive mindset** (internal), the HBR "historical baggage". Fix: a protected innovation subculture (a small lab outside the normal approval chain) and incentives that reward experiments, not only results.

## 6. Leadership (Class 7)

**Leadership** is "an influence relationship among leaders and followers who intend real changes and outcomes that reflect their shared purposes" (Rost 1993). A relationship, not a title. **Followership:** everyone is a follower at some time; most of us more often than leaders.

| | Vision | Mission |
|---|---|---|
| Time | The future (where in 10 years?) | The present, daily steps |
| LinkedIn | "Create economic opportunity for every member of the global workforce" | "Connect the world's professionals to make them more productive and successful" |

An **ambitious vision** challenges the status quo and pushes people to think creatively.

**Transformational leadership** leads changes in vision, strategy and culture and promotes innovation; followers are "encouraged and inspired to see past their own self-interest in order to achieve a greater vision" (Grădinaru et al. 2020). A **soft** approach: inspiring a shared vision, empowering team members, adapting to change, encouraging innovation and initiative, creating ethical, attitudinal and process changes.

| Leadership | Management |
|---|---|
| Compelling vision, strategic context, long-term strategies | Effective, efficient handling of resources: planning, organising, staffing, directing, controlling |
| The horizon | Detailed plans and schedules |
| Doing the right things | Doing things right |

Both are needed; neither is "better".

> [!example] Worked: Bezos as a transformational leader
> Changed Amazon's **vision** (bookstore to "everything store" to AWS), **strategy** (long-term reinvestment over short-term profit) and **culture** ("Day 1", customer obsession): change across all three domains is the definition.

## 7. Creativity and ideation (Class 8)

Creativity as a **process** (generating ideas; managers can steer it) and as a **product** (the outcome). Managing it means holding the tension between **control** and **freedom**.

**Amabile & Pratt (2016), dynamic componential model:** individual creativity needs all three of **domain-relevant skills** (what you know), **creativity-relevant processes** (how you think) and **task motivation** (why you do it). The organisational mirror: resources, innovation-management skills, motivation to innovate. Dynamic loops: **progress** raises motivation; **failure** need not end the process under psychological safety; leaders keep work **meaningful**.

- **Intrinsic motivation** (interest, enjoyment, meaning) sustains creativity; **extrinsic** (pay, deadlines, evaluation) can support or undermine it.
- **Psychological safety:** team members feel safe to take risks and share unconventional ideas without fear of ridicule or punishment; failure becomes learning.
- **Progress principle:** creativity is fuelled by perceived progress in meaningful work; small wins boost intrinsic motivation; setbacks reduce it but can still help if safety is present.

**Ideation tools** separate generating ideas from judging them:

| Tool | Content |
|---|---|
| Brainstorming (Osborn, 1950s, BBDO) | Defer judgment; quantity over quality; wild ideas; build on others' ideas |
| Lateral thinking (de Bono) | Break from linear ("vertical") thinking to new, non-obvious approaches |
| Six Thinking Hats (de Bono) | White facts, red emotions, black risks, yellow benefits, green new ideas, blue manages the process. The whole group wears **one hat at a time** |

> [!example] Worked: why a bonus may not help
> A manager offers a cash prize for the best idea by Friday. That is extrinsic pressure; it can narrow exploration. Amabile & Pratt point instead to making the task meaningful, giving visible small wins, and psychological safety so wild ideas get said.

## 8. Design Thinking and the Double Diamond (Class 9)

The creative process in innovative organisations is **iterative**, **cross-disciplinary**, **user-centred** and **tolerant of failure**.

**Design Thinking** is a user-centred approach to innovation that begins from understanding user needs (IDEO: David Kelley, Tim Brown; Stanford d.school). Five stages: **Empathy, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test**, looping. A good solution meets three lenses:

| Lens | Question | Perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Desirability | Do people want it? | Human |
| Feasibility | Can we build it? | Technology |
| Viability | Can it be a sustainable business? | Business |

**Double Diamond** (UK Design Council): two rounds of diverge then converge.

| Diamond | Phase | Mode | Design Thinking stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem ("the right thing") | Discover | Diverge | Empathy |
| Problem | Define | Converge | Define |
| Solution ("the thing right") | Develop | Diverge | Ideate, Prototype |
| Solution | Deliver | Converge | Test |

"Fall in love with the problem, not the solution." A lack of empathy can result in product failure.

## 9. The first diamond in practice (Class 10)

Pipeline: **interviews → Empathy Map → Persona → Customer Journey Map → design question.** Start with two questions: **who** are you designing for, and **what** is the design problem?

| Tool | Answers | Content |
|---|---|---|
| User interviews | What is their experience? | Learning goal, representative users, **open-ended** questions, record |
| Empathy Map | What is in their head? | Say, Think, Do, Feel; the gaps hide needs |
| Persona | Who is it for? | A fictional user **built from data**: name, background, goals, frustrations, needs, motivations, a real quote |
| Customer Journey Map | Where does it hurt over time? | Stages, actions, emotions, **touchpoints**; mark one key moment |

A **touchpoint** is any interaction between a customer and a brand, product or service, before, during or after a purchase.

**Design question** ("How might we…?"): **user + need + problem**, open on the "how". Templates: "How might we [action] for [user] to [outcome]?"; "How could we [action] to help [user] who [problem]?"

> [!example] Worked: the class design question
> "How might we improve the commuting experience for students who travel to Reichman University by bus, so that it feels more reliable, comfortable, and stress-free?" User: bus-commuting students. Need: reliable, comfortable, stress-free. No solution inside it. "How might we fix public transport?" is too broad; "How might we add a bench at stop 42?" has the answer built in.

See [[run-the-first-diamond|Run the first diamond]] and [[write-a-design-question|Write a design question]].

## 10. The second diamond: prototyping (Class 11)

**Prototyping** creates an early model to test concept, design, functionality and usability. **Rapid prototyping:** shortest time, least cost, simplest model. A prototype is a **tool for thinking**, not a final product. Objectives: test ideas quickly, find flaws early, gather user feedback.

**Plan → Build → Test:** decide what to learn and the critical feature; build a low-fidelity model that shows it; test with real users (at least two), refine, loop. **Iteration** is the repeated test, learn, refine cycle; **product validation** is confirming with users that the solution meets their needs.

| Type | Fidelity | Looks real | Interactive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wireframe | Low | No (layout skeleton) | No |
| Mockup | Medium | Yes | No |
| Interactive prototype (Figma, InVision, Adobe XD) | High | Yes | Yes |

A mockup **looks** like the product; an interactive prototype **behaves** like it. See [[plan-build-test-a-prototype|Plan, build and test a prototype]].

## 11. Putting it together: the four levers

Organisational innovation sits among four levers, **structure, strategy, culture and leadership**, inside an ecosystem and linked outward by **open innovation**. The class read Google through all four:

| Lever | Google |
|---|---|
| Structure | Matrix: agility, cross-functional collaboration |
| Culture | Inclusive, innovative, psychologically safe |
| Leadership | Transformational, empathetic, data-driven |
| Strategy | Innovation-led, user-focused, ethically grounded |

A firm is innovative when the four **reinforce** each other. For "analyse company X", go lever by lever, then say where they reinforce or clash.
