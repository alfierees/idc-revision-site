  - id: "1"
    title: "1 · Creativity, invention or innovation?"
    text: |
      A start-up engineer designs a self-watering plant pot and builds one working model, which stays in the office. Is this creativity, invention or innovation? What would make it an innovation?
    solution: |
      It is an **invention** (and the idea behind it is creativity): something new exists, but nobody uses it. It becomes an **innovation** only when it is **successfully implemented**, for example sold and actually helping customers keep plants alive (Amabile et al. 1996: innovation is the successful implementation of creative ideas).
  - id: "2"
    title: "2 · Classify the innovation"
    text: |
      A software company stops selling one-off licences and switches to a monthly subscription. Which type of innovation is this?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          Product innovation
        why: |
          The software itself is unchanged; what changes is how it is paid for.
      - label: b
        text: |
          Business model innovation
        correct: true
        why: |
          Outward (the market) and strategic (how value is captured).
      - label: c
        text: |
          Process innovation
        why: |
          Process innovation is inward: how the organization works day to day.
      - label: d
        text: |
          Management innovation
        why: |
          Management innovation changes how the organization is organized and led.
    solution: |
      Ask who it is aimed at (the market: outward) and what level it changes (the logic of capturing value: strategic). **Answer (b).**
  - id: "3"
    title: "3 · The four types"
    text: |
      Draw the four types of innovation on their two axes, and place "front-line workers are given authority to decide" in the grid.
    solution: |
      | | Operational | Strategic |
      |---|---|---|
      | **Outward** | Product | Business model |
      | **Inward** | Process | Management |

      Empowering front-line workers changes how people are managed and led, inside the organization: **management innovation** (inward, strategic). The slides note it often implies decentralization.
  - id: "4"
    title: "4 · Is flat best?"
    text: |
      "Flat structures are the best structure for innovation." Give a balanced answer in three or four sentences.
    solution: |
      Flat structures put decision rights next to the work, so teams can experiment quickly, and they are linked to more innovation and faster decisions. But they are hard to implement and scale, give less mentoring and unclear advancement, and can struggle to commercialise the ideas they generate. The better answer is about **fit**: organic, flexible designs suit fast-changing environments and mechanistic ones suit stable environments. Large firms often combine the two with an ambidextrous design, a protected innovation unit beside an efficient core.

  - id: "9"
    title: "9 · Google's culture"
    text: |
      Name three features of Google's culture from the case study, and say which OCAI type it resembles.
    solution: |
      Any three of: a **campus-like** creative workplace mixing seriousness and play; **Googliness**, which reduces the need for formal rules; the **20% rule** (by 2005, half of Google's new ideas came from 20% time); a **"YES" culture** where the default answer to a new idea is to try it; two founding goals, to organise the world's information and to be the best company in the world. It resembles an **adhocracy**, with clan elements.
  - id: "10"
    title: "10 · Internal or external?"
    text: |
      Sort these barriers into internal and external: fear of change; strict regulation; banks unwilling to lend to innovative firms; inability to scale a pilot; rapid technological change; a rigid hierarchy.
    solution: |
      **Internal:** fear of change (restrictive mindset); inability to scale a pilot (lack of acceleration and commercialisation competences); a rigid hierarchy (unsupportive structure).

      **External:** strict regulation (unsupportive government); banks unwilling to lend (shortage of external finance); rapid technological change (technological turbulence, macro environment).

      Source: Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos (2014).
  - id: "11"
    title: "11 · Leadership vs management"
    text: |
      Complete: leadership is about doing ___; management is about doing ___. Give one activity of each.
    solution: |
      Leadership is **doing the right things**: a compelling vision, strategic context, long-term strategy. Management is **doing things right**: planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling resources to specific results. Both are needed.
  - id: "12"
    title: "12 · Transformational leadership"
    text: |
      Define transformational leadership and explain why Jeff Bezos is the class's example.
    solution: |
      Transformational leaders lead changes in an organisation's **vision, strategy and culture** and promote innovation; followers are "encouraged and inspired to see past their own self-interest in order to achieve a greater vision" (Grădinaru et al. 2020). Bezos changed Amazon's vision (bookstore to "everything store" to AWS), its strategy (long-term reinvestment over short-term profit) and its culture ("Day 1", customer obsession).
  - id: "13"
    title: "13 · Paying for ideas"
    text: |
      A manager offers a €1,000 prize for the best idea by Friday. Using Amabile & Pratt, explain the risk and suggest a better approach.
    solution: |
      The prize is **extrinsic** motivation. Creative, open-ended work runs on **intrinsic** motivation (interest, enjoyment, meaning), and extrinsic pressure can crowd it out, narrowing exploration toward the reward. Better: make the task **meaningful** by linking it to the organisation's goals, create **psychological safety** so unusual ideas are voiced, and aim for visible **small wins** (the progress principle). Skills and creative processes must also be present: all three components are needed.

      - label: d

    solution: |
      White facts, red emotions, black risks, yellow benefits, green new ideas, blue process. **Answer (b).**

    title: "15 · The Double Diamond"
    text: |
      Name the four phases of the Double Diamond, say which are divergent and which convergent, and map the five Design Thinking stages onto them.
    solution: |
      | Phase | Mode | Stage |
      |---|---|---|
      | Discover | Diverge | Empathy |
      | Define | Converge | Define |
      | Develop | Diverge | Ideate, Prototype |
      | Deliver | Converge | Test |

      The first diamond is the problem ("the right thing"); the second is the solution ("the thing right").
  - id: "16"
    title: "16 · Fix the design question"
    text: |
      A team writes: "How might we build an app that shows bus times?" What is wrong, and write a better design question.
    solution: |
      It has the **solution inside it** (an app), so the first diamond has been skipped. A design question fixes the **user**, the **need** and the **problem** and leaves the "how" open. Better, from class: "How might we improve the commuting experience for students who travel to Reichman University by bus, so that it feels more reliable, comfortable, and stress-free?"
  - id: "17"
    title: "17 · Which prototype?"
    text: |
      You want to find out whether users can navigate your app's booking flow without help. Which prototype do you need?
    options:
      - label: a
        text: |
          A wireframe
        why: |
          A wireframe tests layout and structure, but users cannot click through it.
      - label: b
        text: |
          A mockup
        why: |
          A mockup looks like the product but is static, so it cannot show navigation.
      - label: c
        text: |
          An interactive prototype
        correct: true
        why: |
          Clickable elements simulate the experience, so you can watch users navigate.
      - label: d
        text: |
          No prototype: ask users in a survey
        why: |
          Users are poor at predicting their own behaviour; watch them use it.
    solution: |
      Match fidelity to the question: navigation and usability need something that **behaves** like the product. **Answer (c).**
  - id: "18"
    title: "18 · Plan, Build, Test"
    text: |
      Describe the three steps of a prototyping cycle and the testing rule from Class 11.
    solution: |
      **Plan:** decide what you aim to learn and choose the most critical aspect to prototype. **Build:** use basic, mostly digital materials to make a low-fidelity prototype that shows the key function or concept. **Test:** try it with real users or stakeholders and refine it on their feedback, then loop. The rule: test with **at least two real users** and use their feedback to fix the solution.
