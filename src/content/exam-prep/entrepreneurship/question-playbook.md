> No past exam is published, so the question shapes come from the lectures' exam-trap warnings and class exercises (see the [[Exam Topic Map]]). The model answers are written from the notes; they show the structure, not a marking scheme.

## The shape of every good answer

1. **Define** the concept, with its source where the slides give one.
2. **Apply** it to the case in the question, using the case's own facts.
3. **Explain why**: the mechanism, not only the label.
4. **Conclude** with a judgement: a type, a rating, a recommendation, a trade-off.

## A. "Define X and explain how it differs from Y"

1. Both definitions, word for word if you can, with sources.
2. The single test that separates them.
3. One example of each, ideally the same situation seen two ways.

> [!example] Model: creativity vs innovation vs invention
> **Creativity** is the production of novel and useful ideas (Sternberg & Lubart). **Innovation** is the successful implementation of creative ideas within an organization (Amabile et al. 1996). An **invention** is something new that may never be used (Wilson 2016). The test is implementation and benefit: an engineer's idea for a self-watering pot is creativity; a working prototype in a drawer is an invention; the pot on sale and helping customers keep plants alive is an innovation.

Other pairs to have ready: business model vs strategy; red vs blue ocean; suppliers vs buyers; vision vs mission; leadership vs management; intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation; creativity as process vs product; diverge vs converge; mockup vs interactive prototype; internal vs external barrier.

## B. "What type of innovation is this?"

1. Draw the 2×2: outward/inward × operational/strategic.
2. Test 1: aimed at the **market** or at the **organization**?
3. Test 2: a **tangible** offering or way of working, or the **logic** of value or management?
4. Give the type, then say why it is not the neighbouring one.

> [!example] Model: a supermarket's AI inventory system
> It is aimed at the organization's own operations (inward) and changes a day-to-day way of working, restocking (operational), so it is **process innovation**. It is not management innovation, because it does not change how people are organised or led. Full routine: [[classify-an-innovation|Classify an innovation]].

## C. "How attractive is industry X?" (Five Forces)

1. One line on what the model does: five forces set competition and so profitability (Porter 1979).
2. A table: each force, a rating, a reason from the industry.
3. Read-out: which forces matter most, and so whether the industry is attractive.
4. If asked for strategy: how a firm could weaken a force (raise entry barriers, lock in buyers, own its supply).

> [!example] Model read-out: streaming
> Entry is medium (capital and content libraries are barriers), suppliers are strong (studios demand premium deals or launch their own platforms), buyers are strong (they cancel easily), substitutes are many (YouTube, gaming, TikTok) and rivalry is very high. Squeezed from both ends, platforms have thin structural margins and invest in original content to cut supplier power. Full routine: [[porters-five-forces-analysis|Analyse an industry with Porter's Five Forces]].

## D. "How does the structure or culture of company X affect its innovation?"

**Structure:**

1. Name the structure (hierarchical, functional, flat, matrix) and the innovation design (centralized, decentralized, hybrid).
2. Run the three channels: **decision rights**, **information flow**, **accountability**. Say for each whether it helps or hurts.
3. Give the trade-off and fit: what the structure gains, what it costs, whether it suits the firm's environment.

**Culture:**

1. Place it on OCAI (clan, adhocracy, market, hierarchy), with evidence.
2. Say what kind of innovation that culture favours: radical (adhocracy, clan) or incremental (market, hierarchy).
3. If it is a mismatch, recommend a fix: protected subculture, new incentives, diverse hiring.

> [!example] Model: Apple's structure
> Apple is the class example of **centralized** innovation design within a functional organisation. Decision rights sit at the top, so a small number of coherent, high-conviction product bets get full backing. The cost is distance from local markets and dependence on the centre's judgement. It suits a firm that competes on a few integrated flagship products.

## E. "Identify a barrier and propose a solution"

1. Name the barrier and its level: internal (mindset, competences, resources, structure) or external (customers, government, finance, rivalry; ecosystem, turbulence, infrastructure, local culture). Cite Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos (2014).
2. Show it in the case's facts.
3. Propose a fix that matches the level: internal barriers are **fixed** by the firm; external ones are **managed** (partnerships, lobbying, timing).
4. Link the fix to a theory: HBR's protected subcultures and incentives, a flatter or ambidextrous structure, transformational leadership, psychological safety.

> [!example] Model: a hospital that cannot scale a pilot
> Doctors in one ward built a successful patient-messaging tool, but it never spread. The barrier is a **lack of acceleration and commercialisation competences** (internal): the hospital can discover and incubate ideas but not scale them. Fix: a small central innovation team that takes proven pilots across wards (a hybrid design), with a budget set aside for roll-out.

## F. "Write a design question" or "run the first diamond"

1. **Who** is the user, and **what** is the problem? No solution yet.
2. Discover: interviews with **open** questions; summarise on an Empathy Map (Say, Think, Do, Feel), a Persona and a Customer Journey Map with one key moment.
3. Define: "How might we [action] for [user] to [outcome]?" with **user + need + problem**.
4. Check scope: broad enough for many ideas, narrow enough to act on, and no solution hidden inside.

> [!example] Model: library study space
> Interviews with students show they arrive at 10:00 and find no free desk; they feel anxious and leave. Key journey moment: arriving at a full floor. "How might we help students who study on campus find a free study space quickly, so that they do not waste time or give up?" Full routines: [[run-the-first-diamond|Run the first diamond]], [[write-a-design-question|Write a design question]].

## G. "Plan a prototype and how you would test it"

1. **Plan:** the one thing you need to learn and the critical feature.
2. **Build:** the lowest fidelity that answers it (wireframe for layout and flow, mockup for look, interactive prototype for usability).
3. **Test:** at least two real users; watch what they do, not only what they say.
4. **Iterate:** what you would change on each likely result.

> [!example] Model: the study-space idea
> Learning goal: will students check a live desk map before walking over? Build an interactive prototype in Figma with fake data, because the question is about use, not looks. Test with two students on their way to the library and note whether they open it and trust it. If they say they would rather be told the floor than see a map, simplify the next version. Full routine: [[plan-build-test-a-prototype|Plan, build and test a prototype]].

## H. "Analyse company X" (the four levers)

1. **Structure:** type and innovation design.
2. **Strategy:** business model vs strategy; red or blue ocean; the key force.
3. **Culture:** OCAI type and evidence.
4. **Leadership:** vision and mission; transformational or not.
5. **Conclusion:** do the four reinforce each other, and which lever is the weak one?

> [!example] Model: Google (class example)
> A **matrix** structure for cross-functional collaboration; an **innovation-led, user-focused** strategy; an **adhocracy** culture with clan elements, made concrete by Googliness, the 20% rule and a "YES" default; **transformational, empathetic, data-driven** leadership. The levers reinforce each other: the culture gives the leadership's vision somewhere to land, and the structure lets ideas cross teams.
