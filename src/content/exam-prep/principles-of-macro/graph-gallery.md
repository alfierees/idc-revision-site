## 2. The steady state (Q7, Q8)

Saving per worker meets the break-even line at $\bar k$. Below it $k$ rises; above it $k$ falls. On the last step, move $s$, $n + \delta$ and $A$.

## 3. A permanent change in saving (Q9, Q12)

A higher $s$ moves the steady state out: $k$ and $y$ rise to a new **level**, and growth stops again. Different steady states with the same function, $n$ and $\delta$ always come from different saving rates.

```graph
type: solow-model
figure: savings-shift
```

## 4. The Golden Rule (Q11, Q17)

Steady-state consumption is the gap between $f(k)$ and $(\delta + n)k$. It is widest where the slope of $f$ equals $\delta + n$, which for Cobb-Douglas means $s = \alpha$. Push $s$ past $\alpha$ and consumption shrinks.


## 5. The poverty trap (Q10)

Economia and Stagnatia (Lec 07): the same economy at two saving rates. Low saving leaves $s\,f(k)$ under the line, so $k$ slides to a low steady state.

## 6. Loanable funds: a government deficit (Q6)

Lower public saving shifts the supply of funds left: the interest rate rises and investment is crowded out. A drop in household consumption does the opposite.

## 7. Loanable funds in an open economy (Q2)

With saving fixed, the world rate decides investment. Above the closed rate, investment falls below saving and the surplus is lent abroad ($NX > 0$); below it, the economy borrows.

## 8. The production function (Q19, Q20)

Output rises with labour, but each extra worker adds less: diminishing returns. The same shape in capital is why a capital-rich economy grows more slowly.
